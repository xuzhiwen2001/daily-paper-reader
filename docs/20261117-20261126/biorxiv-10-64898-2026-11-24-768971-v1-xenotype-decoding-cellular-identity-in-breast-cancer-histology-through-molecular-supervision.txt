Characterizing the cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment from histological images 31 

is fundamental to understanding cancer biology and informing treatment decisions. Deep 32 

learning has enabled automated nuclear detection and classification from hematoxylin and eosin 33 

(H&E) images, yet cell classifiers remain largely confined to broad morphological categories 34 

owing to a reliance on manual annotations. Recent efforts have used spatial molecular 35 

measurements to supervise histological classification, but these approaches often require 36 

separate tissue sections or produce altered H&E staining, and the resulting cell-type annotations 37 

depend on subjective choices such as marker selection and clustering parameters. Here we 38 

develop XenoType, a framework that classifies cells into seven molecularly informed categories 39 

directly from routine histology, trained using paired same-section Xenium spatial transcriptomics. 40 

Cell-type annotations are reconciled from four single-cell RNA-sequencing references, reducing 41 

dependence on marker choices. Across eight held-out breast tissue sections, XenoType 42 

outperformed commonly used CellViT++ retrained on the same molecular annotations under 43 

both four-class (macro-F1: 0.703 vs. 0.569) and seven-class (macro-F1: 0.571 vs. 0.403) 44 

taxonomies, with the largest gains for myoepithelial (F1: 0.175 to 0.572) and endothelial cells 45 

(F1: 0.395 to 0.682). Molecular measurements are required only during training; at inference, 46 

XenoType uses the H&E image alone. Model-reference agreement increased consistently with 47 

annotation confidence, and XenoType generalized to an independently profiled breast tissue 48 

section from a different spatial transcriptomics platform, supporting scalable cell-type mapping 49 

from routine histology without per-specimen molecular profiling. 50

Introduction 52 

Breast cancer comprises heterogeneous populations of malignant and nonmalignant cells 53 

whose composition and spatial organization influence disease progression, immune evasion and 54 

clinical outcome 1–4 . Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining provides a detailed view of tissue 55 

architecture and remains central to histopathological assessment 5,6 . However, morphologically 56 

similar cells may exhibit distinct molecular identities, limiting the cellular resolution achievable 57 

through conventional histological examination 7. Resolving these populations and their spatial 58 

relationships directly from H&E images would enable more detailed characterization of tumor 59 

heterogeneity across routinely collected tissue specimens and the large collections already 60 

available in clinical archives 8–11 .61 

Deep learning has enabled automated nuclear detection and classification in histological images, 62 

with recent foundation models providing transferable representations of tissue morphology 12–14 .63 

Nevertheless, cell classifiers remain constrained by training data: existing annotated datasets 64 

rely predominantly on manual labels, which are labor-intensive, subject to observer variability, 65 

and generally restricted to broad morphological categories, including the human breast 15,16 .66 

Spatial transcriptomics offers an alternative source of supervision by measuring gene expression 67 

while preserving the spatial organization at cellular or tissue resolution 2,17–21 . Recent studies 68 

have begun to address these limitations by leveraging molecular measurements as supervision 69 

for histological analysis 18,22 . Li et al. used co-registered multiplex immunofluorescence to 70 

generate protein-based cell annotations for training H&E classifiers 22 , while STHELAR 71 

established a multi-tissue resource linking spatial transcriptomic measurements with histological 72 

images 18 . Other frameworks have extended this direction toward predicting spatially resolved 73 

gene expression and cell identities directly from H&E 23–25 .74 

Although these approaches demonstrate the potential of molecularly supervised classification, 75 

important gaps remain. Many spatial molecular assays, such as multiplex immunofluorescence, 76 

either require a separate tissue section or alter the H&E stain, since staining is typically 77 

performed after molecular profiling rounds 26,27 . In addition, cell-type assignments in spatial 78 

transcriptomics datasets are typically assigned by clustering expression profiles and manually 79 

inspecting marker genes for each cluster - a process that is subjective, difficult to reproduce and 80 

sensitive to the choice of markers and resolution parameters 28,29 . Automated reference-based 81

However, the resulting assignments depend on references that differ in cellular composition and 83 

annotation granularity, and systematic strategies to reconcile predictions across references and 84 

to characterize annotation concordance remain limited 30,31 . At the model level, leading 85 

frameworks for cell analysis derive classification features from their segmentation backbone, 86

Here, we introduce XenoType, a framework for training cell classifiers on H&E images using 92 

annotations derived from paired Xenium spatial transcriptomics, where H&E staining is 93 

performed on the same tissue section as molecular profiling. We reconcile cell-type predictions 94 

from four single-cell RNA-sequencing references into seven categories and transfer the resulting 95 

labels to nuclei detected in H&E. XenoType separates nuclear segmentation from feature 96 

extraction, allowing a pathology foundation model to supply cell representations while retaining 97 

the same segmentation model. XenoType achieved higher agreement with molecular reference 98 

annotations than CellViT++ retrained on the same labels across eight held-out Xenium breast 99 

tissue sections and an additional section profiled using ATERA. At inference, XenoType requires 100 

only the H&E image, supporting scalable cell-type mapping across tissue collections without 101 

molecular profiling of each specimen. 102

Results 104 

Spatial transcriptomics enables molecular supervision of histological cell classification 105 

We developed XenoType to classify individual cells in images of standard hematoxylin and eosin 106 

(H&E)-stained tissue sections using molecularly informed training annotations ( Fig. 1 ). The 107 

study used paired Xenium spatial transcriptomic and histological data from the same tissue 108 

section of 12 publicly available human breast tissue specimens, linking gene expression profiles 109 

to tissue morphology at single-cell resolution. Using four independent single-cell RNA-110 

sequencing references (Yan, Wu, Dong, and Chen) 2,33–35 , we assigned Xenium cells to seven 111

Multi-reference annotation defines seven molecularly informed cell categories 119 

Spatial transcript density and cell-level gene expression profiles summarize the molecular data 120 

used for annotation ( Fig. 2A ). The four reference datasets 2,33–35 differed in cellular composition 121 

and class representation, as illustrated by their respective embeddings ( Fig. 2B ). We used 122 

SingleR 36 to annotate each Xenium cell independently against each reference, then mapped the 123 

reference-specific predictions to the seven shared categories. 124 

We reconciled the four SingleR predictions by voting and resolved ties using the mean SingleR 125 

score and a predefined reference order. Given only one reference (Wu 2) had normal epithelial 126 

and myoepithelial cells, this reference ’s assignment of these classes superseded other 127 

references ’ votes. For each cell, we recorded how many references supported the final 128 

assignment. The 4,000-cell sample illustrates these patterns across the seven categories ( Fig. 129 

2C ). Because reference datasets differed in the categories they contained, limited support for 130 

epithelial and myoepithelial labels partly reflects reference coverage. The Wu dataset was the 131 

only reference with labels that mapped to the epithelial and myoepithelial categories. 132 

Assignments to either category could therefore receive support from at most one of the four 133 

references. The spatial maps show the distribution of assigned categories and their supporting 134 

reference counts ( Fig. 2H,I ). 135 

Across the 12 sections, 3,316,221 Xenium cells met the minimum thresholds of 10 transcripts 136 

and five detected genes per cell and were assigned molecular labels. Of these, 2,466,189 137 

(74.4%) were successfully matched to H&E nuclear instances (Fig. 2D-G ). Incomplete 138 

correspondence between the two sets of cellular instances may reflect differences in 139 

segmentation, spatial registration, and the constraints imposed by one-to-one matching. 140

XenoType combines nuclear segmentation with independent histological representations 142 

XenoType separates nuclear segmentation from the image representations used for 143 

classification ( Fig. 1D ). A state-of-the-art cell segmentation model (CellViT-SAM-H 13 ) identifies 144 

nuclear instances, while an independently selected pathology foundation model extracts spatial 145 

image features. Crop selection and coordinate transformations associate each nuclear bounding 146 

box with the corresponding feature-token grid, and overlapping tokens are averaged to obtain a 147 

cell-level embedding. A classification head assigns a category to each cell based on its 148 

embedding to generate spatial classification maps. 149 

A preliminary model configuration search evaluated combinations of 10 feature-extraction 150 

configurations, four classification heads, and three loss functions, yielding results for 95 of 120 151 

possible combinations ( Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2 ). The combination of H-Optimus-1 37 152 

with a multilayer perceptron and cross-entropy loss achieved the highest reported validation 153 

micro-F1 of 0.785, compared with 0.713 for the native CellViT-SAM-H configuration using the 154 

same head and loss. These experiments used earlier annotations supplied with the 10x dataset 155 

and preceded construction of the final four-reference annotation resource. The selected H-156 

Optimus-1-MLP configuration was subsequently trained using the final molecularly inferred 157 

labels and evaluated on the eight held-out sections under the four- and seven-class taxonomies. 158

Classification agreement increases with annotation confidence and extends to ATERA-200 

profiled breast tissue 201 

Aggregate F1 scores weight all molecular reference annotations equally, regardless of the 202 

confidence with which they were assigned and, therefore, the reliability of the underlying label in 203 

the molecular reference annotations we construct. To investigate whether annotation confidence 204 

influences apparent model performance, we stratified results across the eight held-out sections 205 

by three proxies of annotation quality: the mean SingleR confidence score of the winning 206 

category across the four references used, the per-cell transcript count, and the number of 207 

uniquely detected genes ( Fig. 5A ). Micro-F1 increased consistently with all three measures, 208 

rising from approximately 0.40 at the lowest SingleR confidence scores to above 0.90 at the 209 

highest, with concordant trends for transcript counts and gene counts. The consistency of these 210 

relationships across three independent axes suggests that a substantial portion of model-211 

reference disagreements concentrate on cells whose molecular annotations are themselves 212 

least certain. The relationship between annotation quality and classification accuracy varied 213 

across cell types ( Supplementary Fig. S7 ). 214 

Tumor, endothelial, and stromal cells showed strong positive trends across all three axes, with 215 

F1 exceeding 0.90 at the highest confidence scores, suggesting that low accuracy at the bottom 216 

of each axis may partly reflect uncertainty in molecular reference annotations rather than model 217 

failure. Lymphocyte and myeloid accuracy improved with annotation confidence but plateaued 218 

or declined at high transcript counts, consistent with atypical cell states or segmentation artifacts. 219 

Epithelial and myoepithelial cells showed inverted-U or declining patterns, indicating that the 220 

boundary between these populations and tumor cells remains ambiguous even for cells with the 221 

richest molecular profiles, which is consistent with the biological continuum between them and 222 

the reliance of both labels on a single reference. 223

cellular categories and used as molecular reference annotations. XenoType achieved a micro-228

Discussion 236 

Accurate identification of cellular populations in histological images is essential for characterizing 237 

the composition and spatial organization of the tumor microenvironment 3,39 . Deep learning and 238 

recent pathology foundation models have driven substantial progress in automated nuclear 239 

detection and classification 12,40 , yet cell classifiers remain constrained by training data that rely 240 

on labor-intensive manual labels, subject to observer variability and generally limited to broad 241 

categories such as neoplastic, epithelial, connective, and inflammatory cells 15,16 . Recent 242 

advances in imaging-based spatial transcriptomics and multiplex immunofluorescence now 243 

resolve molecular identities at single-cell resolution within intact tissue, creating new 244 

opportunities to supervise histological cell classification without manual labels 22,41,42 . Initial 245 

studies have demonstrated this approach using multiplex immunofluorescence-derived labels, 246 

while Xenium-based efforts such as STHELAR, STimage, and SHEST, have linked 247 

transcriptomic cell identities with corresponding H&E images for dataset construction and 248 

classifier development 18,23,24 . However, important limitations persist. Many spatial molecular 249 

assays either require a separate tissue section or alter the H&E stain itself, since H&E is typically 250 

performed after the molecular profiling rounds, producing images that differ from routine 251 

histological preparations and complicating the direct transfer of molecularly derived annotations 252 

to standard clinical-grade H&E. At the same time, leading deep learning models for histological 253 

image analysis have been developed primarily for nuclear segmentation and derive their 254 

classification features from the same segmentation backbone, limiting the independent selection 255 

of representations best suited for distinguishing finer cellular populations 12,13,32 . These 256 

constraints have kept histological cell classification largely confined to broad morphological 257 

categories. 258

Here, we developed XenoType, a framework to classify cells in routine H&E images using 259 

training annotations derived from same-section Xenium spatial transcriptomics. Cell-type labels 260 

were reconciled from four independent scRNA-seq references using SingleR, consistent with a 261 

previous benchmark identifying it as the best-performing method among five evaluated 262 

approaches on Xenium data 28 . Decoupling feature extraction from segmentation allowed the 263 

independent selection of a pathology foundation model for cell-level representations, addressing 264 

a limitation of segmentation-centric architectures. XenoType outperformed CellViT++ retrained 265 

on the same labels under both the four-class and seven-class taxonomies, demonstrating that 266 

molecularly supervised classification can extend beyond conventional broad categories to finer 267 

cellular populations. XenoType ’s improved performance over retrained CellViT++ on the ATERA 268 

section, using cell-type annotations generated independently of our multi-reference annotation 269 

procedure, suggests that its performance gains are not specific to the annotation strategy used 270 

for training. Because molecular measurements are required only during dataset construction, 271 

inference uses the H&E image alone, supporting scalable cell-type mapping without per-272 

specimen molecular profiling. The same-section spatial transcriptomics design reduces 273 

registration uncertainties and staining artifacts inherent to serial-section or post-IF H&E 274 

workflows, enabling direct annotation transfer to standard-quality histological images. 275 

Several limitations should be noted. First, epithelial cells remained the worst performing category, 276 

and per-class analysis confirmed that accuracy did not improve with increasing transcriptomic 277 

depth ( Supplementary Fig. S7 ), suggesting that uncertainty in the reference annotations may 278 

contribute to the observed classification errors. The ∼280-gene Xenium panel may be insufficient 279 

to resolve epithelial from tumor cells - a distinction that also depends on copy number alterations 280 

and somatic mutations not captured by targeted expression panels 43 . Furthermore, off-target 281 

probe binding has been documented in current Xenium breast cancer gene panels, potentially 282 

introducing additional uncertainty into transcriptomics-derived cell annotations 44 . In addition, 283 

only one reference (Wu et al. 2) contained epithelial and myoepithelial categories, leaving these 284 

labels without the multi-reference support available for other populations 2,45 , consistent with the 285 

trends observed for both classes ( Supplementary Fig. S7 ). More broadly, model-reference 286 

agreement increased consistently with annotation confidence, although class-specific trends 287 

varied ( Supplementary Fig. S7 ), indicating that aggregate F1 scores partly reflect ground-truth 288 

uncertainty rather than model limitations alone. Second, the development and held-out 289

evaluation data originated from a single public collection. Although evaluation sections came 290 

from different patients than the development sections, shared sample preparation, staining, and 291 

imaging conditions limit the independence of the comparison. The ATERA slide, profiled on a 292 

different spatial transcriptomic platform, provides an initial out-of-distribution test, but multi-293 

institutional evaluation with diverse tissue preparation and staining protocols remains needed .294 

Finally, XenoType and retrained CellViT++ differ simultaneously in feature extractor and tiling 295 

strategy, so the observed improvements cannot be attributed to any single component without 296 

controlled ablation experiments 13 .297 

A number of directions could extend this work. Although the ATERA section provides initial cross-298 

platform evidence, evaluation on external cohorts from independent institutions remains needed 299 

to establish generalizability. Extending the framework to other tissue types - such as colorectal, 300 

lung, or pancreatic cancers - would test whether molecularly supervised classification transfers 301 

across different tumor microenvironments, while larger training datasets aggregating multiple 302 

spatial transcriptomics platforms could improve robustness. A key goal is to develop even finer 303 

cell categories, such as cancer molecular subtypes or functional immune states. The annotation-304 

confidence analysis ( Fig. 5A ) indicates that richer molecular profiles are associated with greater 305 

model-reference agreement, suggesting broader gene panels, integration of copy number 306 

variation and mutational information, and independent protein validation will be essential for this 307 

goal. Finally, coupling spatially resolved cell-type predictions with clinical metadata could link 308 

cellular composition to patient survival and treatment response, moving from methodological 309 

validation toward biological and clinical discovery. 310 

In summary, XenoType demonstrates that spatial transcriptomics-derived annotations can 311 

supervise histological classifiers capable of distinguishing molecularly informed cellular 312 

populations beyond conventional morphological categories. Model-reference agreement was 313 

correlated with annotation confidence, indicating that aggregate F1 scores partly reflect ground-314 

truth uncertainty, and initial out-of-distribution evaluation supported cross-platform 315 

generalization. Although broader validation remains necessary, these findings support the 316 

potential of molecularly supervised learning to extend the cellular resolution of conventional 317 

histology and provide a foundation for investigating tissue heterogeneity at scale. 318

Methods 431 

Data acquisition and image preprocessing 432 

We used the publicly available Xenium v1 Human Breast FFPE with Biomarkers &433 

Housekeeping Genes Custom Panel dataset generated by 10x Genomics 46 . The collection 434 

comprises 12 human breast tissue sections from 12 donors, including eight ductal carcinoma in 435 

situ (DCIS) specimens, three invasive ductal carcinomas (IDC), and one normal breast specimen. 436 

Tissue sections were profiled using Xenium In Situ with a custom 280-gene panel and 437 

subsequently stained and imaged with H&E, providing paired spatial transcriptomic and 438 

histological data. Publicly available cell-level expression matrices, transcript coordinates, 439 

nuclear boundaries, and H&E whole-slide images were used for downstream analysis. 440 

Specimen characteristics and acquisition procedures are described in the original dataset 441 

publication 19 .442 

Spatial correspondence between Xenium measurements and H&E images was established 443 

through manual landmark-based registration using Xenium Explorer (10x Genomics). 444 

Corresponding anatomical landmarks were identified in the Xenium DAPI and H&E images, from 445 

which slide-specific similarity transformations were estimated by least-squares fitting. The 446 

resulting 3 x 3 affine transformation matrices were exported and used, together with the 447 

corresponding image pixel resolutions, to map cell and nuclear coordinates between the two 448 

imaging modalities. Four tissue sections were selected for model development, while the 449 

remaining eight sections were reserved for independent evaluation and excluded from training, 450 

validation, and hyperparameter selection. Section identifiers, specimen characteristics, and 451 

experimental allocation are provided in Supplementary Table S1 .452 

For external evaluation, we used a publicly available FFPE breast cancer section profiled using 453 

the preproduction ATERA Whole Transcriptome Assay (10x Genomics) 47 . The specimen 454 

comprised ductal carcinoma in situ (ER+/PR+/HER2-), with spatial expression measurements 455 

targeting 18,028 genes and corresponding H&E imaging. The section was not used for model 456 

development or hyperparameter selection. 457 

Single-cell reference preparation 458

Four breast cancer single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) reference datasets (Wu 2, Chen 33 ,459 

Dong 34 , and Yan 35 ) were used for molecular cell-type annotation. Cell identities were obtained 460 

from the original annotations provided with each reference dataset and harmonized into seven 461 

cellular categories: tumor, epithelial, myoepithelial, endothelial, myeloid, lymphocyte, and 462 

stromal cells. Reference accessions, original cell-type annotations, and their correspondence to 463 

the harmonized categories are provided in Supplementary Table S2 .464 

Reference cells were retained if they contained at least 200 detected genes and 500 counts, 465 

with mitochondrial counts comprising no more than 20% of the total. Cells with missing or 466 

uninformative annotations were excluded. Expression data were normalized using log-467 

normalization (scale factor = 10,000), and each reference was restricted to genes shared with 468 

the Xenium panel. Reference sampling was capped at 15,000 cells per label. Principal 469 

component analysis and UMAP were used to visualize reference structure. 470 

471 

Xenium cell annotation and reference agreement 472 

We retained Xenium cells with at least 10 transcripts and 5 detected genes. We annotated each 473 

eligible expression profile independently against the four single-cell RNA-sequencing references 474 

using SingleR 36 . We mapped the reference-specific predictions to the seven-category taxonomy 475 

and combined them by voting. We resolved ties using the mean SingleR score for each tied 476 

category, followed by the predefined reference priority order (Yan, Wu, Dong, and Chen) if a tie 477 

remained. When the Wu reference assigned a cell to the epithelial or myoepithelial category, we 478 

used that assignment as the final label. 479 

We counted the number of references that supported each final assignment and classified the 480 

agreement into high (four reference agreement), medium (three reference agreement), and low 481 

(fewer than three reference agreement). Because each reference could support only the 482 

categories represented in its mapped labels, the maximum possible agreement varied by 483 

category. Supplementary Table S2 lists the categories available in each reference. 484

Nuclear segmentation and annotation transfer 486

Nuclear instances were detected in H&E whole-slide images using the pretrained CellViT-SAM-487 

H-x40-AMP model 13 . To associate histologically detected nuclei with molecularly annotated 488 

Xenium cells, spatial correspondence was established using slide-specific affine transformation 489 

matrices. Nuclear coordinates were transformed into the Xenium coordinate system for nearest-490 

centroid matching, while the resulting annotations were retained in the H&E coordinate system. 491 

Each detected nucleus was matched to its nearest Xenium cell using a k-dimensional tree, with 492 

a maximum matching distance of 15 μm. When multiple detected nuclei were associated with 493 

the same Xenium cell, only the closest match was retained. A 10-μm margin around the Xenium 494 

field of view was applied during spatial eligibility filtering. Successfully matched H&E nuclei 495 

inherited the corresponding Xenium cell identifiers and molecularly inferred cell-type labels, 496 

while the original segmentation was preserved for subsequent classifier training and evaluation. 497 

498 

Tile extraction and dataset curation 499 

Candidate image tiles measuring 1,024 x 1,024 pixels were extracted at a stride of 256 pixels. 500 

Tiles containing fewer than 10 annotated cells or more than 30% whitespace were excluded, 501 

with whitespace filtering performed using an intensity threshold of 220 ( Supplementary Fig. S4 ). 502 

An additional nuclear area filter was configured to identify unusually large instances using a 503 

threshold of three standard deviations above the mean. Candidate selection incorporated 504 

Shannon entropy calculated from the proportions of annotated cell categories, favoring tiles 505 

containing diverse cellular populations. 506 

A greedy selection procedure was used to construct spatially nonoverlapping tile subsets, with 507 

an eight-pixel separation buffer. The resulting dataset comprised 372 training tiles and 66 508 

validation tiles, containing 122,398 and 19,935 annotated cells, respectively, according to the 509 

dataset creation report. Sampling targets of approximately 21,000 training cells and 4,300 510 

validation cells per category guided tile selection, although the final class distributions remained 511 

unequal. All seven categories were represented in both partitions. Training and validation tiles 512 

originated from the same four specimens but occupied nonoverlapping image regions. The final 513 

class distributions and slide-level allocations are reported in Supplementary Table S3 .514

XenoType architecture and cellular feature assignment 516 

XenoType decouples nuclear segmentation from histological feature extraction by combining 517 

CellViT-SAM-H-derived nuclear instances with representations generated by an independently 518 

selected pathology foundation model. The selected configuration used H-Optimus-1 as a frozen 519 

feature extractor and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) for seven-class cell classification 48 .520 

For H-Optimus-1 feature extraction, CellViT-normalized H&E images were restored to RGB 521 

intensity values and resampled to 0.5 μm/pixel using bilinear interpolation. The rescaled images 522 

were divided into overlapping 224 by 224-pixel crops, with crop positions distributed uniformly 523 

to ensure complete image coverage without padding and a maximum stride of 112 pixels and H-524 

Optimus-1 was applied independently to each crop. Auxiliary transformer tokens were excluded, 525 

and the remaining spatial tokens were organized into a 16 x 16 grid of 1,536-dimensional 526 

representations. 527 

To associate these representations with individual nuclei, CellViT-SAM-H bounding boxes and 528 

centroids were transformed into the rescaled image coordinate system. Each nucleus was 529 

preferentially assigned to a 224 x 224-pixel H&E crop (112 x 112 μm at 0.5 μm/pixel) that fully 530 

contained its bounding box. If multiple crops qualified, we selected the one with the greatest 531 

minimum distance between the bounding box and the crop edges. When no crop fully contained 532 

the bounding box, the crop containing the nuclear centroid with the greatest boundary margin 533 

was selected. The bounding box was subsequently mapped onto the spatial token grid, and the 534 

embeddings of all tokens whose image patches intersected the bounding box were averaged to 535 

generate a single 1,536-dimensional embedding per nucleus. Cell ordering was preserved 536 

throughout feature extraction to maintain correspondence with the original nuclear detections. 537 

The resulting embeddings were passed to an MLP comprising a 64-unit hidden layer, rectified 538 

linear activation, dropout, and a seven-class output layer. Predicted cell identities were 539 

associated with their corresponding nuclear instances to generate spatially resolved 540 

classification maps. 541

Model training and configuration selection 543

To select the architecture described above, we first compared combinations of 10 feature-544 

extraction configurations , four classification heads, and three loss functions, completing 95 of 545 

the 120 possible combinations ( Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2 ). This preliminary search used 546 

the original Xenium cell-type annotations harmonized into the seven-category taxonomy 547 

(Supplementary Fig. S5 ). H-Optimus-1 with an MLP head and cross-entropy loss achieved the 548 

highest validation micro-F1 (0.785) among the evaluated combinations and was selected for 549 

subsequent training. Hyperparameter optimization and experiment tracking were performed 550 

using Weights & Biases 49 .551 

We then trained the selected H-Optimus-1-MLP configuration on the final SingleR-derived labels 552 

for seven-class classification using cross-entropy loss and AdamW optimizer. The classifier used 553 

a 64-unit hidden layer with a dropout probability of 0.4544. Training was configured for a 554 

maximum of 100 epochs, with a batch size of 256, learning rate of 3.012 × 10 ⁻⁴, weight decay 555 

of 0.0253, and AdamW momentum parameters β₁ = 0.9 and β₂ = 0.999. Cosine learning-rate 556 

scheduling, mixed-precision computation, and early stopping with a patience of five validation 557 

epochs were applied. A random seed of 15 was used, and class-weighted sampling was disabled. 558 

Histological feature vectors were cached, and the classification head was optimized using the 559 

resulting cell-level embeddings. 560 

The checkpoint with the highest validation micro-F1 score was retained for evaluation. The best 561 

checkpoint was obtained at epoch 19, with a validation F1 of 0.7545, and training stopped after 562 

epoch 24. 563

Model evaluation 565 

XenoType was compared with default CellViT++ and a CellViT++ classifier retrained using 566 

Xenium-derived molecular annotations. Classification was evaluated using four broad cellular 567 

categories and the seven molecularly informed categories. For the four-class evaluation, tumor 568 

cells were assigned to the neoplastic category, epithelial and myoepithelial cells to epithelial, 569 

endothelial and stromal cells to connective, and myeloid cells and lymphocytes to inflammatory. 570 

For each of the eight held-out sections, we matched predicted nuclei to H&E nuclear instances 571 

carrying transferred molecular annotations. We identified candidate pairs with centroid distances 572

of at most 15 pixels in the H&E coordinate system and accepted pairs in ascending order of 573 

distance, allowing each instance to be matched at most once. We performed matching 574 

separately for each model and computed classification scores on its matched pairs. In the four-575 

class evaluation, we excluded pairs with predictions labeled Dead (from CellViT++) after 576 

matching. 577 

We computed micro-F1, macro-F1, and class-specific F1 for each section using the TorchMetrics 578 

multiclass F1 implementation. We report the unweighted mean and sample standard deviation 579 

of the section-level scores and pooled matched-cell counts across sections before calculating 580 

row-normalized percentages. 581 

We compared models using exact, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on F1 scores paired 582 

by tissue section (eight sections). The four-class analysis included all three model pairs, and the 583 

seven-class analysis compared XenoType with retrained CellViT++. For each model pair, we 584 

tested micro-F1, macro-F1, and class-specific F1. We excluded zero paired differences, 585 

assigned average ranks to tied absolute differences, and enumerated all sign assignments to 586 

calculate exact P values. We report unadjusted P values and interpret the comparisons as 587 

exploratory. 588 

To assess cross-platform generalization, the models were additionally evaluated on the ATERA 589 

breast cancer section without further training. Cell-type annotations provided by 10x Genomics 590 

were harmonized into the seven cellular categories and transferred to H&E nuclear instances. 591 

XenoType and retrained CellViT++ were evaluated under both the four- and seven-class 592 

taxonomies, with default CellViT++ additionally included in the four-class comparison. 593 

Classification performance was quantified using the same centroid-matching procedure and F1 594 

metrics described above. 595

To examine the relationship between annotation confidence and classification performance, we 598 

stratified matched cells from the eight held-out sections by three proxies of annotation quality: 599 

the mean SingleR confidence score of the winning category across the four references, the per-600 

cell Xenium transcript count, and the number of uniquely detected genes. Per-cell transcript 601

Software and data availability 613 

Spatial transcriptomic processing, reference-based annotation, and histological image analysis 614 

were implemented using R and Python. SingleR was used for reference-based cell annotation, 615 

while PyTorch-based implementations of CellViT-SAM-H, H-Optimus-1, and the classification 616 

heads were used for histological inference and model training. Training configurations, metrics, 617 

and experiment metadata were recorded using Weights & Biases. 618 

The Xenium v1 Human Breast FFPE with Biomarkers & Housekeeping Genes Custom Panel 619 

dataset 46 and the ATERA In Situ Gene Expression FFPE Human Breast Cancer preview 620 

dataset 38 , together with their paired H&E images, are publicly available from 10x Genomics. The 621 

XenoType implementation, trained checkpoints, derived molecular annotations, and processed 622 

histological datasets are available upon request. 623
