## Abstract 

Pathology-image-to-gene-expression (P2G) studies often change the prediction head, image encoder, and spatial context together, making performance differences difficult to attribute. We examine these factors through paired image-input controls and explicit input regimes. Our main comparison covers five HEST-1K datasets, two 200-gene panels, and three selected held-out slides per dataset, using validation-selected checkpoints. Across all five datasets, replacing conventional image inputs with Virchow2 improves mean PCC-200 in 39 of 40 method–dataset–panel comparisons, while one compari-son decreases. We introduce SimpleP2G , a direct gene-query decoder over frozen embeddings, and SimpleP2G-cross , which adds spatially positioned neighboring embeddings. With shared Virchow2 fea-tures, SimpleP2G has the highest single-patch mean PCC-200 on all five datasets under both panels; the cross-patch variant leads on four of five datasets under each panel. Decoder and context ablations show that greater depth and broader context do not consistently improve prediction. Together, these results suggest improving im-age representations before adding prediction-head complexity: a direct decoder over frozen pathology embeddings already provides a strong baseline. We hope our reimplemented image-encoder-upgraded baselines and SimpleP2G models serve as transparent,

## 1 Introduction 

H&E histology is routinely collected in tissue studies and clinical pathology, whereas spatial transcriptomics (ST) provides costly molecular measurements at aligned tissue locations. Predicting spatially resolved gene expression from H&E images—pathology-image-to-gene-expression (P2G) prediction—therefore offers a prac-tical way to connect abundant morphology with lower-throughput molecular profiling. Recent P2G systems have invested heavily in the prediction stage: contrastive retrieval, diffusion or flow generation, gene-ranking objectives, and spatial graph or Transformer architectures. Yet these methods frequently differ in more than the proposed head. A newer system may also receive features from a stronger pathology foun-dation model, use neighboring patches or whole-slide context, and follow a different training schedule. Attributing such a compound improvement to head design alone can be misleading. We therefore

BLEEP on Brain HMHVG instead decreases from 0.3376 to 0.3258, motivating within-method com-parisons that hold the split, objective, and training schedule fixed. To make the comparison concrete, we introduce SimpleP2G , a direct baseline. A frozen image encoder produces one patch embed-ding; a small MLP converts it to conditioning tokens; and learnable gene queries cross-attend to those tokens before predicting expres-sion. There is no trainable image backbone, retrieval bank, or itera-tive sampling procedure. For cross-patch prediction, SimpleP2G-cross keeps the decoder and loss unchanged while expanding the conditioning sequence to include spatially positioned neighboring patches and, optionally, a slide-summary token. This design pro-vides a transparent reference for assessing how much complexity is needed after choosing a strong image representation. Our main experiments compare ten models on Brain, PRAD, HER2ST, Lung, and ccRCC under two gene panels, with separate single-patch and cross-patch settings. Every method uses frozen Virchow2 image features and the same three selected test-slide folds, training-only gene panels, and validation assignments. Both settings therefore share the same dataset coverage. Decoder and context ablations use one fold per dataset to examine depth, objec-tive, neighborhood size, and slide information separately. We summarize our contributions as follows: 

• We quantify the effect of replacing image representations within existing prediction heads. All 120 paired folds are evaluated, giving 39 positive and one negative mean change across 40 method–dataset–panel combinations. 

• We introduce SimpleP2G and SimpleP2G-cross, direct gene-query decoders over frozen image embeddings. They pro-vide competitive references in both input settings, while ablations identify cases where simpler heads or less context perform better. 

• We compare eight baseline implementations and our two models across five HEST-1K datasets and two gene panels, reporting variation across three held-out slides. Gene-DML retains its target, neighbor, and global pathways in the cross-patch setting; STRank and M2TGLGO adaptations are documented explicitly. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Target-Patch and Cross-Patch P2G 

Early work established that local morphology contains predictive signal for spatial expression. ST-Net [7] treats each measured lo-cation as an image-to-gene regression example, whereas HisTo-Gene [19] lets spots from the same tissue section interact through a spatially informed Transformer. These works define two distinct in-formation settings: a single-patch predictor observes only the patch centered at the queried spot; a cross-patch predictor also observes other image regions from the section. We retain this distinction throughout because an architectural comparison is meaningful only when the available image evidence is matched. Later single-patch methods replace direct regression with more elaborate objectives. BLEEP [31] learns a shared image–expression space through contrastive training and predicts expression by re-trieval. Stem [35] uses conditional diffusion, GenAR [18] uses au-toregressive generation, and STRank [16] emphasizes relative gene ordering. These objectives impose useful inductive biases, but they may also restrict how encoder information reaches the prediction: retrieval is bounded by a reference bank, while iterative gener-ators add a costly inference path. Our single-patch experiments ask whether their reported advantage remains after their image representation is upgraded. Recent research increasingly exploits larger spatial context. Gene-DML [23] combines target, neighbor, and global image pathways with multi-level discrimination and therefore belongs to the cross-patch setting. STFlow [9] performs whole-slide flow matching with spatial attention. MERGE [6] constructs a hierarchical graph from spatial and feature-space relations, and M2TGLGO [21] com-bines spatial topology with gene-level relations. HistoPrism [8] and FmH2ST [30] further explore slide- or foundation-model-level information. Such methods may legitimately outperform target-only predictors because they solve a richer problem. We therefore compare them in the cross-patch setting and, where their inter-faces permit, evaluate both their released/default image input and Virchow2. SimpleP2G-cross uses an explicit target-plus-neighbor conditioning sequence; baselines that natively consume the full section retain that larger context, so the simple control is not given an information advantage. 

## 2.2 Image Representation versus Prediction Head 

Pretrained representations are increasingly used for medical image understanding [3, 4] and molecular prediction [33, 34]. In pathology, UNI [2], CONCH [13], Virchow/Virchow2 [29, 36], and Hibou [15] learn from large histology collections; DINOv2 [17], DINOv3 [22], and SAM [11] provide strong general-vision controls. In P2G, image representations are often treated as interchange-able implementation details, even though changing them can al-ter both prediction accuracy and the behavior of a downstream method. The issue is especially important for feature-dependent heads: nearest-neighbor retrieval changes when the embedding geometry changes, and a graph method may construct different edges or clusters from a new feature space. Accordingly, we distin-guish two questions. An encoder upgrade keeps a method’s down-stream computation fixed and measures how much it benefits from a stronger input representation. A head comparison fixes the encoder and accounts explicitly for the context available to each method; when a native whole-slide method uses more evidence, we retain that advantage rather than claiming an exactly matched input. This two-step comparison supports a more defensible claim than ei-ther an unpaired leaderboard or the assertion that encoder quality always dominates.

## 4 Experiments 4.1 Datasets 

Our main evaluation uses five public spatial transcriptomics datasets from HEST-1k [10]: Brain, PRAD, HER2ST, Lung, and ccRCC. They span five organs, human and mouse tissue, and three sequenc-ing platforms (Visium, Spatial Transcriptomics v1, and Xenium), comprising 117 tissue sections and 226,656 measured spots. We evaluate three selected leave-one-slide-out folds per dataset. Each fold reserves one additional slide for validation and trains on the remaining slides. The assignments are fixed before training and shared by all methods; these are slide-level rather than patient-level holdouts.

## 4.3 Implementation Details 

Models. Main comparisons and decoder/context ablations use a shared frozen Virchow2 encoder for every available image input. SimpleP2G uses its 2560-dimensional embeddings, a 12-layer de-coder of width 384, six attention heads, and four target-conditioning tokens. SimpleP2G-cross adds 24 spatial nearest neighbors; relative coordinates are normalized by median spot spacing. Unless speci-fied otherwise, SimpleP2G-cross uses target and neighbor inputs without a slide token. The optional slide token averages same-slide spot embeddings. 

Training. All methods train for 100 epochs except Stem, which uses 200. A separate validation slide selects the checkpoint with the highest recorded validation PCC-200. Baseline learning rates follow their released implementations; SimpleP2G’s dataset-specific settings are listed in Appendix A. SimpleP2G-cross uses learning rate 5 × 10 −5, weight decay 0.05, and dropout 0.05 throughout. Both SimpleP2G variants use AdamW [12], gradient clipping at 1.0, and EMA. Cross-patch training samples slide-level chunks of up to 256 targets, while single-patch training shuffles pooled-spot batches. 

Batching and Checkpoints. In SimpleP2G, pooled batches con-tain 256 spots and use an augmentation ratio of 2. For SimpleP2G-cross, each slide contributes eight chunks per epoch, with the target, neighbors, and optional slide summary drawn from that slide. A slide is the batch item for slide-based baselines. M2TGLGO caps this item at 1,024 spots, while BLEEP retrieves training examples from its expression bank. These batching choices preserve the native units required by the different heads. The training split supplies expression targets and any training bank. Validation determines only the checkpoint. Ties select the earliest epoch, and each se-lected checkpoint is evaluated once on the corresponding test slide without refitting. 

## 4.4 Strong Impact of Image Encoder 

Table 1 replaces the image representation in four prediction heads with Virchow2. Reference inputs are ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50 for BLEEP, raw patches for HisToGene, features from MERGE’s released TCGA-pretrained ResNet18 checkpoint, and ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50 following M2TGLGO’s released backbone choice. Each pair retains the downstream head, objective, split, and training schedule while adapting the input projection. Retrieval neighbors and feature-derived graph structure are recomputed when required. Arrows report three-slide means with SDs; Δ is the absolute paired mean PCC change. Across 120 paired folds, Virchow2 improves the three-slide mean in 39 of 40 dataset–method–panel comparisons. Absolute mean changes range from −0.0118 to +0.3113. HisToGene on HER2ST HMHVG increases from 0.1209 to 0.4321, while BLEEP on HER2ST HVG increases from 0.2050 to 0.2129. The exception is BLEEP on Brain HMHVG, which decreases from 0.3376 to 0.3258. These com-parisons demonstrate broad, method- and tissue-dependent repre-sentation gains. The underlying slide pairs show how often a positive average agrees with the individual results. PCC-200 increases on 115 of 120 matched slides, with increases on all three slides in 35 of the 40 dataset–method–panel combinations. The five remaining combina-tions improve on two slides each. For example, the three changes for BLEEP on Brain HMHVG are +0.0645, +0.0241, and −0.1242. The negative mean for this combination reflects the magnitude of the third change. Across the paired runs, MSE decreases in 98 of 120 comparisons and MAE decreases in 96 of 120. These error metrics reflect expression magnitude, while PCC measures pattern agreement.

Among the four single-patch methods in Table 2, SimpleP2G obtains the highest mean PCC-200 on all five datasets under both panels. ## 4.6 Cross-Patch Results 

SimpleP2G-cross obtains the highest mean PCC-200 on four of five datasets under each panel in Table 3. M2TGLGO is strongest on PRAD HMHVG and ccRCC HVG. The five-dataset HMHVG/HVG means are 0.5789/0.2722 for SimpleP2G-cross, 0.5685/0.2588 for M2TGLGO, and 0.5538/0.2596 for Gene-DML. These results support competitive performance with tissue-specific exceptions; three se-lected slides provide descriptive variation rather than evidence of statistical superiority. 

## 4.7 Image Encoder Ablation 

Table 4 compares six image encoders within SimpleP2G across all five datasets and both panels, using fold 0 and seed 42. Virchow2 has the highest PCC-200 in seven of ten combinations, including both panels on PRAD, HER2ST, and Lung. UNI leads on Brain HMHVG (0.3859 versus 0.3803 for Virchow2) and ccRCC HMHVG (0.3381 versus 0.2704). DINOv2-L leads on Brain HVG (0.1712 versus 0.1690). Thus, the encoder ranking depends on the tissue and panel, and a general image encoder can outperform pathology-specific encoders in an individual comparison.

## 4.9 Gain from Spatial Context 

Table 6 compares neighbors alone, the target with 𝐾 ∈ { 8, 24 , 48 }

neighbors, and the target with 24 neighbors plus a slide summary across all five datasets and both panels. The loss, decoder speci-fication, and training protocol are fixed; changing branches also changes parameters and the initialization sequence. Adding the target to the 𝐾 = 24 neighbors-only model improves PCC-200 in all ten combinations. Adding a slide token to target+ 𝐾 24 improves seven combinations: HER2ST HVG rises from 0.3823 to 0.3897, whereas HMHVG decreases from 0.7352 to 0.7308. PRAD HMHVG also decreases, from 0.6219 to 0.5881. Increasing neigh-borhood size is similarly nonmonotonic. Context type and tissue therefore matter beyond the amount of available image information. 

## 4.10 Qualitative Analysis 

We pair ResNet50 and Virchow2 on HER2ST fold 0 ( SPA148 ) using the same SimpleP2G-cross head, training-selected gene panel and validation checkpoint rule. For target+ 𝐾 24, replacing ResNet50 with Virchow2 increases PCC-200 by +0.0130 on HMHVG and 

+0.0224 on HVG. PCC improves for 137/200 and 133/199 evaluable genes, respectively. Adding a slide token changes the paired gains to 

+0.0187 and +0.0158. These are within-slide effects under matched training and serve a different purpose from the three-slide encoder comparison in Table 1. Figure 3 and table 7 show the three prespecified examples: IGKC, associated with immunoglobulin expression, LYZ, associated with innate immunity, and TFF3, associated with epithelial secretion [24– 26]. The maps include all 295 measured spots with a shared color scale for each gene. In the target+ 𝐾 24 setting, Virchow2 improves both PCC and RMSE for IGKC and LYZ. TFF3 improves in PCC, while RMSE rises from 1.5775 to 1.6090. Other transcripts can de-teriorate on both metrics: LTA4H PCC decreases from 0.5930 to 0.5555 while RMSE increases from 0.4552 to 0.4724.

## 5 Conclusion 

We studied how image representation, prediction head, and spatial context affect P2G prediction. Across five datasets and both gene panels, Virchow2 improves 39 of 40 paired method–dataset–panel means. The six-encoder ablation also shows that the best represen-tation varies with tissue and panel. Under shared Virchow2 features, SimpleP2G achieves the highest single-patch mean PCC-200 across five datasets and both gene panels. SimpleP2G-cross leads on four datasets per panel, with tissue- and panel-specific exceptions. The decoder and context ablations show that greater depth, Gaussian NLL, and a slide token each help in several settings but are not uniformly preferable. Our central recommendation is to start with a better image representation before adding a more complex prediction head. A direct decoder over frozen pathol-ogy embeddings already provides a strong baseline against which more elaborate heads should be evaluated. These comparisons con-cern the specified implementations and validation-selected training protocols; the three selected slide splits do not establish patient-independent or external-cohort generalization. We hope our reimplemented image-encoder-upgraded baselines and SimpleP2G models serve as transparent, reproducible, and strong controls for future P2G research.

## B Baseline Implementations 

The baseline training recipes use a common validation-based check-point rule and retain each method’s prediction objective. The details below specify the model inputs and any adaptations used in the benchmark. 

BLEEP. A contrastive loss trains the image and gene projections in a shared space of dimension 256. At inference, BLEEP retrieves the 64 most similar training spots in the projected image space and softmax-weights their expression vectors by cosine similarity. The reference trains an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50 on 224 ×

224 patches with the projection heads. In the paired run, frozen Virchow2 features supply the image projection input. Both use a learning rate and weight decay of 10 −3.

Stem. The gene tokens combine a learned gene embedding with a projection of noisy counts. A 12-block diffusion transformer con-ditions on the target image and diffusion timestep through adaptive layer normalization, with width 384, six heads and EMA decay 0.9999. Each draw takes 1,000 diffusion steps. Validation uses one sample per spot with a fixed random stream, while test predictions average 20 samples. 

STRank. An affine predictor is trained on raw counts with the released stranklist_v2 ranking loss, using within-slide permuta-tions to sample pairs. AdamW uses a fixed learning rate of 5 × 10 −5

and weight decay 0.01. Inference receives only the target embedding. PCC compares the raw ranking scores with the common log-count targets. The scores are relative expression estimates.

Train-ing retains target and cluster discrimination, supervised branch outputs, and their fused prediction. The cluster objective uses 18 clusters and weight 1.0. Adam uses learning rate 10 −4 with a step de-cay of 0.95 every 20 epochs. The fused predictor supplies validation and test scores.

## D Exploratory Pathway Analysis 

We test whether genes benefiting from the Virchow2 slide token on HER2ST SPA148 concentrate in particular human pathways. We match gene symbols to Reactome release 94 [20] and keep genes with variable observed expression and a pathway annotation. Of the 156 annotated HMHVG and 139 annotated HVG genes, 52 and 67, respectively, show a positive PCC change. Two-sided Fisher exact tests compare the frequency of gains inside and outside pathways with at least five background genes in each group. None of the 395 tests (283 HMHVG and 112 HVG) reaches 𝑞 < 0.05 after joint Benjamini–Hochberg correction (minimum 𝑞 = 0.9687).
