Abstract
Representation learning for single-cell RNA-seq has been evaluated by the iden-
tities representations capture — cell types, lineages, perturbations — rather than
how faithfully they model the distribution of cell states on the gene expression
manifold. As a result, density estimation, rare-state detection, perturbation scoring,
and gene-level attribution are bolted onto frozen embeddings as separate models,
disconnected from the geometry of the data. We argue that modeling the distribu-
tion of cell states should be the primary objective, and that representation quality
follows from it. Building on LeJEPA, whose SIGReg objective enforces a provably
optimal isotropic Gaussian latent, we introduce LeCellModel, which reads the
encoder’s Jacobian log-volume (JEPA-SCORE) under this Gaussian latent as a
relative typicality score. Across several datasets, LeCellModel outperforms estab-
lished methods on the scIB benchmark for representation quality. Furthermore,
we show that LeCellModel enables detection of rare expression states using JEPA-
SCORE as a typicality measure, applying it to a controlled cytokine panel, where
it identifies stimulated cells across minority fractions and tracks pathway-response
magnitude. We introduce gene-level interpretability of the typicality score — to
our knowledge, the first method for analyzing JEPA-SCORE in feature space.
Leveraging this framework, LeCellModel recapitulates the known association of a
profibrotic macrophage program with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and discovers
a CD177+ activated regulatory T cell subpopulation previously described only in
tumor contexts. Both findings emerge directly from the geometry of the learned
embedding, without relying on supervised annotations. The code is available at
https://github.com/gilk717/lecellmodel_paper.

Introduction
Single-cell RNA sequencing measures gene expression across thousands of individual cells, producing
high-dimensional count data that capture the molecular state of each cell. A central computational
task is to learn representations of these states that organize cells into biologically meaningful structure:
separating cell types, resolving developmental trajectories, and exposing responses to perturbation.
The dominant approach has been to train encoder-decoder models with reconstruction objectives,
from variational autoencoders such as scVI [Lopez et al., 2018] and scANVI [Xu et al., 2021] to
BERT-style foundation models such as Geneformer [Theodoris et al., 2023] and scGPT [Cui et al.,
2024]. Reconstruction, however, operates entirely in gene space, which encourages the encoder
to retain nuisance variation such as batch effects and dropout patterns alongside biological signal.
Adversarial batch correction methods like scDREAMER [Shree et al., 2023] partly offset this, but the
adversary works against a reconstruction objective that itself rewards retaining batch-related variation
to minimize error. Finally, there are emerging approaches, such as GeneJEPA [Litman et al., 2025]

and CellJEPA [ElSheikh et al., 2026], following the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)
paradigm that forgo the decoder entirely, shaping the embedding space through latent prediction
rather than reconstruction. Crucially, all these methods are judged primarily by the identities their
representations capture, rather than by how faithfully they describe the distribution of cell states.
A remarkable yet largely unexploited property of JEPAs is that, when the latent space is con-
strained to an isotropic Gaussian, the encoder provably estimates the density of the training distri-
bution [Balestriero et al., 2025]. We are the first to leverage this property in a practical single-cell
setting, introducing LeCellModel: a JEPA-based model for learning the distribution of cell states. Le-
CellModel builds on LeJEPA [Balestriero and LeCun, 2025], which unlike previous single-cell JEPAs
avoids heuristics such as stop-gradient to prevent collapse, replacing them with a principled isotropic
Gaussian latent enforced via Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization (SIGReg) [Balestriero and
LeCun, 2025]. In addition, rather than scaling to large transformer architectures, we show that
a lightweight MLP encoder is sufficient for single-cell data, keeping the model tractable and the
Jacobian computable. Across several datasets on the scIB benchmark [scverse contributors, 2024],
LeCellModel achieves state-of-the-art representation quality, while its typicality score, read within a
cell type, enables practical detection of rare cell states. Moreover, we show that differentiating the
score with respect to input gene expression attributes local changes in typicality to individual genes,
yielding per-gene attribution in the input’s native coordinates. Thus, a single trained encoder serves
simultaneously as a representation, a typicality score, and an attribution map.
Contributions.
• A density-estimation strategy for cell states. We propose a strategy for estimating the
relative density of cell states that relies on JEPA-SCORE [Balestriero et al., 2025], the
implicit density estimator of Gaussian-embedding JEPAs. Combined with biologically
meaningful data augmentations and batch-effect controls, JEPA-SCORE yields a relative
estimate of cell-state density on the learned manifold, which we read within a cell type as
typicality, despite the stochastic, sparse, and batch-confounded nature of the data.
• Differentiable gene-level attribution of density. A key novelty of LeCellModel is that
the density score is itself differentiable with respect to gene expression. We therefore
differentiate it to identify, for each cell, the genes whose local expression changes most
affect its JEPA-SCORE, turning a cell-level relative density estimate into direct gene-level
attribution. Unlike differential expression, it requires no predefined contrast group.
• LeCellModel: a Gaussian-latent JEPA for single-cell RNA-seq. We instantiate this idea
in a teacher-free, single-network JEPA combining a lightweight MLP backbone, gene-space
augmentations, SIGReg [Balestriero and LeCun, 2025], and adversarial batch correction.
The architecture is light enough to train per-dataset and to retain a tractable Jacobian, which
is what makes the density readout practical (Section 2.3).
• Cell identity preserved at atlas scale. LeCellModel preserves cell identity while integrating
across batches at atlas scale, leading scIB Total on HLCA Core (584K cells) and Heart v2
(704K cells, 32 preparations), and tying scVI on Pancreas; it also leads GeneJEPA trained
on the same data and the foundation models scGPT and UCE (Section 3.2; App. K).
• Density-driven discovery in disease tissue. JEPA-SCORE recovers stimulated cells in a
controlled cytokine panel and tracks pathway-response magnitude. Applied to idiopathic
pulmonary fibrosis lung, it surfaces a profibrotic monocyte-derived macrophage program
and a CD177+ activated-Treg subpopulation previously described only in tumors — both
identified purely from the geometry of the embedding (Sections 3.3–3.4).

Methods
LeCellModel is built on the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) framework [Balestriero
and LeCun, 2025]. JEPAs learn representations by requiring augmented views of the same input to
be mutually predictable in embedding space — without reconstructing the input. For single-cell data,
this is natural: since a cell’s identity is encoded across many correlated genes, randomly masked
and Poisson-resampled views retain enough signal to agree on the same cell state. In LeCellModel,
all augmented views are trained to converge to a shared projection center; no momentum teacher or
asymmetric predictor head is used. SIGReg regularizes the projection distribution toward an isotropic
Gaussian — preventing collapse, enforcing diversity both across embedding dimensions and between

2.1
Problem setup and notation
Let x ∈RG denote a cell’s raw expression vector over G highly variable genes, b ∈{1, . . . , B} its
batch label, and c ∈{1, . . . , C} an optional biological grouping (cell type, or a coarser grouping such
as lineage). We train an encoder fθ : RG →Rd and a projector gϕ : Rd →Rk, and write F = gϕ◦fθ
for their composition, so that z = fθ(x) and p = F(x). An adversary head hψ : Rd →RB predicts
batch from the representation. Encoder, projector, and adversary head are all lightweight two-layer
MLPs; full architectures are in App. C.
2.2
View generation and augmentation
Each cell yields ng = 1 global and nℓ= 6 local views through biologically motivated augmentations
on raw counts: Poisson resampling (sequencing-depth stochasticity), library-size normalization
followed by log(1 + x), and random gene masking at rates mg = 0 (globals) and mℓ= 0.6 (locals,
more aggressively perturbed). Poisson resampling is omitted on the atlas-scale benchmarks (HLCA
Core, Heart v2). The global view is left unmasked so that it serves as an information-rich target for
the masked local views (Eq. 1); two global views, masked less than the local views, give similar scIB
scores (App. I).

2.3.4
Displacement auxiliary
View prediction primarily constrains the projector along directions induced by the augmentations,
leaving sensitivity to other local gene-space perturbations under-determined. We therefore optionally
add a displacement auxiliary: a small predictor estimates the projection shift ∆p = F(x′)−p induced
by a sparse additive gene-space perturbation, trained with a combined cosine-direction and magnitude
loss on a 25% batch subsample after warmup. This provides a modest boost of scIB (+0.02 Total on
atlas-scale datasets), but regularizing the Jacobian may reduce the precision of the JEPA-score as a
typicality signal; the loss form, perturbation procedure, and ablation are in App. H.

Interpretation. Balestriero et al. [2025] showed that, under isotropic Gaussian regularization, the
Jacobian log-volume of a trained JEPA approximates log p(x) at optimality, validated globally on
synthetic and image data. Single-cell data departs from the assumptions of this result (sparse counts,
masking and resampling views, adversarial batch correction), so we read S(x) as a relative rather
than a calibrated log-density. Cells also form a structured manifold of related types with very uneven
abundances: across types the score tracks each type’s abundance in the dataset, which reflects
sampling as well as biology (Section 4), and the biologically interesting signal is rarely a globally
rare cell but a coherent rare program inside a known type. We therefore read the score within a cell
type, as typicality.
Two mechanisms drive low scores onto rare-and-predictive states. SIGReg’s empirical statistic is
averaged across the batch, so a sub-population influences it only in proportion to its mixture weight;
the rare component is therefore less strongly constrained by the global Gaussian-matching objective.
If, additionally, a rare state’s cross-view predictability is carried by a compact gene program, the
prediction loss is satisfied with few features and points the same way. Rare-and-predictive states
therefore concentrate onto few dominant Jacobian directions, yielding a low log-volume score; we
confirm this empirically in Section 4, and Section 3.4 uses the score to localize disease-enriched
programs and reads them gene-by-gene through ∇xS.
Computation. The stabilized score is computed in a single vectorized pass using composable
functional transforms (vmap, jacrev, svdvals); details in App. G. For a transformer the size of
GeneJEPA (over 100M parameters), the per-cell Jacobian costs roughly 2,000× more than for our
MLP, which makes atlas-scale scoring impractical.

Baselines.
We compare LeCellModel to four standard baselines: scVI [Lopez et al., 2018], a
variational autoencoder with a negative-binomial likelihood and an explicit batch-conditional decoder;
Harmony [Korsunsky et al., 2019], an iterative batch-correction method operating in PCA space;
Scanorama [Hie et al., 2019], a mutual-nearest-neighbor alignment in reduced space; and raw PCA
as an unintegrated lower bound. We also evaluated GeneJEPA [Litman et al., 2025] (trained from
scratch and fine-tuned), scGPT [Cui et al., 2024] (zero-shot and fine-tuned) and UCE [Rosen et al.,
2026] (zero-shot). None was competitive: GeneJEPA scored below PCA, and scGPT and UCE below
Harmony; single-run results are in Appendix K.
Batch-integration metrics.
We follow the scib_metrics [scverse contributors, 2024] reimple-
mentation of scIB [Luecken et al., 2022] and report ten metrics: bio-conservation — Isolated
Labels, Leiden [Traag et al., 2019] NMI, Leiden ARI, Silhouette Label, cLISI; batch correction —
BRAS [Rautenstrauch and Ohler, 2025], iLISI, kBET, Graph Connectivity, PCR comparison. Bio

3.3
Validating rare-state detection with ground truth
The JEPA-score targets unsupervised discovery, so its validation requires a setting where a rarity label
exists but is withheld from training. The Parse Biosciences PBMC cytokine-stimulation panel [Parse
Biosciences, 2026] provides this: stimulated and resting cells are independently labeled by exposure
condition — though within the stimulated population the magnitude and character of the response
varies, and not every labeled-stimulated cell exhibits a detectable cytokine signature. We subsample
stimulated cells to fractions f ∈{0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.10} of the training mix and train without using
the label. We report AUROC and AUPRC against the held-out label, and Spearman ρ between
the score and the canonical pathway signature (STAT3 targets for IL-10; STAT6 for IL-4) within
stimulated cells only — asking whether the score also ranks cells by degree of response, not merely
stim vs. resting.

3.4
Rare program discovery
Within a cell type, low-JEPA-score cells flag rare-and-predictive states (Section 2.4). We can recover
the gene program of such a region from two complementary directions: gene →cells, by finding
genes whose expression spatially co-locates with low scores on the embedding kNN graph (bivariate
Moran’s I; App. O, Table 16), or cells →genes, by taking a low-score neighborhood and ranking
genes by their local effect on the score (∇xS; Section 2.5). The two views converge on the program
where they overlap, but ∇xS operates per-cell, surfacing score drivers rather than co-occurring
markers — a distinction no population-level correlation can make. We apply this to the HLCA
monocyte–macrophage cohort (13 studies) and the HLCA lymphoid normal+IPF cohort (19 studies);
batch correction across these contributors is what makes a shared program recoverable.
Monocyte-derived macrophages.
Within monocyte-derived macrophages, LeCellModel isolates a
low-JEPA-score subpopulation corresponding to a profibrotic macrophage program (SPP1, CHI3L1,
CHIT1, ADAMDEC1) [Morse et al., 2019], consistent with the fibrosis-associated SPP1+ state
highlighted in the HLCA disease analysis [Sikkema et al., 2023] and further extended here by matrix-
remodeling and immunosuppressive effectors (CTSK, SIGLEC15). This population is enriched for
IPF cells (31.7% vs. 12.5% baseline within monocyte-derived macrophages; 2.53-fold enrichment),
spanning 53 donors (16 IPF).
CD4 T cells.
Within CD4 T cells, LeCellModel isolates a low-JEPA-score population defined by
FOXP3, CD177, CTLA4, CCR8, and LAYN — the CD177+ activated-Treg phenotype previously
characterized in tumor microenvironments [Kim et al., 2021]. Its appearance in non-cancerous,
fibrotic IPF lung is, to our knowledge, previously unreported. LeCellModel identifies it from
embedding geometry alone: Tregs appear only in a single research group’s per-study labels (Meyer
2019/2021) — and not at all in HLCA’s harmonized vocabulary — so a label-based analysis of this
cohort would likely have missed the population entirely. The recovered program overlaps strongly
with the CD177+ tumor-infiltrating Treg state described by Kim et al. [2021], including the highly
activated and suppressive phenotype associated with recent tissue infiltration. It is also strongly
enriched for IPF cells (34.7% vs. 5.3% baseline within CD4 T cells; 6.57-fold enrichment), spanning
95 donors (17 IPF). Restricting this cluster to CD177+ cells further strengthens the disease association,
raising the IPF fraction to 56.9% (10.76-fold over the CD4 baseline), across 51 donors (13 IPF)
(Figure 4).
Gene-level attribution. Given a low-JEPA-score subpopulation C, we rank genes by the absolute
cluster-mean input×gradient, |Ex∈C[x⊙∇xS]|, where the per-cell gradient ∇xS(x) ∈RG is defined
in Section 2.5. Unlike integrated gradients on reconstruction or classification losses, which tend
toward cell-type markers, this highlights genes that shape a cell’s local typicality and rare-state
geometry — extending the JEPA-score from a per-cell anomaly detector to a gene-level interpretation
tool without any predefined gene list. In CD4 T cells, CD177 and CCR8 carry the two largest
raw-gradient magnitudes (|Ex∈C[∂S/∂x]| ≈11.0, 9.4) — the score is most locally sensitive to the
defining markers of the activated-Treg subpopulation. In monocyte-derived macrophages, the gradient
elevates matrix-remodeling and checkpoint effectors (MMP9, CTSK, SIGLEC15), while SPP1 — the
most abundant marker of the recovered program — falls outside the gradient top: the gradient tends
to surface what locally drives the score rather than general markers. The ranking recovers known
pathway targets and complements differential expression: on the CD14 cytokine pairs it places the
STAT3 and STAT6 targets F13A1 and ALOX15 first, and in IPF it ranks the program genes CD177

4
Analysis
Predictive selectivity.
At the cell-type scale, the score behaves as the global density reading of
Balestriero et al. [2025] predicts: on Heart v2, mean JEPA-score increases with cell-type abundance
(Spearman ρ = 0.86 between log10 frequency and mean score). Section 2.4 predicts a finer mecha-
nism within a cell type: under SIGReg, rare states whose cross-view predictability is carried by a
compact gene program should concentrate their representation onto fewer dominant directions than
common states do. We test this directly: for each population, we measure how well its embedding
can be approximated by the one or two best-aligned genes (best-1 and best-2 cosine similarity, cos1,
cos2).
The prediction holds. For CD177+ Tregs (FOXP3 > 1 and CD177 > 1, n = 182), LeCellModel
reaches cos1/cos2 = 0.812/0.867 against 0.367/0.477 on the broader CD4 T-cell pool (n = 500);
profibrotic monocyte-derived macrophages show the same gap (0.717/0.821 vs. 0.385/0.534). The
effect is selective to the SIGReg-induced geometry: under scVI, the rare-vs-common contrast
almost vanishes (CD177+ Treg 0.468/0.593 vs. CD4 0.359/0.473; profibrotic monocyte-derived
macrophages 0.326/0.465 vs. general monocyte-derived macrophages 0.335/0.450). The score’s
9


geometric mechanism, not just embedding quality, is what makes within-type rare-state recovery
work.
Regularizer ablation.
Replacing SIGReg with VICReg [Bardes et al., 2022], which constrains
only the first two moments, weakens both the score and the representation: the CD177+ Treg share
in the rarest 1% falls from 69% to 37%, IL-10 AUROC drops by 0.03 on average, and HLCA Core
scIB Total falls from 0.689 to 0.659 (App. I).
5
Conclusion
The key design choice in LeCellModel is that SIGReg both prevents collapse and gives the Jacobian
log-volume its meaning. The JEPA-score and its gradient therefore come directly from the trained
model rather than being added afterward, and they are interpretable because of how the model was
trained.
Toward atlas-scale reference embeddings.
A natural next step is to use LeCellModel as an atlas
reference: train once on a large multi-study cohort — potentially with a heavier encoder backbone
such as a transformer — freeze the model, and map query cells into the resulting coordinate system.
New samples would inherit an integrated embedding, a per-cell JEPA-score against a fixed reference
geometry, and gene-level ∇xS attribution, turning rare-state discovery into a query-time operation
rather than a per-dataset retraining cycle. An open question is how the score’s typicality interpretation
transfers when query cells fall outside the training manifold.
Limitations.
LeCellModel’s cross-seed variance on Total scIB is larger in the low-data regime
(Pancreas σ = 0.008) than at the atlas scale (Heart v2, HLCA Core σ ≤0.003), so per-seed rankings
on small datasets should be read with caution. The displacement-auxiliary ablation (App. H) uses
slightly different adversary and SIGReg coefficients than the with-displacement runs, selected for
stability without the auxiliary; the comparison is therefore not perfectly hyperparameter-matched.
The JEPA-score is a geometric quantity of a trained model, not a guaranteed biological readout:
it depends on training dynamics and on the augmentations the encoder was exposed to, so runs
can disagree at the margin of the rare tail, though changed cells stay near the cutoff (App. Q). We
cannot yet separate model uncertainty from genuine rarity or test the gradient against finite count
perturbations, and our negative control (App. S) confirms only known phenotypes; new states need
replication and experimental validation. Finally, ∇xS identifies the genes whose local expression
most strongly affects the score; these are interpretive markers of the rare-state geometry, not validated
biological drivers.

C
Architecture
The encoder fθ is a two-layer MLP with a trailing LayerNorm:
Linear(G →h) →LayerNorm(h) →GELU →Dropout →Linear(h →d) →LayerNorm(d),
with default d = 256, h = 512.
The projector gϕ is a two-layer MLP without a trailing norm:
Linear(d →512) →LayerNorm(512) →GELU →Dropout →Linear(512 →k),
with default k = 128.
The adversary head hψ is a two-layer MLP with a leading LayerNorm and LeakyReLU activation,
producing logits over B batches:
LayerNorm(d) →Linear(d →d) →LayerNorm(d) →LeakyReLU →Dropout(0.1) →Linear(d →B).
The conditional adversary prepends a learned cell-type embedding ec ∈R32 to the input, replacing
the first LayerNorm and Linear with LayerNorm(d + 32) →Linear(d + 32 →d). The conditional
variant is not used in any experiment reported in this paper; all results use the unconditional adversary.

Baselines.
scVI is trained with n_layers = 2, n_latent = 40, n_hidden = 256 for up to
200 epochs with early stopping. Harmony and Scanorama are run on the standard scanpy log-
normalization pipeline (104 target sum, log1p; Harmony additionally scales to unit variance at max
value 10 and takes the top-50 PCs as input); both use library defaults. PCA uses 50 components on
log-normalized, scaled expression.
Compute.
All LeCellModel training runs use a single GPU per job, drawn from a shared insti-
tutional cluster with a mixed pool: NVIDIA A40, NVIDIA A100 (40 GB and 80 GB PCIe), and
NVIDIA H100 NVL. Training and inference both use bf16 mixed precision. A full 150-epoch
training run takes roughly 1 hour for the atlas-scale benchmarks (HLCA Core, Heart v2) and roughly
3 minutes for Pancreas; exact wall-clock varies with GPU type. The reported runs represent the final
configurations; hyperparameter development required additional runs of comparable scale.

Cytokine-stimulation panel: pair details
All pairs draw from a fixed pool of 16,710 resting CD16 monocytes and 110,999 resting CD14
monocytes. We consider four cytokine–cell-type settings: IL-4 × CD14 Mono, a canonical M2-like
IL-4/STAT6 response; IL-10 × CD14 Mono, a canonical anti-inflammatory IL-10/STAT3 response;
IL-10 × CD16 Mono, an IL-10/STAT3 response in a distinct CD16+ background; and IL-4 × CD16
Mono, a weaker IL-4-response setting reported in Appendix M. Density-based baselines use scVI
with n_latent=30, 100 epochs.
Pathway signatures.
The Spearman ρ in Table 14 uses the mean z-score (z-scored on resting
cells) of an up-set minus a down-set: IL-10, up = {CD163, CD163L1, IRAK3, MARCO, MRC1,
F13A1} (STAT3 targets), down = {IL1B, IL6, CXCL10, CCL3, CCL4, CXCL8} (IL-10-suppressed
pro-inflammatory mediators); IL-4, up = {CCL17, CCL22, MRC1, ALOX15, CCL18, CHI3L1,
F13A1} (STAT6 targets), no down-set.

Rare-program discovery: statistics and cluster selection

Cluster selection for gradient attribution.
Gradient attribution requires a concrete cell cluster.
We identify connected neighborhoods of low-JEPA-score cells via a pit-finding routine; the exact
cluster boundary does not materially affect the attributed genes. The routine operates on a cosine
kNN graph (k = 10) restricted to the bottom 10% of cells by JEPA-score (the subgraph), with a
data-adaptive distance threshold δ — the 10th percentile of kNN edge distances within the subgraph
— controlling which neighbors are traversable.
1. Smooth the JEPA-score: replace each cell’s score with the mean of itself and neighbors at
distance ≤δ, one pass.
2. From seeds at the bottom 0.5% of JEPA-score (all cells), follow the lowest-scoring neighbor
at distance ≤δ until no lower neighbor exists; the landing cell is a local minimum (“pit
bottom”).
3. Group seeds by pit bottom; discard pits with fewer than max(3, 5% of seeds) converging
seeds.
4. Iteratively merge the least-prominent adjacent pit pair: the prominence of pit B is the lowest
cross-boundary score between A and B minus the score of B’s bottom (i.e. how much the
score rises to leave B); merge until all remaining pits are separated by more than 0.3 σS,
where σS is the score standard deviation within the subgraph.
5. Expand each surviving pit into a core cluster by BFS within distance δ.

Stability of JEPA-score rankings
We retrain each IPF cohort with four seeds (42, 1, 2, 3) and, at seed 42, under two further augmentation
settings (global/local mask rates 0.2/0.6 and 0.1/0.4, against 0.5/0.7 in Table 5) and with Poisson
resampling disabled. Runs score the same cells in the same order, so rankings are compared cell
by cell (Table 17). Across seeds, per-cell scores correlate at mean pairwise Spearman ρ = 0.965
(monocyte–macrophage, 257,893 cells) and 0.843 (lymphoid, 159,652 cells). About 90% of each
run’s rarest 1% is shared with any other run. The cells that differ sit just past the cutoff: their median
score percentile in the other run is 1.13–1.14%, and 99.9% (monocyte) and 99.6% (lymphoid) fall
within its rarest 2%. Within the shared tail, the ordering is preserved (Spearman 0.82–0.89), and a
consensus of three runs recovers 91–92% of the held-out run’s rare set. The rare tail is nearly as
stable across augmentation settings: of the cells whose rare-1% membership changes between mask
regimes, 90.6% (monocyte) and 94.2% (lymphoid) stay within the rarest 2%, and disabling Poisson
resampling gives the same picture.

Gradient attribution against pathway targets and differential expression
We rank genes by the absolute cluster-mean input×gradient, |Ex∈C[x ⊙∇xS]|, and compare with
the raw gradient, |Ex∈C[∇xS]|, and with Wilcoxon differential expression (Table 18). Without any
pathway list, the gradient ranks canonical STAT3 and STAT6 targets at the top on the CD14 pairs
(F13A1 and ALOX15 first), and lower on the CD16 pairs. Where the two rankings disagree, the
gradient favors genes expressed in a subset of the cluster (CD177, SIGLEC15, MARCO, CCL18),
while DE favors broadly expressed markers such as SPP1. The gradient thus captures local drivers of
the score rather than abundance differences between groups, and needs no predefined contrast group.

Negative control on healthy heart tissue
As a negative control we use healthy tissue, where no disease program is present: the subsampled
Heart Cell Atlas distributed with scvi-tools [Litviˇnuková et al., 2020]. We train with this dataset’s
own configuration: batch size 512, λsig = 0.4, adversary weight 0.8 with progress factor 2.0, one
global and four local views with mask rates 0.2/0.5, d = k = 256, no displacement auxiliary,
150 epochs. In this model, the lowest-scoring regions include an activated venous-endothelial state
(n = 46; top input×gradient genes ACKR1, SELE and VCAM1). We then remove every cell at or
below the 20th percentile of JEPA-score within its cell type (18,641 →14,908 cells) and retrain on
the remaining cells. The retrained model finds one low-score region instead of four; the remaining
ventricular-cardiomyocyte region lies 0.67 standard deviations below the mean, against 1.71 before
pruning, and the score distribution tightens (mean −818 →−747, s.d. 88 →79): homogeneous
populations receive more uniform scores.
