## Abstract 

Accurately predicting cancer drug response remains a major challenge in precision oncology. Although some therapies are guided by clinically-approved biomarkers, these markers often yield limited predictive value and are unavailable for many drugs. Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning approaches trained on large-scale cancer cell line pharmacogenomic screens offer a promising route to more comprehensive response prediction, yet they generally assume that phenotypic readouts are measured without error. This assumption is problematic because viability-based drug response assays are inherently noisy, potentially causing models to learn experimental artefacts rather than biologically meaningful determinants of sensitivity. Here, we show that phenotypic measurement noise is present in four widely used cancer cell line screening datasets and introduce Deep Concordance Index Networks (DeepCINET), a deep learning framework that explicitly accounts for this noise. Across datasets, incorporating this noise-aware ranking strategy improves generalization across experimental conditions and to previously unseen samples. DeepCINET outperforms baseline models in multiple settings, with particularly notable gains in prioritizing the most drug-sensitive cell lines. Together, these results establish phenotypic assay noise as a critical and previously overlooked source of error in drug response modelling and provide a principled framework for noise-aware multivariable biomarker discovery.

## Introduction 

Predicting which patients will respond to a given cancer therapy remains an open problem of profound clinical importance. For some therapies there exist biomarkers ,collections of (molecular) measurements that have been associated with a given treatment 

outcome and which can be used to guide treatment decisions [1] . For example, the presence 

of an EGFR mutation in non-small cell lung cancer is often predictive of response to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), and is widely used as a clinically-approved biomarkers for these 

targeted therapies [2] . While the discovery of predictive biomarkers has greatly improved the 

precision with which treatments are selected, the predictive value of existing biomarkers is often limited. For instance, patients with an EGFR mutation do not uniformly benefit from the 

TKI erlotinib  [3] . Additionally, not every drug has a biomarker that can be used to determine 

the potential for patient response  [4] .

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) methods are increasingly being 

used to predict cancer drug response  [5,6] . Conceptually, these models function as 

multivariable biomarkers that capture complex tumour biology more comprehensively than single molecular markers. Most biomarkers are developed using large in vitro cancer cell line (CCL) viability screens, where drug-dose response curves are summarized as IC50 or 

area above the dose-response curve (AAC)  [7] . However, we and others have shown that 

these phenotypic readouts are inherently noisy  [7,8] . This variability has been largely 

overlooked in prior AI/ML studies, despite the risk that models may learn experimental artefacts rather than biologically meaningful response patterns. In particular, when the measured responses of two cell lines differ only within the expected range of assay noise, their relative ordering is uncertain and may not reflect a true biological difference in sensitivity. We therefore hypothesized that explicitly accounting for assay noise during model training would improve the identification of genuine genotype-phenotype relationships. 

Here we present Deep Concordance Index NETworks (DeepCINET), an approach to explicitly account for phenotypic measurement noise during training of drug response prediction models. DeepCINET is a unique combination of two complementary techniques from the learning-to-rank literature: ranking using pairwise comparisons made with siamese 

neural networks as presented in RankNet [9]  and ignoring potentially noisy comparisons [10] .

To our knowledge, DeepCINET is the first application of those techniques to drug response prediction. We first show that this noise is present in four cancer cell line viability screens widely used to train and evaluate AI/ML models. We then demonstrate that a noise-aware version of DeepCINET frequently outperforms an otherwise identical version that does not account for measurement noise. Finally, we show that DeepCINET can outperform machine learning baseline models when evaluated on new samples. Notably, these gains are especially pronounced for identifying the most drug-sensitive cell lines, a task of particular practical relevance for biomarker discovery and treatment prioritization.

## Results 

We developed DeepCINET, a novel model for drug response prediction that accounts for phenotypic noise. Specifically, DeepCINET is implemented as a siamese neural network 

architecture  [11]  that processes pairs of cell lines simultaneously. For each pair, the 

molecular omics profiles of the two cell lines are passed through a shared neural network to 

produce sensitivity scores. Following the RankNet framework  [9] , the model is trained on

Our study uses four cell line viability screens that are commonly used in the machine

Since there is some overlap in the RNA-seq data between GDSC1 and GDSC2, the independent evaluation on the test set is about the phenotypic assay noise only . We only trained and evaluated DeepCINet on solid tissue tumour cell lines. 

Quantifying the Noise in the Drug Response Data 

To quantify the phenotypic noise in our four datasets we analyzed the distribution of 

differences in area above the dose-response curve (AAC)  [14]  measurements across 

replicates within each study. We found that the distributions are concentrated around zero with an average absolute difference between replicates around 0.05 in GDSC2, gCSI, and CTRPv2 and closer to 0.07 in GDSC1 (Table 1). These distributions also have heavy tails meaning that for the same drug-cell line pair the measured sensitivity values can be quite different (Table 1, Figure 2a 2b, Supplementary Figure 1). In fact, we see cases where the same drug-cell line combination can have an AAC measurements, which lie between 0 and 1, that differ by over 0.5 between replicates (Figure 2b, Supplementary Figure 1). This means that in one replicate a cell line may be measured as sensitive to a drug and in 

another it may be measured as resistant [16]  (Figure 1c). 

In addition to confirming the noise intrinsic to viability screens, our results corroborate 

our previous work on inconsistency between studies  [7,8] . For example, the correlation 

between AAC measurements in the training set and the test sets is modest  [17] , with the 

value lying below 0.6 in most cases (Figure 3A-C left side, vertical bars). These inconsistencies affirm the need for AI/ML models to be tested on multiple independent datasets and highlight the need for caution in developing biomarkers from a single cell line screen. 

Noise in the phenotypic measurement across poses an issue for machine learning models as it can lead to spurious conclusions about the relative sensitivities between cell lines. This problem persists even if one takes summary measurement across replicates such as the mean or median. The summarized measurements for two cell lines can be within the

## Discussion 

We have presented DeepCINET, a novel approach to training deep neural networks for drug response prediction. The core innovation of DeepCINET is the combination of techniques 

from the learning to rank literature  [9,10]  and their application to drug response prediction. To 

justify the need for DeepCINET, we have expanded on our previous work  [7,8]  and have 

shown that there is indeed substantial phenotypic measurement noise in four cell line viability screens commonly used in the machine learning literature. We then showed that a 

strictly positive  can improve generalization beyond experimental conditions. In particular, δ

for cell lines that DeepCINET encountered during training, a positive  improves the δ

performance of the model in most cases. More importantly DeepCINET is able to generalize to new samples better than strong baselines in a number of cases. 

These results show that substantial care needs to be taken when building predictive precision oncology models from cancer cell line viability screens. We have provided evidence that explicitly accounting for phenotypic measurement noise can improve model performance, supporting the need for careful benchmarking of existing supervised models as they have been fit to data with noisy labels. Given the inconsistency within studies shown 

here as well as across studies in our prior work  [7,8] , it is imperative for models to be 

evaluated across multiple CCL viability screens. Within the context of precision oncology, DeepCINET presents a meaningful advance to developing biomarkers that are robust to experimental noise and that generalize beyond a single cell line screen. Currently, DeepCINET is only an advance in pre-clinical drug modeling. We plan to investigate the utility of DeepCINET to predicting real world clinical patient data in the future. 

This study has several limitations. First, we have only tested DeepCINet on cancer cell lines which limits the immediate clinical utility of the model. We hope to evaluate DeepCINet on patient data in future work. Additionally, while we used gene expression as input for the prediction of drug response, DeepCINET can be applied to other types of omics measurements, such as mutation, copy number, ATAC-Seq, etc. It is possible that for some of the drugs analyzed in this paper the right type of molecular measurement for predicting genotype-phenotype relationships is not gene expression. Another limitation is that we only considered pancancer modelling. Building DeepCINET models that are cancer-type-specific may yield better results depending on the available sample size. This approach would also 

allow for the inclusion of cancer specific datasets such as GRAY [29]  or NCI-Sarcoma [30] .

Further, we only tested DeepCINET with a fixed architecture. It is possible that the model has either too high or insufficient capacity for some drugs and is well suited for others. Exploring the space of architectures could lead to improved performance. Lastly, there are cases where DeepCINET is outperformed by comparatively simpler models. As such DeepCINET does not represent a clear improvement over the state of the art in every case. 

Several promising directions remain for future research. A natural next step is to evaluate DeepCINet using additional molecular features, including DNA methylation, somatic mutations, and copy-number variation. All of these data modalities are known to be 

implicated in drug response [31,32] ,[33] . Given the rise in multi-modal approaches to drug 

response prediction  [34,35]  it is possible that replacing any one omic measurement type with 

a multi-modal embedding from one of many recently developed foundation models

that our results show that DeepCINET seems to provide the best performance improvement in ranking metrics, it is possible that the model as described here can be effectively applied to this domain without much modification. 

In conclusion, our findings show that explicitly accounting for phenotypic assay noise can improve the robustness of drug-response prediction. DeepCINet provides a promising framework for reducing the influence of unreliable response measurements and supporting more reliable multivariable biomarker discovery. Future work should evaluate its generalizability across additional datasets and molecular data types. 

## Materials and Methods 

Data Collection & Preprocessing 

Data for the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRPv2), Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE), Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC1 and GDSC2), and the Genentech Cell Line Screening Initiative (gCSI) were downloaded from the ORCESTA 

database  [40]  and pre-processed using the PharmacoGx R package [41] . For the AAC 

values we used the provided `aac_recomputed` field available in the downloaded objects, 

which are computed by fitting 3-parameter Hill curves to the original study data  [41] . No 

truncation of dose ranges nor harmonization of AAC values between studies was performed. 

Drugs were selected for analysis by subjecting the 19 compounds tested in all four viability datasets - CTRPv2, GDSC1, GDSC2, and gCSI - to two quality control filters. First, drugs which had fewer than 500 cell lines for training were excluded from further analysis. Drugs that had a correlation below 0.25 in AAC measurements in common cell lines between the training screen (CTRPv2) and any of the testing screens (gCSI, GDSC2, GDSC1) were also excluded from analysis. Drugs were described as ‘broad effect’ or 

‘narrow effect’ using the methods described in our prior work. [7] 

All models used gene expression measurements (RNA-seq) as input features. Input genes were selected to be those plausibly relevant to cancer biology. Specifically, a set of candidate genes was constructed by taking the union of genes in the KEGG “Pathways in 

Cancer” gene set [42] , genes in the COSMIC Gene Census [43] , as well as genes thought to 

be targets to the 14 analyzed drugs. The potential targets were downloaded from the 

Therapeutic Target Database (TTD)  [44] . The final features were those that were in the 

candidate set and also had expression measurements in every sample in each dataset. The total dimensionality of the input was 923 genes. Expression measurements were quantified 

in units of log-transformed transcripts per million (TPM). [45]  Concretely, if  is the 𝑥 𝑖𝑗

Area above the dose response curve (AAC) measurements for each drug and cell line combination were computed as the mean across replicates in each dataset. Cell lines were restricted to only those derived from solid tumours. 

DeepCINET Architecture 

The architecture used for DeepCINET was a two layer neural network with each layer having a width of 256 neurons, Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activations, and single dimensional output layer that served as the cell line score. The model was trained with drop 

out [46]  with a probability of 0.5 and batch normalization  [47] . DeepCINET was built using the 

Pytorch library  [48] .

Established Machine Learning Models 

All of the baseline ML models, including kernelized ridge regression [19] , elastic net [20] , and 

random forests  [21]  - were built using the scikit-learn python  [49] . The single variable 

predictors were computed by selecting the gene which had the highest mutual 

information [50]  between its expression level and measured AAC values. The final predictor 

was a linear regression model that used that gene’s expression value as the only covariate. This model was also built using scikit-learn. 

Model Training 

The models built with scikit-learn were fitted using the built in solvers. The hyperparameters for each of the ML models, including DeepCINET, were selected using 10-fold cross validation on the training set (Supplementary Table 4). 

Every DeepCINET configuration was trained for 30 epochs using the Adam  [51]  optimization 

procedure. In all cases the learning rate was set to 0.00001. Following RankNet [9]  the binary 

cross entropy loss was used to ensure that DeepCINET assigned higher scores to cell lines more sensitive to a given drug. In the experiments where delta was tuned we again used 10 fold cross validation.
