Background 36 

Perturb-seq couples genetic perturbations with transcriptional readouts in single cells, enabling 37 

precise measurement of how perturbations reshape cellular states. Comprehensive mapping of 38 

genetic perturbations to rich transcriptional phenotypes under diverse cellular contexts has 39 

facilitated in silico prediction of perturbation responses, in which predictive models are trained to 40 

forecast transcriptional outcomes of unseen genetic perturbations. Recent predictive 41 

approaches increasingly incorporate biological prior knowledge, including co-expression 42 

patterns and Gene Ontology graphs [1–3], to enhance generalizability. Integrating multiple 43 

priors, such as pathway annotations and pretrained gene embeddings, can further improve 44 

prediction of unseen perturbation response and cross-dataset generalization [4,5]. 45 

However, prior knowledge is not equally informative for every gene or cellular context. Perturb-46 

seq measures response in a specific cell type and experimental condition, whereas prior 47 

sources combine information from heterogeneous tissues, assays, annotations and 48 

observational datasets [5]. A gene may therefore be well characterized in existing databases but 49 

still show a context-specific perturbation response that those sources do not capture. Aggregate 50 

benchmark scores can obscure differences among individual targets. At the aggregate level, 51 

Systema showed that response components shared across perturbations can affect aggregate 52 

predictive performance [6]. 53 

Recent studies have also examined factors associated with target-level performance. GEARS 54 

examined knowledge-graph connectivity and model-derived uncertainty in relation to 55 

generalization performance [1], whereas TxPert related target knowledge and graph degree to 56 

perturbation-level performance [7]. PRESCRIBE further used target-to-training similarity and 57 

training-data quality to quantify model uncertainty and estimate confidence for unseen 58

To address these questions, we considered agreement among prior sources and train–test 68 

similarity calculated from prior knowledge. We compared these prior-derived measures with 69 

agreement between prior knowledge and the target’s observed perturbation-response 70 

relationships, and with train–test similarity calculated from those responses. Within each model 71 

and dataset, we assessed how closely each measure tracked relative differences in predictive 72 

performance across held-out targets. Because genome-wide and essential-gene screens 73 

sample different target spaces and perturbation responses depend on cellular context, we also 74 

examined how these relationships varied across target sets and cellular contexts. 75 

Accordingly, we developed a gene-similarity framework that systematically compares eight 76 

heterogeneous prior knowledge sources with six Perturb-seq datasets. We quantified how 77 

consistently each gene was represented across priors, which we termed prior agreement, and 78 

how well the priors matched two Perturb-seq-derived relationships: similarity of perturbation 79 

effects (co-function) and similarity of measured-gene responses across perturbations (co-80 

regulation). Prior agreement accounted for substantial gene-to-gene variation in co-function and 81

Results 96 

A gene-similarity framework linking prior knowledge to Perturb-seq responses and 97 

predictive performance 98 

To enable direct comparison across heterogeneous prior knowledge sources and Perturb-seq 99 

datasets, we converted each source and dataset into gene-wise similarity profiles ( Fig. 1A ). 100 

Each gene was represented by a vector of its similarity to all other genes. In Perturb-seq, 101 

similarity between perturbation effects is referred to as co-function, whereas similarity between 102 

the responses of measured genes across perturbations is termed co-regulation. 103 

Using these shared representations, we compared similarity profiles at the gene level using the 104 

local overlap of nearest neighbors and global correlation across the full profile ( Fig. 1B ). These 105 

local and global measures were used for two types of comparison. First, comparisons among 106 

priors measured how consistently each gene was represented across prior resources. We 107 

summarized this consistency as prior agreement at the gene level. Second, comparisons 108 

between each prior and Perturb-seq measured how closely prior knowledge matched co-109 

function or co-regulation relationships. These comparisons were summarized as co-function and 110 

co-regulation agreement. We then assessed the mean and standard deviation of co-function 111 

agreement across four essential-wide screens to distinguish context-stable from context-specific 112 

agreement patterns ( Methods ). 113 

Next, we assessed how much variation in co-function and co-regulation agreement was 114 

explained by prior agreement, and whether perturbation-target annotations and gene expression 115 

characteristics explained additional variation. Using linear models, we estimated the 116 

associations of prior agreement and these additional factors with co-function or co-regulation 117 

agreement across cell lines ( Fig. 1C ). 118

Similarity profiles enable direct comparison of heterogeneous prior knowledge and 129 

Perturb-seq data 130 

First, we collected eight prior knowledge sources spanning four types of biological information: 131 

graph-derived databases (MSigDB and STRINGdb), model-derived embeddings (GenePT and 132 

scGPT), CRISPR-based resources (DepMap and PERISCOPE), and genome-scale 133 

observations (regulatory networks from Marbach et al. and gene–trait associations from 134 

UnTANGLeD; Fig. 2A ) [9–14]. To compare all eight priors using the same set of genes, we 135 

defined the 10,875 genes shared across all eight sources as the reference gene set. We then 136 

asked how much of each Perturb-seq dataset remained represented in this common gene 137 

space. Individual priors covered 75–100% of perturbation-targets and 86–99% of measured 138 

mRNAs, whereas the reference gene set covered 69–83% and 77–81%, respectively (Fig. 2B ). 139 

Although requiring coverage by all eight priors reduced coverage relative to individual sources, 140 

the common gene space retained most perturbation targets and measured mRNAs across the 141 

Perturb-seq datasets. 142

The prior resources and Perturb-seq datasets contain features with different dimensions and 143 

meanings, making their raw gene profiles difficult to compare directly. We therefore represented 144 

each gene by a similarity profile describing its relationship to all other genes ( Methods ). In 145 

Perturb-seq, similarity between perturbation profiles defines co-function, whereas similarity 146 

between measured-gene responses across perturbations defines co-regulation ( Fig. 1A and 147 

2C ). 148 

A response component shared across many perturbations can increase pairwise similarity 149 

through systematic variation rather than perturbation-specific effects [6]. Consistent with this, 150 

uncentered co-function values were shifted upward in all essential-gene and TF screens and for 151 

co-regulation in the RPE1 essential-wide dataset ( Fig. 2D ). We therefore mean-centered the 152 

response profiles before calculating similarity, removing the response component shared across 153 

perturbations. The resulting co-function and co-regulation distributions using all perturbation 154 

targets or measured mRNAs were comparable to those using only genes in the reference gene 155 

set ( Supplementary Fig. 1 ). 156 

We next tested whether similarity profiles improved cross-dataset comparability in Perturb-seq, 157 

where raw and similarity representations could be benchmarked directly. For a matched 158 

analysis, we restricted the perturbation and mRNA spaces of the K562 genome-wide screen to 159 

those of the K562 essential-wide screen. Across comparisons using Pearson correlation, 160 

Spearman correlation, and cosine similarity, similarity profiles more clearly separated matched 161 

gene pairs from randomly paired genes. Accordingly, the median energy distance increased 162 

from 0.43–0.51 to 0.61–0.65 in the perturbation space and from 0.44–0.50 to 0.63–0.79 in the 163 

mRNA space ( Fig. 2E ). These results show that similarity profiles improve cross-dataset 164 

discrimination of corresponding genes on both axes of the matched Perturb-seq response 165 

matrices, supporting their use in the following prior–prior and prior–Perturb-seq comparisons. 166

Gene-level agreement reveals shared and distinct structure across prior knowledge 167 

sources 168 

To assess how consistently each gene was represented across prior sources, we quantified 169 

both local and global agreement between its similarity profiles. Local agreement measured 170 

whether two sources identified the same closely related genes, whereas global agreement 171 

measured concordance across all gene relationships. Both measures were standardized 172 

against randomly paired genes to obtain Z-scores (Methods; Supplementary Fig. 2A, B ). 173 

Local and global Z-score distributions were shifted above zero, indicating greater agreement 174 

between priors than expected by chance ( Fig. 3A ). The two scores were moderately correlated 175 

(Pearson's R = 0.39; Spearman's ρ = 0.33; Supplementary Fig. 2C ). To summarize local and 176 

global agreement in a single score, we averaged them to define the gene agreement score 177 

(GAS), a combined measure of gene-level agreement for a given pair of similarity matrices. 178 

For each prior pair, we next calculated the fraction of genes with significant local or global 179 

agreement above the threshold (Z-score > 2.5; Fig. 3B ). Five sources—scGPT, GenePT, 180 

MSigDB, STRINGdb, and DepMap—showed consistently higher mutual agreement. The 181 

STRINGdb–GenePT pair showed local agreement above this threshold for 67% of genes. We 182 

refer to these five sources as the functional consensus group in subsequent analyses. 183 

PERISCOPE, UnTANGLeD, and Marbach et al. were less concordant with the gene 184 

relationships shared by the five-prior group. This difference may reflect the distinct biological 185 

relationships and assays represented by these sources. 186

A priori assessment of target-level predictability in prior-informed predictive models 310 

We then asked whether two prior-derived measures—(1) prior agreement and (2) train–test 311 

similarity—calculated without the observed test response were associated with predictive 312 

performance for individual held-out targets. We compared their forecastability with that of 313 

corresponding response-informed measures. Using a benchmark of three prior-informed 314 

models, GEARS, scLAMBDA and PRESAGE [1,5,31], on held-out perturbations [32], we 315 

quantified predictive performance for each test perturbation. To distinguish how well a metric 316 

tracks target-level performance from the model’s predictive accuracy, we defined forecastability 317 

as the Pearson correlation across test perturbations between a predefined predictability metric 318 

and observed predictive performance ( Fig. 5A ). Thus, a high positive forecastability value 319 

indicates that perturbations with higher metric values tend to have higher predictive 320 

performance. We compared forecastability across predictive models, perturbation-target sets, 321 

and cellular contexts. 322

perturbation responses ( Fig. 5B ). This yielded a two-by-two comparison of the relationship 325 

measured ( A or S) and the information used (prior-derived or response-informed). Agreement-326

Discussion 400 

Prior-informed models such as GEARS, PRESAGE, and TxPert [1,5,7] incorporate biological 401 

knowledge to predict responses to unseen perturbations. Recent benchmarks have shown that 402 

systematic variation can affect aggregate predictive performance [6]. Comparisons with simple 403 

baselines further show that conclusions about model performance depend on the evaluation 404 

task and metric [22, 32]. In addition, differences between training and test perturbation sets can 405 

also influence overall benchmark performance [33]. However, aggregate scores can mask 406 

variation in predictive performance across individual perturbations. To address these gaps, we 407 

introduced a gene-level framework linking agreement among priors, agreement with observed 408 

perturbation-response relationships, and perturbation-specific predictability. Prior agreement 409 

and prior-derived train–test similarity, calculated without the test response, were informative 410 

about relative predictive performance across held-out targets, with associations varying across 411 

models and datasets. 412 

Prior–response agreement was biological program-specific and differed between co-function 413 

and co-regulation. Agreement was concentrated in specific biological programs, with translation 414 

and rRNA maturation represented in both co-function and co-regulation comparisons and 415 

transcription initiation and the mediator complex specific to co-function comparisons. scGPT 416 

showed the strongest co-regulation agreement, whereas GenePT, MSigDB, STRINGdb, and 417 

DepMap more closely matched co-function. 418 

At the gene level, we next examined gene-to-gene variation in co-function and co-regulation 419 

agreement. Prior agreement accounted for a substantial component of this variation, with 420 

additional variation associated with perturbation-target annotations and gene expression 421 

characteristics. Spliceosome genes illustrated that co-function agreement can differ across 422

therefore captured a substantial but incomplete component of gene-level prior–response 424

Forecastability complements aggregate predictive performance by assessing whether gene-427 

level measures distinguish relatively easier and harder perturbations to predict. For agreement-428 

based metrics, co-function agreement showed higher forecastability than prior agreement 429 

across the three models ( Fig. 5C, D ; Supplementary Fig. 5A ). Notably, prior agreement was 430 

positively correlated with target-level predictive performance in scLAMBDA and PRESAGE 431 

(Pearson's R = 0.268–0.358 and 0.297–0.408, respectively). For train–test similarity, the metric 432 

based on observed perturbation responses generally showed higher forecastability than prior-433 

derived metrics, but some metrics derived from individual priors showed comparable or higher 434 

forecastability in specific model–dataset settings. Thus, response-informed agreement metrics 435 

consistently showed higher forecastability than their prior-derived counterparts, whereas this 436 

advantage was less consistent for train–test similarity. 437 

These differences suggest that prior-derived measures are not equally informative across 438 

predictive models. In PRESAGE, averaging train–test similarity across the functional consensus 439 

priors generally showed higher forecastability than metrics derived from individual priors. By 440 

contrast, specific priors were more informative in the other models: GenePT-derived similarity 441 

showed higher forecastability than the five-prior average in four of five datasets in scLAMBDA, 442 

whereas scGPT-derived similarity showed relatively high forecastability in GEARS ( Fig. 5F, G ;443 

Supplementary Fig. 5C ). The informativeness of prior-derived train–test similarity is therefore 444 

model-dependent rather than a fixed property of the prior itself, and averaging across priors 445 

does not necessarily yield the most informative train–test similarity measure for every model. 446

Taken together, these model-dependent associations suggest that prior-derived metrics may 447 

help distinguish easier and harder targets to predict within a given model and dataset. Because 448 

they do not require the test response, these metrics can be calculated before it is measured. 449 

The extended PRESAGE analyses in HEK293 and HCT116 further showed higher 450 

forecastability when both training and test perturbations were restricted to essential genes, even 451 

after matching the number of target perturbation. 452 

Because these metrics can be calculated without the test response, their associations with 453 

predictive performance can inform relative confidence in target predictions and help prioritize 454 

perturbations for experimental characterization. The fixed-target training-set analysis further 455 

extends their use to selecting training perturbations. High-similarity training sets yielded better 456 

predictions than low-similarity sets with matched distributions of Aprior (Supplementary Fig. 6 ). 457 

This study has several limitations. First, both families of metrics were associated with predictive 458 

performance, yet substantial variation remained, suggesting that other factors, such as 459 

sequencing depth or cell numbers per perturbation, may also influence predictive performance. 460 

Differences across screens also cannot be attributed to cellular context alone because target 461 

composition and experimental protocols differ. Second, prior-derived estimates may be 462 

constrained by the incomplete coverage and context specificity of current prior knowledge. 463 

Requiring coverage by all eight priors may favor well-characterized genes, while shared 464 

information and reuse of the same prior resources limit the independence of the agreement 465 

measures. Finally, our analyses were restricted to perturbation responses measured in the 466 

mRNA expression space. Whether similar agreement patterns and prior-derived associations 467 

with predictive performance extend to protein-level, multi-omic, or imaging-based phenotypes 468 

remains to be established [34–37]. 469

Conclusions 470 

Our analyses show that the usefulness of prior knowledge depends on both the biological 471 

relationship of interest and the model in which the prior is used, and that these patterns vary 472 

across cellular contexts and perturbation targets. Agreement among priors explained part of the 473 

gene-to-gene variation in how well prior knowledge matched observed perturbation responses. 474 

Together with prior-derived train–test similarity, it also provided information about relative 475 

predictive performance across held-out targets before their responses were observed. These 476 

metrics could inform experimental prioritization strategies, including active-learning frameworks 477 

[38], by highlighting perturbations for which existing information may provide limited support for 478 

prediction. Large-scale Perturb-seq datasets spanning broader target sets and cellular contexts 479 

could help evaluate the generalizability of prior-derived measures of perturbation predictability. 480 

Prospective studies can test whether experimentally characterizing such perturbations improves 481 

subsequent prediction of unseen perturbations. 482

Prior knowledge sources 484 

Eight knowledge sources were assembled as comparative priors: model-derived embeddings 485 

(GenePT and scGPT) [11,12]; graph-like resources (MSigDB embeddings from medium-486 

confidence edges and STRINGdb embeddings) [9,10]; resources derived from observational 487 

studies (Marbach et al. and the UnTANGLeD gene–trait association matrix) [19,39]; and 488 

CRISPR knockout (KO) datasets (PERISCOPE and DepMap) [13,14]. For the GenePT 489 

embedding, graph-like resources, and DepMap dataset, we used preprocessed matrices 490 

generated by the PRESAGE study as gene-feature embeddings [5]. GenePT embeddings were 491 

corrected using the mean embedding across all genes in the reference gene set because 492 

random gene pairs showed high basal correlation. Source-specific preprocessing was 493 

performed for resources not directly adopted from PRESAGE. scGPT gene embeddings were 494 

extracted from the pretrained scGPT blood model by encoding gene-vocabulary IDs; the 495 

resulting gene-by-embedding matrix was used. We loaded the gene-feature matrix measured by 496 

PERISCOPE in A549 cells and converted the Marbach et al. regulatory network from chronic 497 

myelogenous leukemia (CML) cells into a gene-by-TF weight matrix, setting missing edges to 498 

zero. For UnTANGLeD gene–trait association data, we retrieved the MultiXcan gene–trait 499 

association matrix and mapped Ensembl gene identifiers to gene symbols. The resulting 500
