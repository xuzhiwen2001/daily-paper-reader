Abstract 

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) measures the expression of hundreds to thousands of genes within tissue architecture, often accompanied by histology images. However, it remains challenging to systematically characterize which genes exhibit associations with tissue morphology, whether these associations vary spatially, and which genes show additional spatial expression patterns beyond those captured by morphology. Here we introduce MorphoANOVA, a gene-wise statistical framework for characterizing morphology– expression relationships across genes measured by ST. Using nested spatially varying coefficient models and sequential ANOVA, MorphoANOVA assesses slide-wide associations with an H&E-derived morphology feature, location-specific changes in these associations, and additional spatial variation after accounting for the selected feature. The resulting statistics enable gene prioritization and pathway enrichment analysis, while spatial component maps support the interpretation of individual genes. Simulations demonstrate MorphoANOVA’s ability to distinguish the modeled components and recover their spatial patterns. Applications to human cortex and breast cancer identify biological programs associated with different morphology–expression patterns and reveal distinct relationships with cell composition. Across three HER2-positive breast cancer patients, invasive-cancer marker genes consistently exhibit stronger evidence of location-specific morphology associations. MorphoANOVA thus provides a framework for systematically interpreting spatial gene-expression patterns in relation to tissue morphology.

# Introduction 

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies enable the measurement of hundreds to tens of thousands of genes while preserving their spatial locations within tissue architecture, providing insights into spatial cellular organization and function. Advances in spatial resolution have expanded ST from multi-cell capture spots, as in early spatial barcoding approaches [1], to single-cell resolution with methods such as Slide-seq [2] and HDST [3], and even to subcellular levels, as achieved by high-resolution in situ hybridization methods like MERFISH [4], seqFISH [5], and Xenium [6]. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained histology images, routinely generated in clinical and research workflows, provide complementary structural and morphological information and have also been shown to be associated with spatial gene expression patterns [7]. These observations suggest that tissue morphology may offer important clues to spatial molecular organization. Morphology–expression relationships may differ across genes and tissue regions. Even within the same cell type, transcriptional programs can vary across tissue regions because of differences in microenvironmental signals and cellular states [8], as well as tissue-specific regulation [9, 10]. Consequently, histologically similar regions may exhibit distinct expression patterns, while the association between a morphology feature and a gene’s expression may be strong in some regions but weak or absent in others. These observations motivate a systematic approach to determining, for each gene, whether its expression is associated with tissue morphology, whether this association varies spatially, and whether additional spatial expression patterns remain after accounting for the selected morphology feature. Numerous computational methods have been developed to jointly analyze ST and histological images, broadly focusing on expression prediction or multimodal integration [11]. Prediction-based approaches aim to predict gene expression from histology, either in unmeasured regions or at higher spatial resolution. Early methods such as ST-Net [12] use convolutional neural networks, while Hist2ST [13] combines image features with spatial context for expression prediction. Subsequent studies include BLEEP [14], which uses contrastive bimodal embeddings for prediction, and iSTAR [15], which uses a self-supervised vision Transformer to extract hierarchical histology features and predict gene expression at higher spatial resolution. Integration-based approaches combine ST and histology, often to improve spatial domain identification. SpaCell [16] and stLearn [17] both use pretrained ResNet50 [18] to extract histology features. SpaCell embeds these jointly with gene expression, whereas stLearn combines them with spatial neighborhood information to adjust expression values. SpaGCN [19] integrates ST and histology through graph convolutional networks to identify spatial domains. Other integration methods, including conST [20] and ConGI [21], use contrastive learning to integrate ST and histology for spatial clustering. Despite these advances, existing methods provide limited tools for systematically characterizing how morphology– expression relationships vary across tissue and across genes. Early studies, such as those by Badea et al. [22] and ImageCCA [7], associated histological image features with gene expression but operated mainly at the whole-slide or tissue-sample level. More recent methods, such as SPADE [23] and MorphLink [24], identify morphology-associated genes, with MorphLink additionally quantifying local morphology–expression concordance within tissue subregions. However, these methods do not provide a unified gene-wise framework for separately assessing slide-wide morphology associations, location-specific changes in these associations, and additional spatial expression patterns after accounting for the selected morphology feature. Such a framework would enable researchers to compare morphology–expression relationships across genes and identify where distinct spatial patterns occur within tissue. Here we introduce MorphoANOVA, a statistical framework for systematically characterizing morphology–expression relationships across genes measured by spatial transcriptomics. For each gene, MorphoANOVA uses sequential analysis of variance (ANOVA) of nested spatially varying coefficient models (SVCMs) to assess three questions: whether gene expression is associated with a selected H&E-derived morphology feature across the tissue, whether this association varies by location, and whether additional spatial expression patterns remain after accounting for the selected feature. MorphoANOVA assesses the statistical evidence for these patterns, summarizes the corresponding sequential improve-ments in adjusted R2, and maps their spatial distributions. These results support two complementary applications: prioritizing genes and biological pathways with distinct morphology–expression relationships, and identifying tissue regions where morphology-associated and additional spatial expression patterns occur. Applications to human cortex and breast cancer demonstrate how these patterns can relate differently to cell composition and tumor states. 

# Results 

Method overview 

MorphoANOVA systematically characterizes morphology–expression relationships across genes measured by spatial transcriptomics. As shown in Fig. 1a, the framework takes paired H&E histology and spatial gene-expression measure-ments as input and extracts a morphology feature at each spatial location. For each gene, MorphoANOVA fits four SVCMs (Fig. 1b). Starting from an intercept-only model, the sequence adds a slide-wide H&E effect, a location-specific H&E effect capturing spatial changes in the association, and additional spatial variation after accounting for the selected H&E feature. We next clustered spots based on the fitted total H&E effect and spatial variation beyond H&E for the F32 - and 

F43 -based top-200 gene sets, respectively, to examine heterogeneity within anatomical compartments (cortical layers L1–L6 and white matter) and cortical layer organization (Fig. 3a,g). Within annotated white matter, spots assigned to H2, H3, and H5 had mean estimated oligodendrocyte proportions of 52.0%, 62.6%, and 67.5%, respectively. Distinguishing morphogenomic components of gene expression at cellular resolution 

We next applied MorphoANOVA to a Xenium breast cancer dataset to examine morphology–expression relationships at single-cell resolution. The dataset comprises measurements of 313 genes across 167,780 segmented cells, together with a high-resolution H&E image [6]. To obtain a data-driven representation of tissue morphology, we extracted hierarchical histology features using iSTAR [15] and used their first principal component as the morphology covariate (Fig. 4a). We then examined which genes were associated with this learned feature, how these associations varied spatially, and which additional spatial expression patterns remained after accounting for it. We first prioritized genes with strong location-specific H&E effects or spatial variation beyond H&E. For each gene, we summarized the corresponding model-comparison statistics F32 and F43 (Fig. 4b). After requiring a sequential increase in adjusted R2 of at least 0.03 for each component, we ranked genes by F32 and F43 , respectively, and selected the top 50 from each ranking for functional enrichment analysis (top six shown in Supplementary Fig. 4a). GO enrichment identified supramolecular fiber organization and neuron projection development in both gene lists, with tissue development and axon development among the leading terms in the F32 - and F43 -based lists, respectively. KEGG enrichment identified Cadherin signaling and Cornified envelope formation in both lists, with Proteoglycans in cancer additionally enriched among genes with strong location-specific H&E effects (Supplementary Fig. 4b). We next examined CEACAM6 , which ranked first in both gene lists, to illustrate how MorphoANOVA characterizes an individual gene’s spatial expression pattern. The full model achieved an adjusted R2 of 55.9%, with sequential increases of 4.7, 36.2, and 15.0 percentage points upon adding the slide-wide H&E effect, location-specific H&E effect, and spatial variation beyond H&E, respectively. The fitted components showed distinct spatial patterns (Fig. 4c), with regions of statistical support shown in Fig. 4d. To examine their relationships with tissue composition, we calculated Spearman correlations between the fitted spatial components and estimated cell-type proportions across spatial bins (Fig. 4e). The total H&E effect was more strongly correlated with the proportion of cells annotated as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) category 2 ( ρ = 0 .44 ) than spatial variation beyond H&E ( ρ = 0 .08 ). Conversely, spatial variation beyond H&E showed a stronger absolute correlation with invasive-tumour proportions ( ρ = −0.52 ) than the total H&E effect ( ρ = 0 .28 ). To characterize the spatial relationship between stromal-associated genes and the selected H&E feature, we focused on four genes involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization (outlined in Fig. 4f). Within each spatial bin, we averaged the fitted total H&E effect and spatial variation beyond H&E across the four genes. Both components were positively correlated with stromal-cell proportions (Spearman ρ = 0 .393 and 0.431 , respectively) but showed distinct spatial patterns (Fig. 4g). In stromal-rich bins, the H&E-associated contribution was higher near the tumour, whereas spatial variation beyond H&E was higher in more distant stroma. These results show how MorphoANOVA distinguishes morphology-guided patterns from additional spatial variation beyond morphology. 

MorphoANOVA reveals shared and patient-specific morphology–expression patterns in breast cancer 

We next applied MorphoANOVA to HER2 + breast cancer data from the HER2ST study [28] to examine whether morphology–expression relationships were consistent across patients. We analyzed sections A1, G2, and H1 from three patients. These sections cover all tumor subtypes in the original cohort and were previously analyzed by MorphLink [24]. We used a mean-centered H&E intensity feature extracted from approximately 100 μm-wide image patches at each spot as the morphology covariate (Fig. 5a). Through differential expression analysis, we identified 25 invasive-cancer marker genes shared across the three sections and compared their morphology–expression relationships. The shared invasive-cancer markers had higher median F32 values than the remaining genes in all three sections, indicating stronger evidence for location-specific H&E effects, whereas differences in F43 were less consistent (Fig. 5b). We further examined correlations between the fitted spatial components and estimated cell-type proportions across spots. Both the total H&E effect and spatial variation beyond H&E were predominantly positively correlated with epithelial-cell proportions in all three sections. In contrast, correlations with cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) proportions varied across sections. They were predominantly negative for both components in A1 and generally weak in H1. In G2, correlations with CAF proportions were weak for the total H&E effect but positive for spatial variation beyond H&E (Fig. 5c). Results for six additional cell types are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. We selected CD24 to illustrate these patterns (Fig. 5d). Both components were positively correlated with epithelial-cell proportions in all three sections, but their relationships with CAF proportions differed. In G2, spatial variation beyond H&E was positively correlated with CAF proportions ( ρ = 0 .284 ), whereas the total H&E effect showed little correlation ( ρ = −0.056 ). Both components were negatively correlated with CAF proportions in A1, with negligible correlations in H1. These results show that shared invasive-cancer markers exhibit consistently stronger evidence for location-specific H&E effects across patients, while their fitted spatial components show patient-specific relationships with tissue cell composition. 

# Discussion 

We developed MorphoANOVA, a framework for characterizing morphology–expression relationships in ST. Using nested SVCMs and sequential ANOVA, it assesses slide-wide H&E effects, location-specific changes, and additional spatial variation beyond morphology. Simulations showed accurate recovery of these patterns with near-nominal type-I error rates. Applications to human cortex and breast cancer revealed distinct biological programs and cell-type associations, while shared invasive-cancer markers showed stronger location-specific H&E effects but patient-specific relationships with cell composition. MorphoANOVA complements existing approaches for jointly analyzing histology and spatial transcriptomics. Whereas prediction-based methods estimate gene expression from histology and integration-based methods combine the two modalities for tasks such as spatial clustering, MorphoANOVA focuses on systematically characterizing morphology–expression relationships across genes. Unlike morphology-linking approaches such as MorphLink, which quantifies local morphology–expression concordance within tissue subregions, MorphoANOVA jointly assesses slide-wide associations, location-specific changes in these associations, and additional spatial expression patterns after accounting for morphology. These analyses help researchers prioritize genes with distinct morphology–expression relationships and identify where these patterns occur within tissue. MorphoANOVA can also be applied to different types of H&E-derived morphology features. We demonstrated this flexibility using image-intensity features in human cortex and HER2 + breast cancer, and a learned iSTAR-derived histology feature in Xenium breast cancer. In particular, MorphoANOVA provides a way to interpret learned histology features by identifying genes and tissue regions associated with these features, as well as additional spatial expression patterns that they do not capture. This capability complements existing image-feature extraction methods by providing gene-level and spatial interpretation of their relationships with gene expression. There are several limitations to our study. First, MorphoANOVA analyzes one morphology feature at a time, whereas 7

Sequential ANOVA and incremental adjusted R2

We used sequential analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess statistical evidence for each additional model com-ponent. For each comparison, let RSS r and RSS f denote the residual sums of squares of the reduced and full models, respectively, and let edf r and edf f denote their effective degrees of freedom. The F-type statistic is 

F = (RSS r −RSS f )/(edf f −edf r )RSS f /(n−edf f ) . Because the spatial terms were estimated using penalized smooths, model com-plexity was represented by effective rather than nominal degrees of freedom, yielding approximate F-type tests whose empirical calibration was evaluated through simulations. For each gene, the three model comparisons address distinct hypotheses. F21 (M2 vs. M1) tests H0 : β1 = 0 ,corresponding to no slide-wide H&E effect. F32 (M3 vs. M2) tests H0 : β1(z) ≡ 0, corresponding to no additional location-specific H&E effect. F43 (M4 vs. M3) tests H0 : β0(z) ≡ β0, corresponding to no additional spatial variation beyond the total H&E effect. Among the three model-comparison statistics, F32 and F43 directly characterize spatial heterogeneity. Therefore, in the gene prioritization and pathway enrichment analysis, we focused on F32 and F43 .We summarized the sequential improvements in model fit using adjusted R2. For model Mk, adjusted R2 was calculated as R2(M k) = 1 − RSS k /(n−edf k )Pni=1 (yi−¯y)2/(n−1) , where ¯y is the mean expression across locations and R2(M1) = 0 .We defined ∆R221 = R2(M2) , ∆R232 = R2(M3) − R2(M2) , and ∆R243 = R2(M4) − R2(M3) . These increments correspond to adding the slide-wide H&E effect, location-specific H&E effect, and additional spatial variation, respectively. They depend on the specified model order and do not represent an order-invariant partition of gene-expression variance. We defined the remaining adjusted-R2 complement as R2residual = 1 − R2(M4) .

Gene prioritization and pathway enrichment 

To prioritize genes with strong location-specific H&E effects, we retained those with ∆R232 ≥ 0.03 and ranked them by 

F32 . Similarly, to prioritize genes with strong spatial variation beyond H&E, we retained those with ∆R243 ≥ 0.03 and ranked them by F43 . We selected the top N genes from each ranking, where N was specified for each dataset. The two gene lists could overlap because a gene may exhibit both patterns. We performed GO Biological Process and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses separately for the two lists, with enrichment p-values adjusted using the BH procedure.

Real-data analyses 

Human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex dataset. We used log-transformed expression and retained 8,919 genes with a standard deviation greater than 0.2 across spots. The morphology covariate was the mean green-channel intensity within 7 × 7-pixel H&E patches centered at each Visium spot and mean-centered across the section. After applying the threshold of ∆R2 ≥ 0.03 , we selected the top 200 genes ranked by F32 and F43 , respectively. GO Biological Process and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses used clusterProfiler [30]. We obtained precomputed proportions of nine cell types from Spacelink [31] and calculated Spearman correlations with the fitted total H&E effect or spatial variation beyond H&E for the 102 genes shared by the two top-200 lists. For component-based clustering, we applied 

k-means with k = 7 to the corresponding fitted components for the two top-200 gene sets. 

Xenium breast cancer. We used segmented cell centers and log-transformed expression for model fitting. We extracted hierarchical histology features using iSTAR [15] and used the first principal component (PC1) as the morphology covariate. After the same ranking procedure, we selected the top 50 genes from each ranking for GO Biological Process and KEGG pathway enrichment. Using the provided cell annotations, we grouped cells into 100 × 100 μm bins containing at least ten cells and calculated cell-type proportions. We averaged the fitted total H&E effect and spatial variation beyond H&E within each bin and calculated Spearman correlations with cell-type proportions for the 36 genes shared by the two top-50 lists. For the four ECM genes, stromal-rich bins contained ≥ 50% stromal cells, while tumour-rich bins contained ≥ 50% DCIS or invasive-tumour cells. We calculated distances from stromal-rich bins to the nearest tumour-rich bin and averaged component scores within [0 , 200] , (200 , 300] , (300 , 500] , and > 500 μm.

HER2-positive breast cancer. We analyzed sections A1, G2, and H1 separately, retaining genes detected in at least 10% of spots. Counts were log-transformed, and the morphology covariate was the mean RGB intensity within approximately 100 μm-wide H&E patches centered at each spot and mean-centered within each section. We identified invasive-cancer markers using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests comparing invasive-cancer with other annotated regions, retaining genes with positive z-statistics, higher mean expression, and BH-adjusted p < 0.05 in each section, yielding 25 shared markers. We then calculated Spearman correlations between proportions of eight major cell types from HER2ST [28] and the fitted total H&E effect or spatial variation beyond H&E for these markers.
