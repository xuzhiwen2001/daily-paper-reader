Cells decode external inputs into intracellular activities of varying magnitude, and inferring both 
input identity and strength is crucial for cell-fate engineering. However, perturbation-response 
models typically treat all cells exposed to a given input as a discrete class, ignoring cell-to-cell 
variation in response magnitude. Here, we introduce a structured-label framework that infers 
both input identity and strength from combinatorial perturbation screens without dose-resolved 
training labels. We represent each perturbation combination in a binary combinatorial space 
and replace one-hot supervision with a Boltzmann distribution that decays with the Hamming 
distance between combinations. This approach preserves classification accuracy while 
improving calibration and resolving continuous pathway activities across individual cells. 
Importantly, intermediate scores primarily reflect ambiguity in cellular response states rather 
than insufficient model knowledge. Applied to engineered and natural tissues, the calibrated 
model successfully recovered graded activities. These results establish combinatorial 
perturbation screens as a self-calibrating framework for decoding graded cellular responses. 
   
INTRODUCTION  
While external inputs drive cell fate changes and 
physiological adaptation, the exact choice a cell makes 
depends not only on the identity of the input, but also 
the magnitude of the response within the cell 1–4 . The 
classic example is tissue patterning in developing 
embryos, where spatially graded ligand concentrations 
instruct the formation of adjacent cell-type domains that 
are distinct from one another 1. However, response 
magnitudes not only depend on the input concentration, 
but also the internal cellular state. Different cell types 
respond to the same input with different strengths. 
Similarly, signaling pathways often act in parallel to alter 
the internal state of the cell, and thus the response 
magnitude to one pathway is affected by the presence 
of other pathways 
5–8. Furthermore, even clonal 
populations of cells exposed to identical inputs can 
respond with different magnitudes due to noise, leading 
to distinct fate choices 9–11. Therefore, resolving the 
magnitude of signaling response, by taking into 
consideration of both the external input and internal cell 
state, is essential for understanding how cells make 
decisions and for engineering cell fates towards 
therapeutic intervention and regeneration medicine12. 
Many machine learning models trained on perturbation 
data do not explicitly model perturbation magnitude. 
Instead, they frame perturbation prediction as a binary 
classification task: determining whether a perturbation 
is present or absent 13–18. This formulation is inherited 
from cell-type classification objectives, which are 
intrinsically binary 19–21. Consequently, it masks the cell-
to-cell variation in response magnitude within the 
training data. These models therefore have limited 
capability to recover the response magnitude in target 
cell types, which can vary substantially with biological 
context. They may also perform poorly when a 
response magnitude lies in an intermediate state 
between the nominal perturbed and unperturbed 
classes 22. Directly training such models to infer

Combinatorial structure of perturbation screens 
provides an internal scale for graded signaling 
inference 
We sought to leverage the combinatorial structure of 
perturbation screens to infer the relative activation 
strength of defined signaling pathways from single-cell 
transcriptional states (Fig. 1a, left).  When a pathway 
is activated alone and in several co-stimulation 
conditions, its nominal label remains "on" in every 
condition. 
However, 
interactions 
with 
co-active 
pathways can alter both the magnitude and composition 
of its transcriptional response. Across cells and 
perturbation 
combinations, 
the 
screen 
therefore 
samples a distribution of response magnitudes for each 
pathway. We hypothesized that this combinatorial 
structure could provide an internal calibration scale for 
converting binary perturbation labels into graded 
pathway activity scores.

To determine whether combinatorial labels have a 
defined geometry that reflects transcriptional response 
similarity, 
we 
analyzed 
combinatorial 
signaling 
perturbation screens in mouse and human pluripotent 
stem cells 13,25 . Stem cells were treated with diverse, 
randomly selected ligand combinations, and the 
transcriptional states of the resulting differentiated cells 
were profiled by single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-
seq). Importantly, each ligand was applied at a single 
fixed concentration across all combinations. To encode 
this combinatorial label structure, we represented each 
combination of the six pathways (BMP, FGF, RA, SHH, 
TGF-β, and WNT) as a six-bit vector, and the geometric 
distance between two combinations as the Hamming 
distance, which is the minimum number of bit flips 
required to interchange two binary sequences (Fig. 1a, 
middle). For pairs of cells, we compared the distance 
between their combinatorial signaling labels with the 
distance between their transcriptomic profiles. Cells 
exposed to more similar signaling combinations also 
exhibited more similar transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 1b). 
To confirm that this relationship was not confounded by 
the high dimensionality of the data, we independently 
evaluated it using shared-nearest-neighbor connectivity 
and observed the same association (Supplementary 
Fig. 1) 26. Together, these analyses support the idea 
that the combinatorial perturbation structure encodes 
information about the relative strength of pathway 
activation.  
Incorporating combinatorial geometry in model 
training 
We reasoned that a model that preserves geometric 
relationships among perturbation combinations could 
use these response states to learn graded pathway 
scores, even when the experimental labels remain 
binary (Fig. 1a, right). Existing label-classification 
approaches generally fall into two categories. Standard 
binary classifiers treat each pathway as independent 
and derive a combinatorial prediction by concatenating 
individual 
pathway-specific 
output, 
whereas 
conventional multiclass classifiers treat each pathway 
combination as a separate category 
27. Neither 
approach 
leverages 
the 
inherent 
combinatorial 
geometry of the data to represent the relationships 
among nearby combinations in label space. By 
encoding this geometry during training, a model should 
be 
encouraged 
to 
distribute 
probability 
across 
biologically neighboring combinations and, after 
marginalization, produce graded scores for individual 
pathways.  
To incorporate the geometric relationships among 
perturbation combinations, we changed how model is 
taught what constitutes a correct prediction. In 
conventional model training, each cell is assigned a 
one-hot target, and the model is trained with standard 
cross-entropy 
loss 
that 
treats 
all 
alternative 
combinations equally incorrect. In contrast, we 
proposed a structured approach using Boltzmann-
smoothed cross-entropy loss, which assigns the 
highest probability to the observed combination but 
gives progressively lower probability to combinations 
that are farther away in Hamming distance (Fig. 1c).

replaced the one-hot target with a soft target distribution

We implemented this objective in a shared scVI/scANVI

derived

activity

by 
marginalizing the predicted probability over all 
combinations that contained the pathway of interest. 
This common readout enabled direct comparison of 
categorical performance, probability calibration, and the 
prevalence of graded pathway scores across models.

Furthermore, we asked whether these gains could be 
achieved by generic label smoothing alone. We applied 
conventional label smoothing to the Binary model by 
decreasing the target probability by a fixed value (ε) and 
relocating that probability to the alternative class during 
cross-entropy training. We found that this improved 
calibration over a broad range of smoothing strengths 
without materially reducing exact-match accuracy, 
indicating 
that 
the 
original 
binary 
model 
was 
overconfident (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, 
CIRCE further improved calibration by distributing 
target probability according to the known geometry of 
the combinatorial perturbation space, rather than 
treating all alternative labels as equally plausible. Thus, 
CIRCE prevents over confidence and learns a more 
graded representation of probabilities.  
Finally, we examined how model performance varied 
with the number of cells sampled per signaling 
combination. Predictive performance plateaued at 
approximately 80 cells per combination, suggesting that, 
beyond this point, expanding coverage of distinct 
combinations is more valuable than collecting additional 
cells from already sampled condition (Supplementary 
Fig. 4). 
Intermediate pathway scores predominantly reflect 
variation in cellular responses 
Next, we asked whether intermediate pathway scores 
reflect genuine variation in cellular response magnitude. 
An intermediate prediction can arise for two distinct 
reasons. First, it may reflect epistemic uncertainty, 
because the model has not observed sufficiently similar 
transcriptional states during training and therefore lacks 
confidence in its prediction. Alternatively, it may reflect 
aleatoric ambiguity, because the cell occupies a 
response state that does not fall definitively into an 
individual perturbation class (Fig. 3a). The latter 
possibility is consistent with the biological premise of 
our model, in which hard treatment labels may conceal 
a 
continuous 
distribution 
of 
cellular 
response 
magnitudes to the same nominal perturbation. 
We used a Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement 
(BALD) approach to distinguish these possibilities 30–33. 
We first withheld subsets of cells from the training data, 
quantified uncertainty of each held-out prediction, and 
iteratively added selected cells back to the training set 
before retraining the classifier (Fig. 3b). If uncertainty 
primarily reflects missing coverage of signaling state

We quantified uncertainty using Monte Carlo dropout 
(MC dropout), which generates multiple predictions for 
each cell (Fig. 3c) 31. Total uncertainty was quantified 
as predictive entropy, which measures the spread of 
probabilities assigned to each of the classes 34. As 
expected, predictive entropy increased as performance 
declined across all models and signaling pathways, 
confirming that this metric identified difficult or 
ambiguous predictions (Supplementary Fig. 5).  
We further decomposed total uncertainty into an 
aleatoric component, calculated as the entropy of the 
forward passes through the network, and an epistemic 
component, calculated as the difference between total 
entropy and aleatoric uncertainty. We validated this 
decomposition using synthetic datasets with known 
sources of uncertainty.  These synthetic data sets were 
generated with a 40-gene response indicator block 
which is increased in the positive signaling cases along 
with 200 non-response genes. To create an aleatoric-
dominated case, we introduced label noise by flipping 
individual pathway labels. To create an epistemic-
dominated setting, a synthetic gene set composed of 
120 genes with random expression values was 
simulated in cells. This represented unobserved gene 
expression for the model. In each round of training, the 
model was introduced to these cells and can learn the 
synthetic gene expression. In both cases, the estimated 
uncertainty decomposition correctly recovered the 
known 
dominant 
source 
of 
uncertainty 
(Supplementary Fig. 6). 
We 
then

this

to 
evaluate 
uncertainties of model predictions on the screen 
datasets. Across model classes and across both in-
sample and held-out-batch evaluations, BALD-guided 
acquisition did not outperform random sampling, 
measured by either exact-match accuracy or Hamming 
distance (Supplementary Fig. 7). In several settings, 
random acquisition performed better than BALD. 
Consistent with this result, uncertainty decomposition 
showed that aleatoric uncertainty dominated across all 
models (Fig. 3d).  At the single-cell level, intermediate 
pathway scores were associated with higher estimated 
aleatoric uncertainty and disagreement with the binary 
treatment labels. (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Figs. 8-9). 
These results indicate that ambiguous predictions arise 
predominantly from intermediate biological states 
between two perturbation classes, rather than from 
insufficient training coverage, and thus intermediate 
pathway activation scores might represent graded 
pathway activities.  
To further evaluate whether these intermediate pathway 
activation scores align with graded activation of 
signaling pathways, we measured their correlation with 
the levels of canonical signaling response genes (Table 
1) 
35. The correlation was normalized to a null 
distribution of randomly chosen genes and quantified as 
the monotonicity. Canonical response genes varied 
continuously with the corresponding pathway activation 
scores, confirming that the output probabilities align 
with the strength of signaling response (Fig. 3f). 
Together, these analyses support the interpretation that 
intermediate scores capture real heterogeneity in how 
strongly cells respond to a nominal perturbation, rather 
than simply identifying regions in which the model lacks 
training data. 
Graded 
scores 
recover 
imposed 
signaling 
gradients in a controlled in vitro system 
To experimentally test whether the graded pathway 
scores 
corresponded 
to 
independently 
defined 
differences in signaling strength, we analyzed a human 
endoderm patterning system in which morphogen 
gradients were imposed through a microfluidic system

posterior to form posterior-to-anterior concentration 
gradients, whereas anterior identity was maintained by 
BMP and TGF-β inhibition (Fig. 4a). This dataset 
provides a stringent test because the model was trained 
on discrete combinations but evaluated on cells 
exposed to continuous spatial gradients. 
CIRCE predictions successfully recovered the spatial

2016). CIRCE predictions varied continuously across 
this axis, with BMP, WNT, and FGF scores increasing 
toward the posterior in the expected direction, 
reconstructing the geometry of the imposed gradients 
from 
transcriptional 
state 
alone 
(Fig. 
4c, 
Supplementary Figs. 10-11). To avoid ambiguity in the 
ground-truth labels, we focused on signals that were 
activated by an externally provided agonist. The 
recovery of continuous, correctly-oriented gradients 
demonstrates that the model’s intermediate scores 
contain information about relative signaling activity

Discussion 
Binary combinatorial screens do not merely produce 
binary responses to each perturbation; instead, they 
contain a hidden response space that varies 
continuously in magnitude across all cells. We show 
that this structure can be used to learn graded 
representations of cellular responses to perturbations 
without requiring continuous experimental labels. 
Crucially, in a combinatorial screen, each perturbation 
is 
observed 
across 
multiple 
cellular 
contexts, 
generating a range of response magnitude that has 
inherently encoded structure. We exploit this structure 
by using Boltzmann smoothing over Hamming 
distances 
to 
encourage 
predicted 
probability 
distributions to be locally coherent in combinatorial 
space. Consequently, predictions that differ from the 
observed condition by a single pathway receive more 
credit than those separated by multiple pathways. 
Marginalizing the resulting joint distribution produces 
pathway scores that are less polarized, better calibrated, 
and aligned with variations in signaling levels across 
tissues. Thus, combinations become not a classification 
burden, but a source of calibration.  
Our results further suggest that uncertainty can capture 
meaningful biological signals. While the model is 
trained on discrete identity labels, cellular responses 
themselves do not necessarily segregate into hard 
categories. Our active-learning and gene-response 
analyses indicate that much of the resulting uncertainty 
reflects irreducible ambiguity due to a continuum of 
response states rather than inadequate model training. 
Therefore, we interpret the prediction scores as relative 
measures of pathway activity. Future datasets 
containing matched ligand doses, direct pathway 
reporters, or phosphoproteomic measurements will be 
needed to map these scores onto absolute activity 
levels. 
This structured-label smoothing approach also has 
practical implications for experimental design. Our 
finding suggest that models may scale more effectively 
by training on diverse combinations than by collecting 
ever-larger numbers of cells from a narrow set of 
conditions 
or 
exhaustively 
titrating 
each 
input. 
Combinatorial perturbation screens could therefore be 
designed not only to identify interactions, but also to 
span the response space required for calibration. An 
important direction for future work will be to determine 
which combinations are most informative for calibration 
and out-of-distribution prediction. 
More generally, we show that distinct training objectives 
can leverage seemingly unrelated properties of 
biological data. Even with a simplified representation of 
perturbation relationships, such as by using the 
Hamming distance between combinations, our models 
learned both signal identity and graded response states. 
A useful next step would be to define or learn pathway-
specific distance measures from perturbation data or 
prior network knowledge, allowing the label geometry to 
capture synergistic, antagonistic, and directional 
interactions. Additionally, incorporating key inhibitory 
molecules, such as signaling pathway antagonists, into 
a probabilistic framework should further improve the 
ability to fine-tune predicted signaling histories or 
spatial distributions.  
In 
summary, 
our 
structured-label 
framework 
repurposes binary combinatorial perturbations to learn 
continuous representations of cellular states. By 
recovering graded pathway activities without dose-
resolved training annotation, it provides a general route 
towards modeling dose-dependent cell-state transitions. 
Applied to developmental signaling contexts, these 
models could guide the differentiation of stem cells into 
desired cell types, and the design of organoids with 
increasingly precise multicellular organization. More 
broadly, extending this approach to small-molecule 
drug perturbation screens could enable predictions of 
how drug doses shape target-pathway activity, 
supporting the rational design of dose-optimized 
therapies.

Perturbation screen data processing 
For the combinatorial signal perturbation screens 13,25, single-cell RNA-sequencing data were processed using 
scanpy. Raw counts were library-size normalized to 10000 counts per cell and log-transformed. Highly variable 
genes were selected using standard dispersion-based criteria (min_mean = 0.0125, max_mean = 3, 
min_dispersion = 0.5). Cells were drawn from six experimental batches from two combinatorial signaling ligand 
screens, both of which include six key developmental signaling pathways (BMP, FGF, RA, SHH, TGFB, WNT).

binary strings of signaling combinations was computed between the cells. The transcriptional distance was 
computed through three different metrics in the highly-variable gene space: cosine, Euclidean and Wasserstein.

Signaling state classification benchmarking 
Pathway-state classifiers were implemented by fine-tuning scANVI models on the scVI backbone. Classifiers 
were fine-tuned for five epochs using early stopping (patience = 10), a batch size of 256, and gradient clipping 
at 1.0. We compared four baseline formulations with CIRCE. (1) The binary formulation used six independent 
pathway classifiers. Pathway-specific architectures were selected by cross-validation over depth L in {1,2, hidden 
width H in {512,2048, and latent dimension Z in {16,32,64. (2) The conventional multiclass formulation used a 
single 64-class softmax classifier (L = 2, H = 1,024, Z = 64). (3) The multitask formulation used a shared latent 
representation with six binary cross-entropy heads. (4) The joint formulation optimized both multiclass and binary 
objectives. CIRCE used the same architecture as the conventional multiclass model but was trained with the 
Hamming-Boltzmann soft-label objective described below.

Active Learning 
Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement (BALD) was implemented by using the Monte Carlo dropouts and 
ensembles to calculate the difference between the entropy of the mean prediction and the mean entropy of 
individual stochastic predictions 43. To test whether uncertainty identified informative missing examples, one 
experimental batch was held out in each cross-validation fold. 20% of held-out cells were reserved for testing, 
and the remaining pool of 80% were successively acquired during the experiments. The classifier was initialized 
with acquisition-pool labels masked. In each of four rounds, 15% of the remaining pool was selected, labels were 
revealed, and the classifier was retrained.

We compared two acquisition strategies: BALD and random sampling. In random sampling, cells were chosen 
randomly from the remaining pool. Random sampling was repeated with three seeds and followed the same 
acquisition ratios as BALD. The same analysis was performed for six held-out-batch folds and for an in-sample 
random split. 
Synthetic Data Generation 
Counts were simulated for all 64 possible combinations of the six pathways from a negative-binomial model in 
which each pathway increased the log-mean expression of its own 40-gene marker block alongside 200 non-
responsive genes. Aleatoric uncertainty was introduced by flipping the recorded labels with a probability of 0.3, 
independent of expression. Epistemic uncertainty was simulated by adding additional cells which expressed a 
120-gene program that was not present in any of the other cells. All models were trained on the labeled cells 
with the same architectures and settings as the other analysis. 30% of labeled cells were held out for testing. 
Uncertainty was decomposed with 15 MC-dropout passes into aleatoric and epistemic components.  
Evaluation Metrics 
Categorical performance was evaluated using exact-match accuracy and normalized Hamming distance. Exact-
match accuracy was the fraction of cells for which all six pathway labels were predicted correctly. Normalized 
Hamming distance was the fraction of pathway-level mismatches per cell. Pathway-level performance was 
additionally assessed using accuracy, area under the precision-recall curve, area under the receiver operating 
characteristic curve, and F1 score.  
Calibration was quantified using expected calibration error (ECE) by grouping predictions according to 
confidence and calculating the weighted difference between confidence and empirical accuracy across bins. For 
multiclass models, calibration was evaluated both for the complete combination and for marginalized pathway 
scores. Intermediate pathway scores were defined as values between 0.1 and 0.9; this threshold was used 
descriptively to compare the polarization of model outputs and was not treated as a biochemical activity range.

Analysis of graded signaling systems 
For the microfluidic endoderm dataset, diffusion pseudotime was used to order cells along the inferred anterior-
posterior axis. Diffusion pseudotime generates a diffusion map from the nearest-neighbor graph (k = 15) 
transition matrix and each cell’s pseudotime is its diffusion distance from a designated root cell 44. Diffusion 
pseudotime was computed using scanpy.tl.diffmap in scanpy 1.10.0. These trajectories were rooted based on 
marker genes relevant for each cell type. Axis orientation was verified using OTX2 and CDX2 expression. 
Pathway scores were predicted for individual cells and summarized within each bin along the pseudotime axis. 
Where indicated for comparison of spatial shapes, each pathway profile was min-max normalized independently. 
For neural tube differentiation, BMP, WNT and SHH scores were ordered along the dorsal-ventral axis with 
classic differentiation markers (Table 2). The same trained framework was applied to embryonic somitic 
mesoderm, and predicted activities were visualized across the inferred developmental axis.
