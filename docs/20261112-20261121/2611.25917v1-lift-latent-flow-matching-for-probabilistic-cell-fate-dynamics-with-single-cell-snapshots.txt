LIFT: Latent Flow Matching for Probabilistic Cell Fate Dynamics with Single
Cell Snapshots∗
Bowen Yang† , Junda Ying‡ , Yuxuan Wang§ , Peijie Zhou¶, and Lei Zhang∥
Abstract.
Lineage tracing links cell states through clonal ancestry, but does not generally identify unique source-
descendant pairings.
Learning from such data therefore requires a model of conditional outcomes, rather
than only the terminal population distribution. We introduce LIFT, a latent flow framework for probabilistic
cell-state generation from lineage-informed couplings. Variational inference encodes alternative outcomes in a
latent space, while a conditional prior enables sampling from the initial state alone. A Mean Flow decoder
predicts future cell state directly, requiring one decoder evaluation per sampling, which makes it convenient
to predict cell fate through large scale Monte-Carlo sampling. This construction combines a representation of
one-to-many relationships with efficient sampling and an analysis of conditional prediction error. Experiments
on synthetic and real datasets demonstrate LIFT’s ability to recover both population distribution and cell fate
probabilities.
Relevance to Life Sciences.
Predicting differentiation and reprogramming requires estimating both which outcomes are possible and
how their probabilities depend on the observed initial state.
We evaluate LIFT on lineage-traced mouse
hematopoiesis and fibroblast reprogramming using transcriptomic state representations.
Lineage-informed
training improves fate-probability prediction relative to geometric coupling, while the hematopoietic application
illustrates graded fate bias in regions with mixed clonal outcomes. Synthetic branching experiments further
demonstrate accurate population reconstruction and branching probabilities at low sampling cost.
Mathematical Content.
We formulate barcode resolved observations as measures linked by an unknown true lineage-compatible
coupling, distinguish this target from the empirical coupling used for training, and quantify reconstruction error
via conditional Wasserstein discrepancies. The irreducible error of deterministic endpoint maps is characterized
by the true conditional variance. We establish reconstruction estimates for flow matching and Mean Flow
decoders and bound the total conditional error by coupling discrepancy, decoder reconstruction, and mismatch
between the aggregated posterior and sampling prior.
Under regularity and bounded Gaussian prior scale
assumptions, the latter terms are controlled by the training objectives. Boundary sensitive probability estimates
and Monte Carlo concentration bounds then connect conditional reconstruction to the accuracy of estimated
fate proportions, separating model error from finite-sampling uncertainty.
Key words.
lineage tracing, single-cell trajectory inference, cell fate prediction, conditional generative
modeling, flow matching, Mean Flow, Wasserstein distance

1. Introduction. Understanding how an early cell state constrains its future fate is a
central problem in developmental biology and cellular reprogramming. Single-cell RNA se-
quencing provides detailed molecular snapshots, but its destructive nature generally prevents
repeated measurements of the same cell. Trajectory inference must therefore reconstruct devel-
opmental relationships from population snapshots and additional assumptions [13]. Lineage-
tracing technologies provide an experimental source of such information. Methods such as
CellTagging and LARRY recover heritable barcodes together with transcriptomic states, link-
ing clonal ancestry to subsequent outcomes [1, 22]. These observations motivate models that
use lineage information to predict not only a likely terminal distribution, but also the proba-
bilities of cellular fates.
A shared barcode does not, however, generally determine a unique pairing between in-
dividual cells observed at different times. When several source and terminal cells belong to
the same clone, their molecular correspondence remains only partially specified. We repre-
sent the true endpoint relationship by an unknown coupling π⋆and distinguish it from the
lineage-informed coupling π constructed for training. The prediction target is the conditional
law
K⋆(x, A) = Pπ⋆(Y ∈A | X = x),
where x is the observed initial state and A is a region of terminal state space. Different source-
descendant relationships can have identical marginals but imply different fate probabilities.
Recovering this law requires more than matching the endpoint populations.
Probabilistic models of cellular differentiation range from fate mapping on observed states
to continuous descriptions of population dynamics. Palantir estimates terminal fate prob-
abilities from transitions on the transcriptomic manifold [15], while CellRank 2 integrates
multiple sources of directional and temporal information through Markov state modeling [21].
Waddington-OT estimates probabilistic couplings between successive populations while ac-
counting for growth and death [14], and PRESCIENT learns stochastic cell-state dynamics
from expression time series [24]. These approaches already represent heterogeneous outcomes
and, in several cases, provide fate probabilities. Without explicit lineage constraints, the in-
ferred fate relationships depend on assumptions or constraints used by each method, but they
need not coincide with those supported by lineage observations.
Explicit incorporation of lineage information provides additional constraints on these re-
lationships. LineageOT uses lineage trees to inform ancestral-state estimation and transport
coupling [3]; CoSpar reconstructs transition maps from sparse clonal observations using spar-
sity and local coherence [20]; and moslin combines expression similarity with lineage geometry
through fused Gromov–Wasserstein transport [9]. LineageVAE further uses variational latent
dynamics to reconstruct historical states toward unobserved common progenitors [11]. These
studies establish lineage observations as valuable information for both transition inference
and state reconstruction. Nevertheless, an endpoint transition map does not uniquely specify
intermediate paths. Estimating a lineage-informed coupling and constructing a continuous
generator that retains its conditional structure are thus separate, complementary tasks.
Generative modeling provides tools for constructing continuous conditional models. Flow
matching learns time-dependent velocity fields without numerical integration during training
[10, 17], and Mean Flow addresses sampling cost by learning interval-average velocities for

Augmented Bridge Matching addresses cou-

For lineage-informed applications, the remaining task is to combine one-to-many conditional
representation with direct time-dependent sampling. This combination motivates the LIFT
framework developed below.
We introduce LIFT, a latent flow framework that combines conditional representation,
efficient generation, and error control for this task.
Building on variational inference and
conditional latent-variable modeling [7, 16], an encoder infers latent codes from training pairs,
while a conditional prior predicts their distribution from the initial state alone. We formulate
Flow Matching and Mean Flow reconstruction surrogates and use the Mean Flow decoder for
direct generation. Its average velocity parameterization [4] allows only one decoder evaluation
per sampling, and keeping the latent code fixed across time defines a continuous generated
path. The coupling can incorporate geometric or clonal information without changing the
sampling architecture.
Our theoretical analysis makes the conditional prediction objective explicit. We charac-
terize the best deterministic approximation by its irreducible conditional-variance error and
establish reconstruction bounds for both FM and Mean Flow decoders. Under the stated
regularity and prior-scale assumptions, the total conditional Wasserstein error is bounded by
the discrepancy between π and π⋆, reconstruction under encoder samples, and mismatch be-
tween the aggregated posterior and the sampling prior. We further translate this bound into
fate-probability estimates, including the effects of classification boundaries and finite Monte
Carlo sampling. These results concern a fitted model and do not identify the true coupling or
a unique intermediate biological trajectory.
The experiments follow the same conditional prediction perspective. A synthetic branching
system tests recovery of both population distributions and branching probabilities. Compar-
isons across numerical sampling budgets assess the balance between one-step efficiency and
prediction accuracy. Finally, mouse hematopoiesis and direct reprogramming demonstrate the
use of lineage information for cell fate prediction, with improved probability scores relative to
LIFT trained with geometric couplings.
We organize this paper as follows. Section 2 formulates the reconstruction problem, Sec-
tion 3 develops LIFT, and Section 4 establishes its error estimates. Section 5 presents the
experiments, followed by Section 6 on conclusions and future work.

4. Error Analysis of Conditional Dynamics and Fate Prediction. We analyze how the
training objectives control conditional reconstruction and fate prediction. The analysis pro-
ceeds in three steps. First, the FM and MF losses control reconstruction under the training
coupling π. Second, reconstruction and latent distribution matching are related to the true
conditional error under π⋆. Finally, this error is translated into fate-probability error and
combined with the uncertainty of finite Monte Carlo sampling.

6. Discussion and Conclusion. Lineage tracing links molecular states to clonal ancestry,
but does not generally reveal unique cell-to-cell pairings across destructively sampled popu-
lations. This motivates a conditional-generation problem: the same observed state may be
compatible with multiple future outcomes, whereas a deterministic state map assigns it a single
endpoint. Consequently, reproducing the terminal population distribution alone does not en-
sure accurate fate probabilities. A model must also represent the one-to-many relationships
supported by the available lineage information.
LIFT addresses this mismatch through a variational latent representation of conditional
variability. An encoder learns latent distributions from endpoint pairs, a conditional prior
predicts them from the initial state, and a Mean Flow decoder generates alternative outcomes
without numerical integration. Under the stated regularity and prior-scale assumptions, we
bound the true conditional distribution error by the discrepancy in the training coupling,
decoder reconstruction error, and latent training-sampling mismatch. The analysis further
connects these errors to fate-probability estimation, providing a theoretical basis for the con-
ditional prediction objective.
The experiments show that LIFT combines accurate branching-probability prediction with
competitive population distribution reconstruction and efficient one-step sampling. In mouse
hematopoiesis and direct reprogramming, lineage-informed training improves fate prediction
relative to the geometric-coupling variant. The hematopoietic example further illustrates how
graded predictions represent mixed clonal outcomes instead of imposing hard assignments.
Together, these results support the use of LIFT for efficient prediction of differentiation and

reprogramming probabilities from molecular states and lineage information.
The current framework nevertheless has several limitations. Its generative dynamics do
not explicitly model unbalanced effects such as proliferation and cell death, and its predic-
tions remain dependent on the supplied coupling and chosen state representation. Endpoint
constraints also do not uniquely identify intermediate biological paths or the sources of condi-
tional variability. Future work will extend the framework to unbalanced dynamics, incorporate
additional observation times and biological constraints. These extensions could improve both
the biological fidelity of generated trajectories and the reliability of fate predictions.
Appendix A. Mathematical and Computational Background.
This appendix introduces the concepts connecting barcode-resolved observations, condi-
tional generation, and fate-probability estimation.
Probabilistic modeling of cell dynamics. A probability measure µt describes the relative
abundance of cell states at time t. Population evolution can therefore be modeled as a con-
tinuous family of distributions connecting µ0 and µ1, rather than as a single representative
trajectory. Waddington-OT infers probabilistic couplings between observed populations, ac-
counting for proliferation and death [14]. TrajectoryNet learns continuous paths using neural
ordinary differential equations and optimal-transport regularization [18], while PRESCIENT
models population evolution through stochastic dynamics [24].
These approaches connect
snapshot observations through assumed dynamics. A coupling additionally specifies how ini-
tial and terminal states are paired; lineage information constrains these relationships [3]. The
conditional law K⋆(x, ·) then describes the possible outcomes of a fixed initial state, which are
not determined by the endpoint population measures alone.
Variational inference and variational autoencoders. Latent-variable models generate obser-
vations through auxiliary variables whose values are not directly measured. Inferring their
posterior distribution is often computationally difficult. Variational inference replaces this
posterior with a tractable approximation and optimizes a lower bound on the data log likeli-
hood. Variational autoencoders (VAEs) implement this idea using neural encoder and decoder
networks [7, 8]. Their objective balances reconstruction against a Kullback–Leibler (KL) di-
vergence penalty, which compares the encoder distribution with the prior.
In conditional
generation [16], the model also receives an input x. LIFT uses qϕ(z | x, y) during training
and samples pη(z | x) at inference. Different latent samples allow the same initial state to
generate different outcomes.
The latent variable represents conditional variability, not an
automatically identified biological mechanism.
Flow matching and Mean Flow. Flow matching trains a velocity field by regression against
velocities of easily sampled reference paths [10, 17]. For the linear path Xt = (1 −t)X + tY ,
the reference velocity is Y −X.
Training therefore avoids solving the learned differential
equation, although generation with an FM decoder still requires numerical integration. Mean
Flow instead learns velocity averaged over a time interval [4]. For r < t, its average velocity
along a trajectory satisfies

Here, the average is over time along a trajectory, not over alternative fates. Learning this
quantity allows LIFT to predict each requested state directly, with a latent code held fixed

required to move probability mass from one to the other [12]. For probability measures P, Q

SM3.1. Experimental setup and evaluation protocol.
Data and implementation. The controlled benchmark is a two-dimensional branching sys-
tem with an analytically specified branch probability. The biological applications use barcode-
resolved hematopoietic data from LARRY [SM22] and direct-reprogramming data from CellT-
agging [SM1]. Dataset sizes and the correspondence between experimental and rescaled times
are recorded in Table SM1.
The reported LIFT implementation uses multilayer perceptrons with ReLU activations
for the encoder, conditional prior, and decoder, with a two-dimensional latent variable in
the principal experiments. Training uses Adam and the objective introduced in Section 3.3.
Our timing results were obtained on an Intel i7-13700 CPU. Dataset-specific network widths,
optimization parameters, and sensitivity results are contained below.
Training couplings and comparison methods. We compare geometric endpoint couplings with
lineage-informed couplings, denoted by πgeo and πlin. The latter use shared barcodes to restrict
the admissible source–target relationships; the reprogramming experiment additionally uses

The primary comparisons include deterministic CFM and Mean Flow, the score-matching
baseline denoted SM. We also compare latent decoders sharing the encoder–prior construction:
a static MLP, an FM decoder, and the Mean Flow decoder used by LIFT. These experiments
distinguish changes in the coupling from changes in the generator.
Evaluation and data separation. Population reconstruction is assessed by a Wasserstein
distance in the evaluation state representation. Conditional evaluation uses analytic branch
probabilities in the controlled benchmark and lineage derived outcome information in the bi-

per initial state. The terminal fate assignment rule is shared across methods and is specified
by using the convex hull as fate region. Throughout evaluated datasets, convex hulls of dif-

randomly separate cells from 20% barcodes as test set.

SM3.5. Additional qualitative comparisons.
Conditioned prediction fields. Figure SM1 displays the instantaneous velocity fields of CFM
and the interval-average velocity outputs of the unaugmented Mean Flow model. Both exhibit
a low-magnitude region separating the two branch-directed regimes.
In Figure SM2, the
location of this separating region changes with the latent code in LIFT. For a fixed code, the
prediction remains deterministic, but different codes allow the same observed state to receive
different predictions. This provides a qualitative illustration of latent conditioning; it is not a
proof of nonintersection or of a topological property of the learned dynamics.
Posterior and conditional-prior representations. Figure SM3 compares posterior means mϕ(x, y)
and conditional-prior means mη(x) for the hematopoietic application. Endpoint pairs associ-
ated with Mo and Neu outcomes occupy different regions in the posterior representation. The
encoder receives the terminal state, so this separation illustrates how endpoint information
is encoded, rather than an independent prediction made from the initial state. The prior
representation uses the initial state alone and also shows fate-associated structure. Fate prob-
abilities, however, are obtained by sampling the full prior and applying the decoder, not by
assigning a fate to the prior mean. These visualizations characterize learned representations
without identifying a latent biological mechanism.
Baseline fate maps. Figures SM4 provide the complete baseline fate maps for the hematopoiesis
applications. The maps compare geometric and lineage-informed couplings for CFM and Mean
Flow, together with SM. They complement the numerical population and probability scores
by showing where the spatial patterns of predicted fates differ. Gray regions in the baseline
visualizations mark predictions outside the assigned fate regions, rather than an additional
biological fate. The maps should be read together with the quantitative comparisons, since
visually separated outcomes do not by themselves establish accurate conditional probabilities
or irreversible fate commitment.
