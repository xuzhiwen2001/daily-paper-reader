Abstract
Background: Precision oncology increasingly relies on integrating heteroge-
neous data spanning the biological axis from genomics and transcriptomics to
proteomics and digital pathology. However, many current fusion methods pri-
oritize predictive performance while offering limited support for structured,
biologically grounded explainability, which can reduce robustness and scalabil-
ity when applied to large clinical cohorts with heterogeneous and incomplete
modality availability.
Methods: We propose a Hierarchical Interpretable Tensor Fusion, HIT-Fuse,
for integrating six modalities: somatic copy number variation, DNA methylation,
RNA sequencing, microRNA expression, protein abundance (RPPA), and vision
transformer-extracted pathomics features across four TCGA cohorts: glioma,
lung adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, and kidney cancer. High-dimensional inputs
are first compressed into latent representations using modality-specific autoen-
coders, followed by a two-stage CP tensor decomposition that preserves bio-
logically aligned structure. To handle missing modalities, we reconstruct latent
representations for patients with available clinical data by leveraging structured
clinical covariates together with observed omics profiles, and retrain downstream
models on the expanded cohort. Global and local explainable artificial intelligence
(xAI) attributions are computed through a mathematical traceback mechanism.

Results: Across glioma, breast, and kidney cancer cohorts, HIT-Fuse yields well-
separated latent subgroups with significant survival stratification in both com-
plete and reconstructed settings. Cohort expansion produces cohort-dependent
performance gains, including a 56.4% relative improvement in weighted
F1-score for glioma. Cross-domain biological validation integrating DepMap
CRISPR dependency screens, miRTarBase interactions, and RPPA annotations
confirms functional support for decoded drivers across four cancer cohorts.
Essential gene rates range from 6.2% (glioma) to 7.6% (lung adenocarcinoma),
with representative confirmed drivers including ANAPC11 (glioma), SNRPD1
(breast), PLK1 (lung), and RPL14 (kidney). Additionally, 71–92% of decoded
miRNAs show regulatory support in miRTarBase, and 77–84% of selected genes
are covered in DepMap.
Conclusions: By combining latent-space fusion, biologically structured ten-
sor decomposition, and attribution-based explainability, HIT-Fuse provides a
scalable and interpretable framework for multi-omics and digital pathology
integration, enabling robust cancer prognosis modeling at cohort scale.

1 Introduction
Precision oncology increasingly depends on the integration of heterogeneous biolog-
ical data spanning multiple molecular layers and tissue-level phenotypes. Advances
in high-throughput sequencing enable comprehensive profiling of genomic alterations,
DNA methylation, transcriptomic activity, proteomic abundance, and microRNA
regulation at population scale [14]. In parallel, computational pathology and self-
supervised vision models have enabled scalable representation learning from whole-
slide histopathology images, extracting high-dimensional morphological embeddings
from gigapixel tissue sections [23]. Together, these modalities provide a systems-level
characterization of tumor biology across the central dogma, from DNA to RNA to pro-
tein and tissue phenotype. However, integrating these heterogeneous data streams into
scalable, interpretable, and biologically grounded predictive models remains a central
challenge. Many multi-omics integration frameworks prioritize predictive performance
through feature concatenation, kernel-based fusion, or deep neural architectures that
treat modalities as flat feature spaces [3, 26]. While such approaches can achieve
strong classification or survival performance, they often underrepresent the hierarchi-
cal organization of biological systems, limiting mechanistic interpretability and clinical
transparency [15, 17, 22]. Tensor-based representations offer a principled alternative
for modeling structured multi-way relationships in high-dimensional biomedical data.
By preserving interactions between patients, modalities, and latent biological factors,
tensor decompositions extend beyond conventional matrix-based fusion [18]. Prior
work has applied CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) and Tucker decompositions to
multi-omics feature extraction, subtype discovery, and survival modeling [31]. More
2


recently, scalable tensor factorization strategies have been developed for large biomed-
ical datasets, highlighting their suitability for big data contexts [27]. Nevertheless,
most tensor-driven approaches operate at a single, flat fusion level, potentially diluting
biologically meaningful structure across molecular layers. Concurrently, multimodal
learning frameworks integrating digital pathology with molecular profiles have demon-
strated improved prognostic modeling and molecular subtype prediction [8, 10]. Vision
transformer and self-supervised learning approaches, including DINO-based architec-
tures, enable robust whole-slide representation learning that can be fused with omics
data [23]. However, image features are typically incorporated as auxiliary embeddings
or fused at late stages, limiting the ability to uncover biologically aligned cross-modal
interactions. A further practical challenge arises from incomplete modality availabil-
ity in real-world cohorts. Large-scale resources such as The Cancer Genome Atlas
(TCGA) provide rich but heterogeneous multi-omics datasets with pervasive miss-
ingness across molecular layers [1]. Restricting analysis to the strict intersection of
complete cases substantially reduces cohort size and statistical power. While proba-
bilistic multi-view and representation learning approaches have explored cross-modal
alignment and latent completion [3, 20], few frameworks integrate biologically struc-
tured fusion, modality completion, and external biological validation within a unified
and interpretable pipeline.
To address these limitations, we propose an explainable and scalable hierarchical
tensor framework, termed HIT-Fuse (Figure 1), for integrating multi-omics and digital
pathology data. The framework learns structured patient representations that respect
biological organization across molecular layers while supporting interpretable subtype
prediction and survival modeling. To accommodate incomplete clinical cohorts, HIT-
Fuse incorporates a modality completion mechanism that enables learning beyond
strict intersection subsets. We evaluate the framework across four independent TCGA
cancer cohorts: glioma, lung adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, and kidney cancer.
Contributions.
We summarize the main contributions of this work as follows:
• We propose a biologically structured two-stage tensor fusion framework that
integrates multi-omics and digital pathology while preserving central-dogma rela-
tionships across molecular layers, enabling interpretable patient representations at
cohort scale.
• We introduce a latent completion framework that reconstructs missing modali-
ties using observed omics and clinical covariates, enabling analysis beyond strict
intersection cohorts.
• We establish an end-to-end biological validation workflow that links hierarchical
tensor explainability to external functional evidence, validating discovered drivers
using DepMap CRISPR dependencies and pathway enrichment across multiple
cancer cohorts.
• We develop a hierarchical explainability framework that delivers both global cohort-
level drivers and patient-specific molecular attributions by tracing tensor meta-
factors back to raw biological features.

2 Related Work
Deep learning has become a dominant paradigm for multi-omics integration due
to its ability to capture nonlinear relationships in high-dimensional molecular data.
Chaudhary et al. [7] demonstrated that stacked autoencoders can learn modality-
specific latent representations from RNA-seq, miRNA, and DNA methylation data
for survival prediction in hepatocellular carcinoma. Similarly, DeepProg combined
autoencoder-based embedding with classical machine learning to achieve cross-cohort
prognostic modeling across TCGA datasets [25]. While these frameworks achieve
strong predictive performance, they typically rely on flat latent concatenation, treating
modalities as independent feature blocks and overlooking hierarchical biological orga-
nization across molecular layers. Probabilistic multi-view factor models have sought
to improve interpretability by explicitly modeling shared and modality-specific varia-
tion. MOFA+ introduced a scalable Bayesian group factor analysis framework capable
of learning structured latent representations across heterogeneous omics layers [4].
Although such models improve interpretability relative to purely deep architectures,
they remain fundamentally matrix-based and do not explicitly model higher-order
modality interactions or hierarchical biological flow.
Tensor decomposition provides a principled alternative for modeling multi-way
biomedical data. Foundational work by Kolda and Bader [19] established CANDE-
COMP/PARAFAC (CP) and Tucker decompositions as scalable tools for structured
high-dimensional analysis. Subsequent applications demonstrated the utility of ten-
sor methods for cancer subtype discovery and survival modeling from multi-omics
4


cohorts [30]. A closely related approach by
[5] combined modality-specific autoen-
coders with single-stage CP tensor fusion for cancer risk stratification. While this
framework showed the benefit of integrating deep representation learning with ten-
sor analysis, it performs fusion at a single flat level and does not explicitly encode
biological hierarchy, incorporate digital pathology, or provide structured end-to-end
biological validation.
In parallel, multimodal learning frameworks integrating digital pathology with
molecular profiles have demonstrated improved diagnostic and prognostic perfor-
mance. Attention-based co-learning models such as MCAT [9] and transformer-based
multimodal survival frameworks such as PORPOISE [11] enable joint modeling of
whole-slide images and genomic data. Self-supervised vision transformers have further
strengthened histopathology representation learning [23]. However, most existing mul-
timodal systems employ late fusion or flat concatenation strategies and treat image
features as auxiliary modalities rather than embedding them within a biologically
structured fusion hierarchy.
A persistent challenge in large-scale cancer cohorts is incomplete modality avail-
ability. Public resources such as TCGA provide rich but heterogeneous datasets with
pervasive missingness across molecular layers. Although recent multi-view represen-
tation learning approaches have explored cross-modal alignment and completion [21],
most do not integrate structured biological fusion with external functional validation.
Finally, explainable artificial intelligence methods have been increasingly adopted
to enhance transparency in biomedical prediction models. Feature attribution tech-
niques such as Integrated Gradients [28] and SHAP [24] provide post-hoc importance
scores for molecular features. However, these techniques are typically applied to
flat architectures and do not inherently support hierarchical tensor-based fusion or
closed-loop biological validation.
Overall, prior work has demonstrated the effectiveness of deep representation
learning, tensor decomposition, multimodal pathology integration, and explainable AI
in isolation. However, existing frameworks typically combine only a subset of these
components. In particular, most tensor-driven and deep multi-omics approaches rely
on single-stage fusion and provide limited linkage between model explanations and
experimentally grounded biological evidence. These limitations motivate the develop-
ment of a unified hierarchical tensor framework that integrates biologically aligned
multi-modal fusion, modality completion, and end-to-end biological validation within
a scalable and interpretable pipeline.
3 Materials and Methods
This section presents a scalable and biologically grounded framework for integrating
heterogeneous multi-omics and histopathology data using hierarchical tensor fusion
with end-to-end global and local explainability. Throughout, we distinguish between
two evaluation cohorts: a real-only intersection cohort (E1), consisting of patients with
complete observations across all modalities, and a completed all-patient cohort (E2),
consisting of the full clinical population augmented via latent-space super-imputation.

3.2 Phase 2: Hierarchical Tensor Factorization
The primary methodological contribution is a hierarchical tensor fusion strategy that
respects biological organization by separating within-layer integration from cross-layer
interaction modeling. Modalities are grouped according to the central dogma:
D = {SCNV, METH},
R = {RNA, miRNA},
P = {RPPA, IMG}.
The DNA layer (SCNV, METH) captures genomic and epigenomic regulation, the
RNA layer (RNA, miRNA) captures transcriptional activity, and the functional
expression layer (RPPA, IMG) captures downstream biological state. RPPA quanti-
fies protein abundance and pathway activation, while histopathology images reflect
the emergent tissue phenotype shaped by these signaling programs. Both modalities

3.3 Phase 3: Predictive Evaluation
The final fused representation F (HIT −F use) and ablated baselines (M1 group-level
fusion, M2 flat fusion, and modality concatenation) are evaluated on both classification
and survival tasks.
Classification.
We train balanced classifiers, including logistic regression, random forests, and lin-
ear support vector machines, using stratified K-fold cross-validation. Performance is
reported using accuracy and weighted F1 score.
10


Survival analysis.
A Cox proportional hazards model is fit to the fused representations, and concordance
index (C-index) is reported. For unsupervised risk stratification, patient embeddings
are clustered using agglomerative clustering, and group-wise survival differences are
evaluated using the multivariate log-rank test with survival time capped at 3000 days.
Visualization.
Latent spaces are visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-
SNE), and Kaplan–Meier survival curves are plotted for discovered risk groups to
qualitatively assess clinical separation.
3.4 Phase 4: Explainability and Biological Validation
The hierarchical tensor fusion architecture enables a structured interpretability frame-
work that traces predictive evidence across three abstraction levels: fused meta-factors,
biological layers, and raw molecular or imaging features. We distinguish between a
global explanation stage, which identifies cohort-wide important tensor factors and
their dominant biological drivers, and a local explanation stage, which produces
patient-specific molecular attributions by tracing a selected meta-factor back through
the group-level tensor components and modality-specific decoders.

Explainability modes.
Explainability is computed independently within each cancer cohort under two set-
tings. In the standard setting, the hierarchical tensor factors are used as is. In the
balanced setting, we apply per patient ℓ2 normalization to each layer level patient
factor (DNA, RNA, PI) before forming X (HIT −F use) and performing the meta level
CP decomposition, mitigating potential energy imbalance across biological layers for
attribution purposes. For downstream biological validation (e.g., driver inspection and
enrichment analysis), we export both standard and balanced driver tables and addi-
tionally provide a merged table formed by concatenating the two exports; the merged
table does not define a new attribution rule and does not perform any cross cohort
merging.

4.5 Biological Interpretation and External Validation
4.5.1 Cross-Cancer Pathway Enrichment Analysis
To interpret the biological mechanisms captured by the HIT-Fuse framework, we
performed functional enrichment analysis on the union of important genes derived
from meta-factor drivers in each cohort. Enrichment was evaluated using Gene Ontol-
ogy (GO), KEGG, Reactome, and experimentally supported miRNA target libraries.
Figure 5A presents a cross-cancer enrichment fingerprint summarizing significantly
enriched pathways across glioma, breast, lung, and kidney cohorts. Enrichment
intensity is reported as −log10(adjusted p). In glioma, enrichment was strongly
concentrated in immune-related processes, apoptotic regulation, and PI3K–AKT
signaling, consistent with known glioblastoma immune and survival dependencies.
Breast cancer exhibited enrichment in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and receptor
tyrosine kinase pathways. Lung cancer showed moderate enrichment in immune sig-
naling and apoptotic pathways. In contrast, kidney displayed comparatively fewer
significantly enriched pathways in the pooled export, reflecting cohort-specific driver
sparsity. Across cancers, recurrent enrichment in apoptosis, immune signaling, PI3K–
AKT pathway, and cytokine signaling suggests that the HIT-Fuse-derived drivers
consistently capture core oncogenic axes while preserving cancer-specific variation.
4.5.2 External Functional Validation Using DepMap CRISPR
Screens
To further assess functional relevance, we evaluated selected drivers against DepMap
CRISPR–Cas9 gene dependency screens. As summarized in Fig. 5, HIT-Fuse-derived
drivers demonstrate broad coverage within DepMap gene-effect matrices, with 77.2–
83.6% of selected genes matched across cohorts. Among DepMap-matched genes, 6–8%
satisfied the essentiality criterion (¯sg,c < −0.5) in lineage-matched cell lines. Rep-
resentative essential drivers included RPL14 and PRPF31 (kidney), SNRPD1 and
RPL6 (breast), PLK1 and DHODH (lung), and ANAPC11 and PSMB4 (glioma).
In addition, 71.7–92.1% of cohort-specific miRNAs were supported by experimen-
tally validated miRTarBase targets, while RPPA-selected genes exhibited variable
essentiality rates (0–25.7%) across cohorts. Together, these results indicate that HIT-
Fuse-identified drivers are not only statistically salient within the tensor fusion space
but also enriched for experimentally supported functional dependencies.

4.6 Fusion Architecture Ablation Study
To isolate the effect of fusion design, we ablate the fusion architecture while keeping
all other components fixed: (i) the same modality-specific latent representations, (ii)
the same CORCONDIA-guided rank tuning procedure, and (iii) the same downstream
subtype classifiers. Thus, any differences reflect the inductive bias introduced by the
fusion mechanism rather than changes in feature extraction or tuning.
Ablation settings.
We compare four fusion strategies:
• Concat_All (feature concatenation). All modality latents are concatenated
into a single vector per patient and fed directly to the classifier. This baseline
imposes no explicit cross-modal structure.
• M1_GroupCP (group-wise CP). Modalities are first grouped according to
a coarse biological flow: DNA (SCNV+METH), RNA (RNA-seq+miRNA), and
protein–imaging (RPPA+IMG). A CP decomposition is applied separately to each
group tensor to obtain group-specific patient factors, which are then concate-
nated for prediction. This enforces within-group interaction modeling but does not
explicitly model interactions between groups.
• M2_FlatCP (single-stage CP). All modalities are stacked into a single ten-
sor and decomposed with CP, producing one set of patient factors that captures
interactions across all modalities in a single stage, without biological grouping.
• HIT-Fuse (proposed hierarchical fusion). HIT-Fuse extends M1 by introduc-
ing a second-stage meta-factor decomposition over the three group representations
(DNA/RNA/protein–imaging), explicitly capturing cross-group interactions while
preserving the biological grouping.
Quantitative results.
Table 5 summarizes the maximum weighted F1 achieved by each fusion strategy in each
cancer–cohort setting (E1/E2), where the reported value for each fusion corresponds
to its best-performing classifier among the evaluated subtype heads.
Across the eight cancer–cohort combinations, Concat_All attains the highest
weighted F1 in six settings, indicating that strong subtype-discriminative signal is
already present in the aggregated latent features and can be exploited by standard
classifiers without imposing additional structure. Nonetheless, tensor fusion is advan-
tageous in specific cohorts: M2_FlatCP achieves the top result in Glioma (E1), and
HIT-Fuse achieves the top result in LUAD (E1), suggesting that structured interac-
tion modeling can be beneficial depending on cohort characteristics and tumor-specific
cross-modal relationships. Importantly, no single fusion strategy dominates univer-
sally, highlighting that fusion inductive biases interact with cohort composition (E1
vs. E2) and cancer biology.

Interpretability implication.
While HIT-Fuse is not the top performer in weighted F1 in most settings, it remains
competitive and is uniquely designed to support mechanistic interpretation by con-
struction. In particular, HIT-Fuse enables a more direct and biologically aligned

logical layer →modality branch →decoded drivers), whereas flat concatenation
and single-stage tensor fusion do not natively impose this hierarchy and therefore
require heavier post-hoc assumptions to obtain comparable mechanistic narratives.
In this sense, the ablation indicates that predictive optimization (often favoring

5 Discussion
Cohort Coverage and Tensor Stability.
Across four TCGA cohorts and two cohort constructions (E1 intersection vs. E2
completed), the experiments highlight a consistent theme: predictive optimality and
biological structuring are not identical objectives. E2 systematically increases cohort
coverage (Table 1), enabling evaluation on substantially larger and more clinically
representative patient sets. This expansion is aligned with the practical reality of
multimodal clinical cohorts, where complete modality availability is uncommon and
missingness is often structured rather than random [1, 2]. Importantly, the hierarchical
decomposition remains well behaved under expansion: tuned ranks remain moder-
ate and bounded across cancers (Table 2), suggesting that the CORCONDIA-guided,
multi-objective procedure can avoid unstable over-parameterization while adapting to
cohort-specific complexity [6, 19].

This pattern is plausible in multimodal oncology settings: increasing sample size and
heterogeneity can alter subtype separability (especially for imbalanced or clinically
heterogeneous labels), whereas survival modeling often benefits from larger cohorts
and may be less sensitive to marginal class-boundary shifts when trained with regular-
ized Cox objectives [12, 13]. The strong KM separations observed in BRCA, Glioma,
and Kidney under E2 (Fig. 4) further indicate that the learned fused representation
contains clinically meaningful axes that translate into risk stratification, consistent
with prior multimodal survival literature combining pathology and omics signals [29].
Why concatenation often dominates the ablation table.
The fusion ablation (Table 5) shows that Concat_All yields the highest weighted
F1 in most cancer–cohort settings. This is not necessarily surprising in your pipeline
because each modality is already represented by a learned latent embedding (fixed-
dimensional, standardized, and information-dense). When strong predictive signal is
already present in the latent space, a linear or tree-based classifier on concatenated
features can be highly effective, and may outperform structured tensor objectives that
impose additional constraints on interactions. In other words, concatenation can be
viewed as an upper baseline for pure predictive optimization when interpretability
constraints are not enforced.
When tensor structure helps (and what it buys you even when it does not
win F1).
Despite concatenation’s dominance, tensor-based strategies provide two advantages
that matter for the scientific aims of this work. First, they explicitly model multiway
interactions rather than treating modalities as an unordered feature bag, which can
be beneficial when prognostic signal is expressed through cross-modality coupling
(e.g., coordinated DNA–RNA–protein patterns) [19]. This is consistent with settings
where M2 or HIT-Fuse is competitive or best (e.g., Glioma E1 and LUAD E1 in
Table 5). Second, and more importantly for the paper’s contributions, the hierarchical
design induces a structured factorization aligned with biological flow (DNA →RNA →
protein/imaging), which supports coherent interpretation. In HIT-Fuse, each patient’s
meta-factor activations can be traced to a dominant biological layer via meta loadings
and then down to a specific modality branch (and ultimately decoded to ranked
drivers), yielding a reproducible chain of attribution. Flat concatenation does not
provide this decomposition natively, and single-stage tensor fusion (M2) does not
explicitly separate within-layer vs. cross-layer interactions, making such traceback less
direct and less biologically anchored.
Biological validity signals from enrichment and functional screens.
The enrichment fingerprint (Fig. 5A) indicates that driver exports capture both shared
oncogenic programs and cancer-specific signatures, and uses established functional
libraries to contextualize the decoded drivers. External validation using DepMap
23


CRISPR dependency profiles (Fig. 5B) further supports functional relevance: map-
ping drivers to CRISPR target genes and observing substantial essentiality rates
in cancer-matched cell lines is consistent with the Dependency Map paradigm for
identifying cancer vulnerabilities . The miRNA support via experimentally validated
targets (miRTarBase) provides complementary regulatory plausibility for miRNA-
linked drivers. While these validations do not prove causality, they strengthen the
claim that HIT-Fuse’s decoded drivers are not arbitrary latent artifacts but correspond
to experimentally interrogated functional units.
Limitations and practical takeaway.
The results suggest that no single fusion strategy universally dominates across cancers,
cohorts, and endpoints. If the sole objective were maximizing subtype F1, Concat_All
is often the strongest choice in this experimental design. However, the central goal of
HIT-Fuse is to balance competitive performance with biologically structured, mecha-
nistically interpretable fusion. Thus, the main takeaway is not that HIT-Fuse is always
the top classifier, but that it provides a principled representation that (i) remains sta-
ble under cohort completion, (ii) supports clinically meaningful stratification, and (iii)
enables patient-level, layer-resolved driver tracing with downstream biological valida-
tion (enrichment and DepMap), properties that are harder to obtain in a single step
from purely predictive baselines.
6 Conclusion
We introduced HIT-Fuse, a hierarchical tensor-based multimodal fusion framework
designed to integrate genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and imaging represen-
tations while preserving biologically motivated structure. Unlike flat concatenation
or single-stage tensor approaches, HIT-Fuse explicitly separates within-layer inter-
actions (DNA, RNA, protein–imaging) from cross-layer meta interactions, enabling
structured factorization aligned with biological flow.
Across four TCGA cancer cohorts and two cohort constructions (intersection
E1 and completed E2), HIT-Fuse demonstrated competitive subtype classification
performance and stable survival discrimination under cohort expansion. Although
direct feature concatenation frequently achieved the highest weighted F1, HIT-
Fuse remained consistently competitive while uniquely supporting layer-resolved,
patient-specific driver traceback. Cohort expansion through latent completion sub-
stantially increased sample coverage without destabilizing hierarchical rank selection
or degrading prognostic stratification.
Biological interpretability analysis further showed that HIT-Fuse-derived drivers
recapitulate known oncogenic processes across cancers and are enriched for experimen-
tally validated functional dependencies, including DepMap CRISPR gene essentiality
profiles and curated miRNA target interactions. These findings indicate that the hier-
archical representation captures biologically meaningful cross-modal structure rather
than arbitrary latent correlations.
Overall, this study highlights an important distinction between predictive opti-
mization and biologically structured integration. While concatenation may maximize
24


classification accuracy in certain settings, structured multimodal factorization pro-
vides a principled foundation for mechanistic interpretation and cross-modal reason-
ing. The proposed framework therefore offers a scalable and interpretable strategy for
multimodal cancer modeling in heterogeneous clinical cohorts.
