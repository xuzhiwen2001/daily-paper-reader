Abstract 
20 
Spatial omics technologies have revolutionized our ability to map transcriptomic, proteomic, or metabolomic profiles 
21 
within tissue contexts. However, existing spatial multi-omics approaches emphasize clustering or domain identification 
22 
from measured profiles, while a general framework for whole-slice mapping in measured and unmeasured areas at single-
23 
cell resolution across additional omics layers remains lacking. Here we introduce STEP, a computational framework that 
24 
integrates probabilistic inference with deep learning for flexible integration of histology, spatially resolved 
25 
transcriptomics (SRT), single-cell, and/or multi-omics data. We validated STEP’s advantages across diverse applications. 
26 
Furthermore, STEP enables tri-modal integration of Xenium, CITE-seq, and imaging mass cytometry to map 
27 
transcriptome-wide gene expression and protein profiles from a small gene panel. Through quadra-modal integration of 
28 
SRT, histology, single-nucleus RNA sequencing, and inCITE-seq, STEP reconstructs a 3D multi-omics brain cell atlas 
29 
from six low-resolution SRT sections. Collectively, STEP provides a cost-effective tool for exploring spatial biology by 
30 
integrating diverse, complementary modalities. 
31 
Introduction 
32 
Spatial omics technologies have transformed our understanding of biological systems by mapping transcriptomic, 
33 
proteomic, or metabolomic profiles in their native tissue context1–3. By preserving spatial information lost in non-spatial 
34 
assays, they reveal cellular organization, function, and interactions. An ideal goal is to efficiently construct whole-tissue 
35 
multi-omics maps across many sections at single-cell resolution. However, achieving this goal remains significantly 
36 
challenging4–6. 
37 
 
38 
Spatially resolved transcriptomics (SRT) has advanced fastest among spatial omics technologies, but current platforms 
39 
remain limited7–9. Early sequencing-based SRT provides transcriptome-wide coverage but lacks single-cell resolution 
40 
because each spot contains multiple cells8. Recent platforms achieve transcriptome-wide single-cell profiling10,11, yet 
41 
some suffer from molecular lateral diffusion12 and others cannot cover entire tissue sections13. These shortcomings hinder 
42 
the integration and joint analysis of such data with other spatial modalities, such as whole-slide spatially precise 
43 
proteomics data. Notably, although some platforms claim subcellular resolution, analyses often aggregate signals at the 
44 
cell level for robust interpretation (Supplementary Note 1). Imaging-based SRT offers single-cell resolution but requires

46 
single-cell transcriptomes without spatial context14. Recent advances in spatial multi-omics technologies offer a promising 
47 
route toward this ideal15–18, but achieving robust single-cell resolution remains challenging, and high costs and technical 
48 
complexity limit widespread adoption. Computational integration of complementary single-modality data thus offers a 
49

50 
 
51 
Existing computational approaches have primarily focused on the pairwise integration of these modalities. Gene 
52 
imputation combines imaging-based SRT with scRNA-seq for transcriptome-wide coverage20–22, while deconvolution 
53 
combines sequencing-based SRT with scRNA-seq to improve cell typing23–25. Other methods enhance SRT resolution to 
54 
the subspot level26, predict gene expression from hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) images at spot or pixel resolution27,28, or 
55 
infer cellular composition and expression from SRT data29,30. Many of these approaches do not fully exploit 
56 
complementary information across modalities, limiting comprehensive multi-modal inference. Multi-omic spatial-domain 
57 
identification methods31,32 improve niche discovery, but do not infer missing modalities or generate whole-slice single-cell 
58 
maps. Recent tri-modal efforts have demonstrated single-cell spatial multi-omics33 but often omit histology, a valuable 
59 
source of morphological context. OmiCLIP34 partially addresses this gap by aligning histology with SRT through CLIP-
60 
based contrastive learning35, but remains limited to patch-level bi-modal integration without single-cell resolution or 
61 
multi-omics fusion. 
62 
 
63 
We developed STEP, a hybrid computational framework that integrates probabilistic inference with deep learning. 
64 
Building on methods that jointly consider histology, SRT, single-cell, and/or multi-omics data36,37, STEP provides a 
65 
generalizable, platform-agnostic framework for diverse spatial multi-omics integration scenarios. By combining 
66 
morphology with spatial omics information, STEP links SRT measurements to individual cells and supports both 
67 
sequencing- and imaging-based SRT. For sequencing-based SRT, STEP extends single-cell mapping from the sequenced 
68 
area to the whole tissue section; for imaging-based SRT, it expands gene coverage from a few hundred genes to the whole 
69 
transcriptome. In a tri-modal integration of Xenium, CITE-seq, and imaging mass cytometry (IMC) data, it infers 
70 
transcriptome-wide gene expression and protein profiles in human breast cancer at single-cell resolution. In quadra-modal

Results  
76 
1. Overview of the STEP workflow 
77 
STEP is a hyper-modal computational framework that supports flexible integration of diverse spatially resolved 
78 
transcriptomics (SRT) data with single-cell reference data to generate whole-slice maps at single-cell resolution with 
79 
expanded omics layers (Fig. 1). It accommodates sequencing- and imaging-based SRT, optional histological features, and 
80 
transcriptomic or multi-omic references (Fig. 1a–c). Depending on the input settings, STEP generates whole-
81 
transcriptomic or multi-omic maps at single-cell resolution across the whole slice (Fig. 1d).  
82 
 
83 
The STEP workflow comprises Data Pre-processing and three core modules: Cell Identification, Spatial Diffusion, and 
84 
Omics Enhancement (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Notes 2–9 and Methods). Data Pre-processing performs specific operations 
85 
on different data inputs, including cell segmentation38, optional morphological feature extraction from histology, 
86 
informative gene selection39, and batch/platform effect removal using an adjusted conditional variational autoencoder 
87 
(CVAE)40 (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Cell Identification uses probabilistic inference to assign cell types to segmented 
88 
cells within sequenced areas by linking cell types, gene expression, and optional morphological features (Supplementary 
89 
Fig. 3). Spatial Diffusion uses a graph attention network (GAT)41 to propagate information to unsequenced areas, inferring 
90 
cell types and informative gene expression across the whole slice (Supplementary Fig. 4). Omics Enhancement then uses 
91 
scRNA-seq or single-cell multi-omics references to infer whole-transcriptome or multi-omic profiles, respectively 
92 
(Supplementary Fig. 5). 
93 
 
94 
STEP supports multi-modal integration by dynamically adjusting its module usage to accommodate specific modality 
95 
combinations, including bi-modal, tri-modal and quadra-modal configurations (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Figs. 6–9). For 
96

2. Histological cellular features guide single-cell mapping 
03 
Human Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is widely studied to understand cellular heterogeneity and the tumor 
04 
microenvironment. We collected data from two PDAC tissue slices24,42, PDAC-A and PDAC-B, generated by Spatial 
05 
Transcriptomics (spot-level resolution, whole-transcriptome coverage, and matched H&E images), together with InDrop 
06 
scRNA-seq profiles as the single-cell reference, to evaluate STEP’s performance in cell-type identification and gene 
07 
expression prediction at single-cell resolution (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 10, Supplementary Note 14 and Methods).  
08 
 
09 
Since accurate cell-type identification at the single-cell level is crucial for analyses of SRT data, we benchmarked 
10 
methods designed for similar tasks on the PDAC data, which fall into two categories: spot-level cell type deconvolution 
11 
methods including SpaDecon43, CellDART23, CARD24, and Cell2location25, which predict the cell type proportions within 
12 
each spatial spot, and single-cell-level cell type inference methods including STEP, STIE30, and SpatialScope29, which 
13 
infer the cell type of each segmented cell (Methods). Following the benchmarking procedure described in Methods, we 
14 
evaluated cell identification using tissue-region annotations and the known regional enrichment of cell types, with 
15 
separate comparisons against spot-level deconvolution and single-cell-level inference methods. STEP achieved higher 
16 
performance across multiple evaluation metrics in both comparisons (Fig. 2b,c, Supplementary Figs. 10–14 and 
17 
Supplementary Notes 15 and 16).

Since STEP not only identifies cell types at the single-cell level but also predicts gene expression at single-cell resolution, 
48 
we further benchmarked its performance in super-resolution gene expression prediction against BayesSpace, SpatialScope, 
49 
iStar, and XFuse (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22). Among these methods, BayesSpace26 primarily subdivides 
50 
spots into multiple sub-spots, while SpatialScope is limited to predicting gene expression of individual cells within a 
51 
given spot (Supplementary Fig. 22). iStar’s27 results lack cell-level features and suffer from significant blurring artifacts, 
52 
while XFuse28 can identify cell outlines but has low accuracy in gene prediction (Fig. 2g). This blurring presumably stems 
53 
from their image-to-gene regression frameworks that average signals across entire patches without explicit cell-instance 
54 
constraints (Fig. 2g). STEP addresses these limitations by providing fine-grained predictions of gene expression at the 
55 
single-cell level across the whole slice (Fig. 2f). This characteristic allows for more comprehensive spatial mapping of 
56 
biomarkers, facilitating the exploration of complex cellular microenvironments.  
57 
 
58 
We further assessed the spatial enrichment of representative marker genes and their correspondence with predicted cell 
59 
types, supporting the spatial and cell-type consistency of STEP’s gene expression predictions (Fig. 2h,i and 
60 
Supplementary Note 17).

63 
human breast cancer and human colorectal cancer. These supplementary benchmarks enabled direct comparison against 
64 
the original Xenium profiles and annotations, and showed that STEP maintained improvement in both cell identification 
65 
and gene prediction across additional cancer contexts (Supplementary Figs. 23–29).

Next, we evaluated STEP’s robustness to interventions of the single-cell reference. We first performed a comparative 
85

4. Revealing tumor heterogeneity in unsequenced tissue 
02 
While tumor tissues can be analyzed with SRT, the field of view covered by current platforms is often limited, leaving 
03 
substantial regions with only histological images. Inferring gene expression in these unsequenced areas from sequenced 
04 
areas is therefore important, as it enables more accurate modeling of tumor heterogeneity and microenvironmental 
05 
interactions, which are critical for understanding cancer progression and therapeutic resistance. 
06 
 
07 
Using spot masking and transfer experiments on human breast cancer sections, we evaluated STEP’s ability to reconstruct 
08 
spatial profiles in unsequenced tissue. STEP maintained cell identification and gene expression prediction under reduced 
09 
spatial sampling and enabled reconstruction across adjacent sections and different patients (Fig. 4a–g, Supplementary 
10 
Figs. 48–60 and Supplementary Notes 21 and 22). 
11 
 
12 
We next leveraged these high-resolution expression maps to dissect the molecular heterogeneity of breast cancer subtypes. 
13 
By analyzing STEP-predicted expression levels of key markers including estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), progesterone 
14 
receptor (PGR), and ERBB255, we classified the tumor regions into distinct molecular subtypes (Fig. 4i, Supplementary 
15 
Fig. 61 and Supplementary Note 23). The analysis revealed inter-tumor heterogeneity: BRCA-A was dominated by 
16 
Luminal B and HER2-enriched subtypes, whereas BRCA-B1 and BRCA-B2 exhibited aggressive Triple-Negative Breast 
17 
Cancer (TNBC) regions alongside Luminal B cells50,56,57. Given that TNBC is associated with poor prognosis58, STEP’s 
18 
ability to spatially resolve these aggressive subclones within a heterogeneous tumor mass provides critical insights. 
19

invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) and non-invasive lesions, including ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and lobular

5. Application across diverse SRT platforms 
29 
Existing SRT techniques can be broadly categorized into two major classes: sequencing-based and imaging-based 
30 
platforms. Many sequencing-based SRT platforms have relatively low spatial resolution, such as 10x Visium. In contrast, 
31 
imaging-based SRT platforms, including MERFISH60 and osmFISH61, offer single-cell resolution but are restricted to a 
32 
limited gene panel. Additionally, the sequencing area of many platforms is limited, such that only a subset of large tissue 
33 
sections is typically profiled. In this context, STEP compensates for the limitations of diverse SRT techniques, providing a 
34 
solid foundation for an array of downstream analyses (Supplementary Notes 24–26).  
35 
 
36 
For sequencing-based spatially resolved transcriptomics, we evaluated STEP using a mouse cortex dataset derived from 
37 
two serial sections generated by 10x Visium with spot-level resolution, whole-transcriptome coverage, and matched H&E 
38 
images29,62. Following the workflow in Fig. 5a, we performed cell identification and gene prediction on section 2 (Fig. 5b). 
39 
To further assess STEP’s predictive capability in another species under limited tissue coverage, we applied a well-trained 
40 
STEP model from Section 2 to an H&E-only Section 1. Remarkably, despite using only this partial dataset, STEP 
41 
demonstrated superior performance in identifying cell types and predicting gene expression, successfully revealing 
42 
distinct layer-specific distribution patterns within the cortex (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Figs. 64–67). 
43 
 
44

46

47 
cell on the tissue slice (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Figs. 68–80). Specifically, for both 10x Visium and MERFISH data,

6. Tri-modal integration for single-cell spatial multi-omics 
79 
To further investigate the capability of STEP in multi-omics inference for imaging-based spatially resolved 
80 
transcriptomics, we performed a tri-modal integration of Xenium52, CITE-seq50, and imaging mass cytometry (IMC)68 data 
81 
from human breast cancer (Supplementary Fig. 85). These modalities were deliberately selected for their complementary 
82 
strengths: Xenium provides high-resolution single-cell information with spatial localization; CITE-seq offers paired 
83 
single-cell transcriptome and surface proteome profiles, bridging the gap between RNA and protein; and IMC captures 
84 
highly multiplexed protein expression. By integrating these modalities through STEP (Fig. 6a), we minimized information 
85 
redundancy while enhancing cross-modality consistency. Consequently, STEP enables the construction of a single-cell 
86 
spatial multi-omics map that provides a more comprehensive and biologically interpretable representation of breast cancer 
87 
compared to any single modality alone. 
88 
 
89 
Using the proteomic integration result and Xenium cell-type annotations as labels, the UMAP visualization clearly 
90 
showed the differences in protein expression between cancer cells and normal cells (Fig. 6b). Due to the tissue of the IMC 
91 
reference not containing DCIS-type subclones, it can be observed that the model showed a mixture for cancer cell 
92 
(DCIS_1, DCIS_2, invasive tumor) in UMAP (Fig. 6b), unlike normal cell types. More similar physiological conditions 
93 
among the tissues of the referenced multi-omics would improve the model's ability to distinguish different cancer 
94 
subclones. We visualized the proteomic integration result for different cell types using heatmaps and found that CK5 
95 
(cytokeratin 5, a marker for basal epithelial cells) was enriched in myoepithelial cells (4.70-fold,  < 0.0001), whereas 
96 
CD15 (a carbohydrate antigen involved in cell adhesion and signaling) was enriched in all tumor categories (2.65-fold,  
97 
< 0.0001) (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 86). These results were consistent with existing research conclusion69,70.

12

13

14

15

16

Discussion  
65 
Spatial omics reveals cellular organization and interactions within tissues, but comprehensive multi-omics mapping at 
66 
single-cell resolution remains challenging. To address this challenge, we developed STEP, a hybrid framework combining 
67 
probabilistic inference and deep learning to integrate SRT, histology and single-cell reference data. It extends sequencing-
68 
based SRT to whole-section profiling at single-cell resolution and expands imaging-based SRT to whole-transcriptome 
69 
coverage. Benchmarking of STEP’s basic functionality on PDAC and BRCA data demonstrated improved performance 
70 
over existing methods. Through tri-modal integration of Xenium, CITE-seq and IMC, STEP mapped gene and protein 
71 
expression in breast cancer at single-cell resolution. Quadra-modal integration further enabled reconstruction of a high-
72 
resolution 3D brain multi-omics atlas using gene expression data from only six sections for training, supporting cost-
73 
efficient spatial multi-omics profiling.

Although STEP demonstrates strong performance in multi-modal integration, several aspects could be further improved. 
76 
Refining the distance-based assumptions of local consistency and smoothness in its omics enhancement strategy could 
77 
help STEP better capture tissue heterogeneity. Developing computationally efficient generative models offers a promising 
78 
direction for improving omics enhancement in large-scale applications. As spatial omics technologies advance, 
79 
incorporating additional omics layers, such as epigenomics and metabolomics, could further expand STEP’s molecular 
80 
coverage. Combining these measurements with computational inference could help address the resolution–throughput 
81 
trade-off2 and advance the study of complex tissues in basic biology and precision medicine. 
82 
 
83 
Gene–protein correspondence further highlights the limits of cross-modality inference. Variation in transcript–protein 
84 
concordance across proteins and cell types may reflect both biological regulation and uncertainty in protein inference. 
85 
Among immune-related proteins, activation markers and secreted molecules can undergo extensive post-transcriptional 
86 
and post-translational regulation, whereas some lineage-defining receptors may exhibit stronger transcript–protein 
87 
concordance. Transcript-based protein inference therefore captures only part of the regulatory hierarchy shaping cellular 
88 
phenotypes. Incorporating additional regulatory dimensions, such as RNA velocity80, translational control, and 
89 
proteostatic dynamics, could improve the biological realism of STEP and facilitate the study of context-dependent 
90 
transcript–protein divergence across tissues. 
91 
 
92 
Finally, STEP provides a framework for multi-modal integration that could support large-scale efforts such as the Human 
93 
Cell Atlas and the BRAIN Initiative. By linking experimental measurements with computational inference, STEP may 
94 
help reduce data acquisition requirements while revealing molecular relationships beyond individual modalities. Such 
95 
integration could facilitate biological discovery and future applications in precision medicine.

Benchmarking Comparison 
36 
Inspired by CARD24, for the PDAC and BRCA-A datasets, where cells lack true annotations, we use the actual region 
37 
label based on H&E staining to evaluate the metrics. The true region label is assigned according to the cell’s location. The 
38 
predicted labels may vary between datasets. For example, in PDAC-A,  Cancer clone A and Cancer clone B cells are 
39 
typically enriched in the Cancer region, so their predicted labels are updated to “Cancer”. Similarly, in BRCA-A, Cancer 
40 
Epithelial cells are predominantly found in the Invasive Carcinoma region, leading to their predicted labels being adjusted 
41 
to “Invasive Carcinoma”. Finally, we compute evaluation metrics such as ARI, ACC, NMI, and F1-Score using the true 
42 
and predicted labels. The methods compared, their software links and parameter settings are summarized in 
43 
Supplementary Table 5. 
44 
Visium-like pseudo-spots were generated by spatially aggregating single-cell Xenium profiles from human breast and 
45 
colorectal cancer tissues. Using the original Xenium annotations and expression profiles as ground truth, we evaluated 
46 
cell-type deconvolution with MSE and PCC, cell identification with accuracy, and gene prediction with PCC and SPCC. 
47 
The colorectal cancer benchmark was also performed using an unpaired scRNA-seq reference.

Transferring  
50 
The adaptability of GAT to dynamic graph structures enables inference of graphs with changing topologies. This 
51 
characteristic facilitates the application of Spatial Diffusion, allowing a model trained on a specific tissue section to be 
52 
seamlessly transferred and employed across different sections of the same tissue type. Specifically, for another tissue 
53 
section, a    adjacency matrix W is constructed, where  represents the number of cells in the section. By applying 
54 
W and the corresponding morphological feature matrix  to the trained model. Consequently, this approach achieves cell 
55 
type identification across diverse spatial regions of the tissue.

66 
This study is a computational reanalysis of previously published, publicly available spatial omics and single-cell datasets 
67 
described in Supplementary Note 14 and the Data availability section; no new wet-lab experiments were performed. 
68

70

71

72
