# Abstract 

Identifying the drug and dose that produced a cellular response requires preserving treat-ment effects within each cellular background. A joint-embedding predictor can match its targets while ignoring the intervention if its encoder preserves differences across back-grounds but erases differences between treated and control populations within each one. We call this failure interventional contrast collapse . PerturbJEPA encodes the difference in mean gene expression between treated and control populations as an effect vector. It aligns the displacement between their latent centroids with this vector and regularizes the vectors to preserve variation across population pairs. We show that the measured displacements can-not all coincide within a batch when effect-vector variation exceeds the root-mean-square alignment error. Trained with separate drug and dose ranking objectives, the model predicts candidate responses from a common control population and matches them to the observed response without cell pairing or expression reconstruction. We evaluate 3,592 Tahoe-100M queries on held-out combinations of known interventions and cellular backgrounds, each with over 700 drug–dose candidates in a fixed, background-specific catalog. PerturbJEPA achieves 91.46% macro joint drug–dose Recall@1, exceeding STATE by 3.40 percentage points (95% drug-cluster bootstrap interval of 1.72 to 5.07 points). Controlled diagnostics show that treatment–control differences can shrink even when representations remain glob-ally dispersed. With the drug ranking objective fixed, adding effect-vector regularization to alignment raises joint Recall@1 from 18.57% to 87.47%.

1arXiv:2611.20575v1 [cs.CV] 16 Nov 2026 1 Introduction 

Recovering the drug and dose responsible for an observed cellular response requires distinguishing intervention ef-fects within the same cellular background. We study 

source-intervention retrieval : a forward model predicts each candidate response from a common control population and retrieves the closest match to an observed target from a catalog containing the true intervention. This extends transcriptional signature matching [11, 16] by conditioning predictions on the observed control cells. Destructive single-cell measurements preclude tracking indi-vidual cells before and after treatment, requiring prediction between unpaired populations. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn to predict target representa-tions without reconstructing observations [2]. Yet accurate prediction can become uninformative for retrieval if the representation discards treatment effects while preserving larger background variation. 

Global representation diversity is insufficient. 

Marginal regularizers discourage a constant representation by maintaining variation across examples [3, 14]. Yet an encoder can preserve differences between cellular backgrounds while assigning the same representation distribution to control and treated populations within each background. We call this failure interventional contrast collapse (ICC; Fig. 1). Under ICC, returning the control representation can match the target distribution. If the predictor does so for two doses of the same drug, their scores tie. The cellular background is identical for both candidates. 

Effect preservation in retrieval geometry. Although each cell is measured only once, the treated and control means provide a measurable expression contrast without cell pairing. Pseudobulk-to-latent effect alignment (PEA) uses this contrast to supervise retrieval geometry (Fig. 2(b)). It aligns the scaled displacement between the two latent cen-troids with a learned embedding of the expression contrast. An auxiliary head could amplify a small displacement to predict the same target, even if the centroids remain close. Direct alignment ties the target to the displacement itself. Regularizing the effect target is essential: alignment alone allows the target and displacement to shrink together. A finite-batch bound relates effect-target variation and alignment error to measured displacement variation across population pairs. Controlled diagnostics assess treatment– control contrast during training, and separate drug and dose ranking losses train candidate discrimination. Population energy discrepancy (PED) additionally matches distributional structure and scores candidates at inference, when the effect branch is removed. We evaluate on 3,592 Tahoe-100M queries [21] with held-out combinations of known interventions and cellular backgrounds, each ranking over 700 drug–dose candidates. PerturbJEPA reaches 91.46% macro joint Recall@1, exceed-ing STATE by 3.40 percentage points. # 2 Related work 

Predictive representations and selective feature loss. V-JEPA 2 learns predictive representations without observation reconstruction and supports action-conditioned prediction and planning [2]. LeJEPA [3] introduces SIGReg to en-courage an isotropic Gaussian embedding distribution, while LeWorldModel [14] combines distributional regularization with end-to-end latent dynamics learning. These objec-tives address global representation collapse, but preserving marginal diversity does not ensure that every relevant factor survives. Sobal et al. [15] show that predictive embeddings can favor persistent backgrounds over changing features. Our ICC formulation examines this distinction for conditional population responses: background variation can remain while matched treatment–control contrasts disappear. 

Learning change through difference targets. Concurrent work MotionJEPA [9] learns temporal visual dynamics from consecutive images and evaluates downstream planning. Its DISReg objective predicts temporal-difference image embed-dings from paired state embeddings, regularizing both to pe-nalize constant targets. Population response prediction. scGen represents pertur-bation effects through differences between latent population means [12]; CPA composes basal states, perturbations, and covariates [13]; and CellOT learns mappings between unpaired populations using optimal transport [4]. STATE combines cell-state representations with conditional transi-tions and decodes predicted states into expression [1]. We shape the predicted embedding space for direct response matching, comparing candidate predictions with the observed target in that space. 

Cellular JEPA models. Cell-JEPA combines masked-view latent prediction with expression reconstruction, then uses perturbation-label embeddings and a perturbed-state JEPA objective for response prediction [6]. Its distinction between absolute-state prediction and effect-size estimation motivates assessing response information separately from state quality. BioJEPA-AC also conditions latent prediction on genetic and chemical interventions [8]. JEPA-Anything predicts latent treatment–control residuals through fac-torized pathways and evaluates decoded expression [5]. Building on intervention-conditioned latent prediction, we focus on explicit population-displacement constraints and response-based candidate ranking. 

Response-based intervention retrieval. The Connectivity Map and L1000 connect interventions through observed ex-pression signatures [11, 16]. GeneSpeak-FP learns compound and target retrieval from observed cell-level responses relative to control, using a closed candidate library and held-out drug– cell-line groups [18]. PerturbJEPA instead predicts each can-3didate response from a common control population and com-pares these predictions with an observed target. This forward-model interface supports a shared retrieval task across sys-tems with different output spaces. Complementing Systema’s emphasis on perturbation-specific effects beyond systematic variation [19], our evaluation tests whether predictions distin-guish competing interventions within a common context.

Figure 2 summarizes the model. Population prediction, effect preservation, and action discrimination impose complementary requirements on its learned representation.

# 4 Intervention retrieval experiments 

Evaluation protocol. We evaluate source-intervention recovery on Tahoe-100M [21] using 3,592 held-out drug– dose–background queries spanning five cell lines. Each query contains 256 distinct treated cells and 256 matched control cells with the same plate composition. We exclude 

81 test groups with fewer than 256 treated cells; their actions can remain candidates. Each query ranks its complete background-specific catalog of 734 or 735 drug–dose actions from 268 drugs. The split holds out combinations of known interventions and cellular backgrounds; it does not test unseen drugs or unseen cell lines. All methods receive the same frozen cells and candidate catalogs, with no test-time parameter updates. Joint R@1 measures recovery of the exact source drug– dose action. Drug R@1 ranks each drug by its best-scoring available dose. Conditional dose accuracy selects among the true drug’s doses for every query, regardless of drug-ranking accuracy. We use a hierarchical macro average: doses within drugs, drugs within backgrounds, and then the five backgrounds with equal weight. Recall and accuracy are percentages; MRR is multiplied by 100 . ## 4.1 Controlled contrast diagnostics 

Figure 3 isolates representation behavior before ranking supervision. The state-only controls apply SIGReg across population pairs at each cell slot, across pooled population– cell slots, or separately within each population. With 60 

population pairs and 256 cells, their respective sample counts are 60 , 15 ,360 , and 256 ; all use the same sample-count-normalized penalty. This comparison tests whether changing the axis of state regularization preserves intervention con-trasts while PED remains fixed. The alignment-only and complete-PEA configurations instead retain population-axis SMI, isolating the added effect constraints. PED alone collapses globally, whereas all three state-regularization axes retain scale near one while treatment–control contrasts contract. Alignment alone also leaves much smaller contrasts than complete PEA. Over updates 9,001–10,000, complete PEA gives mean energy and centroid contrasts of 0.0202 

and 0.1599 , compared with 0.00402 and 0.0562 for SMI alone, at comparable global scales ( 0.9697 and 0.9737 ). These trajectories measure treatment–control separation. Two interventions can induce equally large shifts, so held-out retrieval tests whether the representation also distinguishes their responses.

# 5 Conclusion and limitations 

The controlled trajectories show that an encoder can retain global diversity as the distance between treated and control populations shrinks. PerturbJEPA uses the change in mean expression to supervise the displacement between population centroids. The finite-batch bound relates variation in the ef-fect targets to variation in these displacements when align-ment error is small. Both learned heads predict the same type of effect target, yet joint recall is highest when the loss acts directly on the centroid displacement in the space used for retrieval. Drug and dose ranking train the predictor to dis-tinguish candidate responses from a common control. To-gether these objectives yield 91 .46% macro joint Recall@1 on Tahoe-100M, compared with 88 .06% for STATE. Two treatments can produce the same mean shift with dif-ferent mixtures of responding cells. They then supply the same target to PEA. Distinguishing them depends on whether the full population embeddings retain the distributional differ-ence scored by PED. Evaluation holds out combinations of known drugs and cellular backgrounds. ## C.7 Interpretation of the finite-batch results 

The guarantees concern measured first-order contrasts and their variation over a realized batch. A nonzero expected centroid contrast δθ (c, a ) rules out equality of the corre-sponding population laws. Sampling can produce a nonzero realized ∆zb even under equal laws. Two responses with the same mean-expression shift supply the same target g(∆ x) to PEA. Differences in variance or cell-state mixtures can affect PED when the population embeddings retain them. The batch-level constraint also permits zero effects for individual interventions and shared effects across actions. For example, all actions within a context could share a nonzero shift h(c)

while these shifts vary across contexts. Such displacements can exhibit batch-level variation and accurate PEA alignment yet carry no within-context drug identity. IDR supplies the separate source-label objective for within-context candidate discrimination. The effect encoder in Equation (17) has a 128 -dimensional hidden layer and a 512 -dimensional output. Thus every effect embedding lies in the image of W2, a subspace of dimension at most 128 , and its covariance cannot equal I512 . The finite projected ESI penalty regularizes the available subspace through its sampled directions. Propositions 3 and 4 remain valid under this bottleneck. The estimated expression difference ∆xb contains sam-pling variation and may contain residual experimental con-founding. The learned map g can compress these differences, so biological fidelity requires evidence about which directions it retains. The analysis establishes the geometric role of effect regularization and alignment; the retrieval experiments assess their utility for source discrimination.

## D.4 Full input-gene-universe comparison 

Matched recipe and input dimensions. The complete HVG2000 and all-gene models use the same six loss weights, model width 512, encoder depth eight, predictor depth four, 

ℓ2 pooling, and training settings from Section A. Both use 16 workers and 15 population pairs per worker, giving 240 pairs per update. The input gene universe expands from 2,000 HVGs to all 62,710 genes. Both use the full-library CP10K log-expression preprocessing in Section A; the all-gene model omits HVG selection. The effect-input projection grows from 128 × 2,000 to 128 × 62 ,710 , and the gene-embedding vocabulary expands to the same input universe. These matrices increase parameter count, so the comparison varies gene coverage and input-layer capacity together while keeping hidden dimensions and layer counts fixed. Prediction and retrieval continue to operate in embedding space. 

Retrieval gains and dose trade-off. Table 10 reports all paired differences and intervals from this comparison. All-gene input improves joint Recall@1 by 3.04 percentage points, joint MRR by 2.38 units, and drug Recall@1 by 4.56 points, while reducing conditional dose accuracy by 1.60 points. Mean absolute log 10 dose error increases by 0.0207 ,with paired interval [0 .0109 , 0.0311] . The two models reach nearly identical joint Recall@50 (99 .69% for all genes and 99 .71% for HVG2000). The results support improved source-drug recovery and front-of-list joint retrieval for this configuration, with weaker conditional dose discrimination.

## D.6 Retrieval-distance sensitivity at fixed checkpoints 

Comparison design. The 2 × 2 experiment crosses energy or Sinkhorn training with energy or Sinkhorn test scoring. Both training recipes retain SMI, complete PEA, and drug IDR with dose IDR disabled. For each recipe, we select a checkpoint using energy scores on the validation panel and evaluate both test scorers at that checkpoint. 

Fixed scoring profiles. The energy profile is the raw-coordinate retrieval scorer in Section A. The Sinkhorn profile uses the training divergence and coordinate scaling in Section D.2, with float32 arithmetic, autocast disabled, and final divergences clamped at zero. Both profiles evaluate full 256-cell predicted and target populations with identical query cells, candidate inputs, model evaluation mode, and tie-breaking rules. All Sinkhorn scores are finite and positive, with no top-one ties. 

Paired comparisons. The bootstrap uses identical sampled drug weights across all four evaluations.
