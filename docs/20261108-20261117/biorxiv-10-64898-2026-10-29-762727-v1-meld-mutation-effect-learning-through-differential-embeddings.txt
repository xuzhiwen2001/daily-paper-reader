Abstract. We introduce MELD (Mutation Effect Learning through Differential Embeddings), a compact model for predicting the functional consequences of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) by training directly on the wild-type (WT) versus mutant differential. Interpreting SNVs is central to clinical genomics, yet experimental characterization cannot scale to millions of observed variants. Leading sequence-to-function models (Enformer, Borzoi, AlphaGenome) are trained on absolute genomic tracks and score variants post hoc through in-silico mutagenesis (ISM), so the WT-mutant difference never enters the training loss. MELD instead places the logit difference ∆z = zmut − zwt inside the supervised objective. It combines a shared CNN-Transformer encoder, a mutant-specific mutation-mask boost, multiscale mutation-centered pooling, and a learned adaptive amplification factor α. On ClinVar (1.57M variants), MELD (1.9M parameters) reached an AUROC of 0.986 versus 0.800 for a same-data, same-backbone ISM baseline, with ablations confirming the contribution of each component. On a shared benchmark, it outperformed frozen foundation models with 3-1,300 x more parameters (AUROC 0.967 vs. 0.924 for AlphaGenome and 0.936 for Nucleotide Transformer). With only 50 , 000 training variants, it exceeded the ISM baseline trained on all 1.48M (AUROC 0.903 vs. 0.796 ) variants. On BRCA1 saturation genome editing, frozen MELD achieved a Spearman ρ of 0.42 , above Borzoi ( 0.40 ), and ClinVar transfer raised it to 

0.45 . These results demonstrate that direct differential supervision is a compact, data-efficient alternative to post-hoc ISM scoring with substantially larger pretrained models.

1 Introduction 

Determining how genetic variation produces functional change is a central problem in genomics and a prerequisite for interpreting the clinical significance of the human variome. 1–4 Single nu-cleotide variants (SNVs) can alter gene regulation, chromatin accessibility, and transcriptional output, yet their effects are typically small, strongly context dependent, and difficult to measure at scale. 5–8 Sequencing has made variant discovery routine, but exhaustive experimental charac-terization of variant function remains infeasible on cost and throughput grounds, leaving the large majority of observed variants functionally uncharacterized. 2, 9–12 

Sequence-to-function foundation models 7, 13–20 have become the dominant computational re-sponse to close this gap. Methods such as Enformer, 19 Borzoi, 20 and AlphaGenome 7 map long genomic windows, spanning tens of thousands to over a million base pairs, to thousands of epige-nomic and transcriptional tracks, and they achieve state-of-the-art accuracy on absolute signal pre-diction. Variant effects are then obtained after training through in-silico mutagenesis (ISM) where the reference and alternate sequences are scored independently and their difference is taken as the predicted effect. A parallel family of self-supervised DNA language models produces general-purpose sequence representations that are transferred to variant scoring in the same post-hoc fash-ion, by comparing wild-type and mutant embeddings. 21–26

This design carries a structural limitation. The training objective is defined over absolute signal, so the wild-type versus mutant difference that actually defines a variant effect never enters the loss optimization. Sensitivity to nucleotide-level perturbation is acquired only indirectly, as a byproduct of accurate absolute prediction. The documented consequences are attenuated response to low-effect variants, unreliable direction-of-effect estimates, and substantial inference cost from the repeated large-model forward passes that ISM requires. Across these backbones, variant interpretation follows a common recipe: a pretrained or task-trained model produces a sequence representation, and the wild-type versus mutant differential is formed over a backbone that was never optimized to encode it. The families differ in what representation is available. Sequence-to-function models 7, 19 expose predicted assay tracks, so we form the differential over those tracks, while DNA language models 22–24 expose only learned trunk embeddings, so we use a light-weight prediction head (one output variable) over those. We hold the evaluation scaffold fixed across all backbones: a frozen encoder, a lightweight paired head over the wild-type and mutant representations, and identical training, split, and selection. Under this protocol the reported score is a supervised classifier probability (classification) or predicted effect score (regression), not an in-silico-mutagenesis contrast and not a zero-shot likelihood ratio. The representation type therefore varies by model class, which we state explicitly, while the head, data, and selection are matched. Here we introduce MELD (Mutation Effect Learning through Differential Embeddings), a sequence-to-function model that places the wild-type versus mutant differential directly inside the supervised objective. A shared CNN-Transformer encoder processes matched reference and mutant windows, and the paired logit difference ∆z = zmut − zwt becomes the quantity the model is trained to predict on. A mutation mask marks the variant position and applies a learned boost to the mutant branch at that location, making the encoder sensitive to the altered site while retain-ing the surrounding sequence context. Wild-type and mutant representations are then pooled at a global scale and at increasingly local mutation-centered neighborhoods, so the differential is read out across multiple context scales rather than at a single resolution. A learned per-scale factor α

provides context-dependent amplification of each scale-specific difference. Variant effect, thereby, becomes a quantity the model predicts directly rather than one recovered post hoc. In contrast to the frozen-transfer-based baselines, the representation itself is shaped to make wild-type versus mutant separation available, and we show this can be achieved with a compact encoder rather than a large pretrained foundation model. An overview of the classification and regression formulations is shown in Figure (1). We evaluate MELD on ClinVar variant pathogenicity classification and on BRCA1 saturation genome editing (SGE) regression under matched data and backbone conditions. On the Clin-Var held-out test set, MELD reaches an AUROC of 0.986, compared with 0.800 for a same-data, same-backbone ISM baseline that scores variants post-hoc, supporting the advantage of the paired MELD formulation over this baseline. With roughly 1.9 million parameters, MELD outperforms frozen pretrained foundation models with 3-1,300x more parameters when their representations are transferred to the same benchmark. The advantage holds across supervised-data regimes spanning ten thousand to more than a million training variants, and it extends from binary pathogenicity to continuous experimental effect. On BRCA1 SGE under a matched frozen-representation protocol, MELD attains a Spearman correlation ( ρ) of 0.42, exceeding the strongest external encoder (Bor-zoi 20 ), and end-to-end transfer from ClinVar pathogenicity predictor raises the ρ to 0.45. 2 Related Work 

Computational variant effect prediction has been approached through two broad families of se-quence models. The first family learns to predict experimental functional-genomics measure-ments directly from DNA, treating variant effect as a downstream consequence of an accurate sequence-to-function map. The second family pretrains large models on genomic sequence in a self-supervised fashion, producing general-purpose representations that transfer to many tasks. In both families, a variant is typically scored post hoc , by comparing model outputs or representations for the wild-type and mutant sequences after the backbone has been trained. 

2.1 Sequence-to-function regression models 

These models predict experimental coverage tracks directly from sequence. In their original for-mulations, variant effects are recovered through in-silico mutagenesis: the trained model is applied to the reference and alternate alleles and the resulting predicted tracks are contrasted, with no ad-ditional supervised head. We provide details about such methods below: 

Enformer: 19 Enformer pairs a convolutional tower with transformer layers, using self-attention to extend its effective receptive field well beyond earlier convolutional architectures. 19 It takes an input window of roughly 200 kb and predicts thousands of human and mouse tracks, spanning CAGE, DNase/ATAC, and histone- and TF-ChIP, at 128 bp resolution, trained as a multitask re-gression against experimental coverage. By improving gene-expression prediction from sequence, it yields, as a downstream consequence, more accurate variant-effect predictions, obtained by con-trasting model outputs for the reference and alternate alleles. 

Borzoi: 20 Borzoi extends this paradigm from summary expression values to full RNA-seq cover-age profiles, predicting cell-type- and tissue-specific coverage directly from sequence. 20 It adapts a convolutional and transformer backbone with a U-net-style upsampling path and processes a 

∼524 kb context, substantially longer than Enformer’s, allowing it to score variant effects across transcription, splicing, and polyadenylation rather than a single expression summary. Borzoi is evaluated on quantitative trait loci and is competitive with and often surpasses models trained on individual regulatory functions. 

AlphaGenome: 7 AlphaGenome unifies and scales this direction further, taking up to 1 Mb of DNA as input and predicting thousands of functional-genomics tracks at single-base-pair resolu-tion across many regulatory modalities, including gene expression, chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, transcription-factor binding, contact maps, and splicing. 7 Its U-Net-style architec-ture downsamples the sequence through a convolutional encoder, exchanges information across positions with transformer layers, and upsamples back to base resolution before assay-specific out-put heads. It is trained in a supervised regime to predict experimental tracks and reports matching or exceeding the strongest external models on the large majority of its variant-effect benchmarks. 4

2.2 Self-supervised DNA language models 

These models are pretrained with self-supervised objectives i.e. masked or autoregressive language modeling and have no task-specific supervision during pretraining; their representations transfer to downstream prediction either by fine-tuning or by training lightweight heads on frozen features. 

Nucleotide Transformer: 21 The Nucleotide Transformer (NT) family spans 50 M to 2.5B pa-rameters and is trained on progressively broader corpora, from the human reference genome to thousands of genetically diverse human genomes to genomes from hundreds of species, using non-overlapping 6-mer tokenization with a context of up to 1,000 tokens. 21 After pretraining, its rep-resentations transfer to molecular-phenotype prediction by fine-tuning or by training light heads on frozen embeddings, with the largest multi-species variant generally strongest in the original benchmarking. Variant effects are derived by comparing representations for wild-type and mutant sequences. 

DNABERT-2: 22 DNABERT-2 is a compact ( ∼117 M-parameter) BERT-style encoder that re-places the overlapping k-mer tokenization of the original DNABERT with byte-pair encoding and swaps learned positional embeddings for Attention with Linear Biases, alongside efficiency-oriented architectural changes. 22 Pretrained on a large multi-species corpus, it reports performance comparable to or better than the 2.5B-parameter Nucleotide Transformer 21 on a multi-task genome-understanding benchmark introduced alongside it, while using roughly 21 x fewer parameters and approximately 92 x less GPU time in pretraining. Its size makes it a practical backbone for genomic sequence-classification tasks under constrained compute. 

Caduceus: 23 Caduceus is a family of bi-directional, reverse-complement (RC) equivariant long-range DNA language models built on the Mamba state-space block. 23 It extends Mamba to a Bi-Mamba component for bi-directional context and a MambaDNA block that enforces RC equivari-ance, and stacks these layers into a bidirectional trunk that can be pretrained on chromosome-length windows with only a few million parameters. Pretrained by masked language modeling on the hu-man reference genome, it reports that on a challenging long-range variant-effect task it exceeds transformer baselines roughly an order of magnitude larger that do not exploit bi-directionality or strand symmetry. As with the other language models, variant effects are read out post hoc by contrasting learned representations for the wild-type and mutant sequences. 

HyenaDNA: 24 HyenaDNA replaces attention with the implicit long-convolution Hyena opera-tor, giving single-nucleotide-resolution modeling with full global context at every layer and sub-quadratic scaling in sequence length. 24 This lets it reach context lengths of up to one million tokens - an up to 500 × increase over prior dense-attention genomic models - while training substantially faster. Pretrained autoregressively by next-token prediction on the human reference genome with far fewer parameters and less data than earlier foundation models, it reaches state-of-the-art on many of the Nucleotide Transformer and GenomicBenchmarks datasets. Variant effects are again obtained post hoc, by comparing representations for the reference and alternate sequences. 5

2.3 Positioning 

For the frozen-representation comparison, sequence-to-function models provide predicted assay tracks, whereas DNA language models provide trunk embeddings. Wild-type and mutant se-quences are processed independently using a fixed pretrained backbone, and differences between their representations are supplied to a task-specific downstream head. The reported outputs are supervised pathogenicity probabilities for classification or continuous mutation-effect scores for regression, rather than unmodified native zero-shot scores. In addition to frozen-representation baselines, we evaluate full end-to-end fine-tuning of DNABERT-2, 22 Caduceus 23 and HyenaDNA 24 for ClinVar pathogenicity classification, updating the pretrained backbones as well as their prediction heads. This extends the comparison beyond heads trained on fixed features to models whose representations are adapted to the downstream task. MELD combines a compact shared CNN–Transformer encoder with mutation-aware modula-tion and multiscale pooling. Classification uses adaptively amplified scalar differences, whereas quantitative regression uses concatenated embedding differences and a separate regression read-out. These formulations allow efficient paired mutation-effect prediction without requiring a large pretrained foundation-model backbone. 

3 Methods 

3.1 Problem formulation 

Each example consists of a wild-type DNA sequence window Swt , a matched mutant sequence window Smut , and a variant-effect label y. For pathogenicity classification, y ∈ { 0, 1}, where 0

denotes benign and 1 denotes pathogenic; for quantitative mutation-effect regression, y ∈ R is a continuous experimental effect score. The objective is to predict the effect of the mutation rather than the absolute function of either sequence alone. Conceptually, the mutation-effect signal is represented as the difference between mutant and wild type logits: 

∆z = zmut − zwt . (1) The paired differential score can be expressed in simplified form as 

zscore = α∆z = α(zmut − zwt ), (2) followed by a sigmoid probability: 

ˆy = σ(zscore ). (3) In our MELD framework, this differential score is computed separately across multiple mutation-context scales using scale-specific adaptive amplification factors, and the resulting amplified logits are averaged to obtain the final score. Classification uses an amplified scalar differential, whereas quantitative regression uses the DeltaEmbedding readout, which concatenates multiscale differ-ences between pooled WT and mutant representations and maps them through a nonlinear regres-sion head with a final linear output, detailed in Section 4.7. This formulation places the WT-mutant differential directly inside the supervised training ob-jective. This differs from post-hoc variant scoring, where a model is first trained to predict an 6

3.2 Shared WT/mutant encoder 

Wild-type and mutant sequences are encoded using the same shared-weight encoder fθ:

Hwt = fθ(Swt ), Hmut = fθ(Smut ). (4) The encoder is shared between the two branches, so WT and mutant representations are pro-duced in the same learned feature space. The active implementation uses a compact CNN-Transformer encoder composed of token and positional embeddings, five residual CNN blocks, one Transformer encoder layer, and LayerNorm: Embedding → 5 × Residual CNN → 1 × Transformer Encoder → LayerNorm . (5) This compact backbone is used to test the paired differential learning objective under a con-trolled model capacity. The primary contribution is the paired WT/mutant objective and differential scoring mechanism, not the use of a large pretrained sequence model.

4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Primary benchmark: ClinVar standard 1kb 

The primary benchmark was constructed from ClinVar 29 using nominal 1,025-bp variant-centred DNA windows. Each row contains a paired wild-type and mutant DNA window, variant metadata, a binary pathogenicity label, and a preserved train/validation/test split. The split and label distri-bution are shown in Table 1. The label mapping is: 0 = benign, 1 = pathogenic. The dataset contains 1,572,808 paired variant rows. Train 1,482,739 1,198,246 284,493 Validation 35,556 29,127 6,429 Test 54,513 46,120 8,393 Total 1,572,808 1,273,493 299,315 The sequence windows are constructed using the hg38 reference genome. 4.2 ClinVar dataset description 

Before final reporting, the ClinVar standard dataset was audited to avoid any split leakage and data-quality issues. The split is chromosome-disjoint, and no cross-split overlap was found at the variant, WT/mutant-pair, or exact sequence-window level. The dataset is ClinVar-only, so no source-label confounding warning was raised. We performed an audit that identified a small number of duplicated WT/mutant pairs and du-plicated sequence windows. These duplicates were documented and retained because they did not introduce cross-split leakage, and the same audited dataset and fixed split were used consistently across all models and baselines. The audit also identified multi-allelic REF positions, which were retained and documented for transparency. In addition to exact split-leakage checks, we performed a sequence-similarity analysis using a MinHash/LSH near-duplicate screen, recording for each validation or test sequence the nearest approximate match in the training set. No validation or test sequence exceeds 0.90 sequence-similarity to the training set; the only nonzero hit was a single mutant validation sequence at 0.875, below the near-duplicate threshold. Full per-split results are reported in Supplementary Table S1. This supports that the held-out performance is not explained by trivial sequence-window leakage.

4.5 Data augmentation 

During standard training, reverse-complement augmentation is applied with probability 0.5. Asingle Bernoulli draw is made for each minibatch, and the WT sequences, mutant sequences, and mutation masks are reverse-complemented jointly to preserve their alignment. The standard configuration also applies positional-shift augmentation. One shared shift is sam-pled uniformly from [−8, 8] for each minibatch and applied consistently to the WT sequences, mutant sequences, attention masks, and mutation masks. Setting the maximum shift to zero dis-ables positional-shift augmentation. During evaluation, reverse-complement test-time augmentation is applied by computing logits for the original and reverse-complement inputs and averaging the two logits before applying the sigmoid function. Reverse-complement consistency metrics are also reported during evaluation, but reverse-complement consistency loss is not part of the standard training objective. 

4.6 Classification model configuration 

MELD uses the shared CNN-Transformer encoder described in Section 3, with 1,917,059 trainable parameters, comprising token and positional embeddings, five residual dilated CNN blocks, one Transformer encoder layer, and a final LayerNorm (Eq. 5). The WT and mutant windows are pro-cessed by the same shared encoder and prediction head. Representations are pooled globally and within mutation-centered neighborhoods using the pooling radii R ∈ { 0, 4, 32 , 128 }, where R = 0 

denotes masked mean pooling over the complete valid sequence and radii 4, 32 , and 128 denote masked mean pooling over mutation-centered neighborhoods. The per-scale effective amplifica-tion factor (Eq. 14) uses αfloor = 1 and αmax = 48 ; the final paired-model logit is the scale-averaged amplified differential (Eq. 16), mapped to a pathogenicity probability by the sigmoid (Eq. 3). The standard training objective combines positive-class-weighted focal binary cross-entropy with an auxiliary WT/mutant delta regularizer. The standard configuration uses focal-loss param-eter γ = 1 , automatic positive-class weighting, delta-regularizer weight λ∆ = 0 .05 , and neutral-variant regularization weight λneutral = 0 .1.

4.11 Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis 

We performed a staged, grouped hyperparameter sensitivity analysis using the compact 5CNN+1T architecture. A WanDB sweep using Bayesian optimization 27, 36 was performed over 36 unique configurations. The configurations independently varied optimizer settings, delta-regularizer weights, alpha formulation and upper bound, reverse-complement and positional-shift augmentation, or dropout. Each configuration started from the same compact base configuration, and later sensitivity groups did not inherit the best settings from earlier groups. Checkpoints were selected using val-idation AUPRC, and classification thresholds were selected by maximizing validation F1. The held-out test set was evaluated only after validation-based checkpoint and threshold selection. 

4.12 Baselines and ablations 

To isolate the contribution of each modeling component, we compare MELD against the following baseline and ablation configurations: • Same-backbone ISM-style baseline: the post-hoc differential baseline defined in Sec-tion 3.8, trained as an absolute single-sequence classifier over the same compact CNN– Transformer encoder family. • No-alpha ablation: fixes the amplification factor to α = 1 , reducing the prediction score to the unamplified paired WT/mutant differential. • No-mutation-boost ablation: disables the learned mutation-mask boost applied to the mu-tant hidden states. • Single-scale ablation: disables mutation-neighborhood pooling and uses only global valid-sequence pooling. • No-RC ablation: disables reverse-complement augmentation during training and reverse-complement test-time augmentation during evaluation. 4.13 Data-efficiency and training-set scaling 

To evaluate how the paired MELD formulation behaves as the amount of supervised training data changes, we performed a controlled data-efficiency comparison against the same-backbone ISM-style baseline. Starting from the fixed ClinVar training split of 1,482,739 variants, we constructed deterministic nested training subsets containing 10,000, 50,000, 200,000, and 500,000 variants, together with the complete training set. The original validation set of 35,556 variants and held-out test set of 54,513 variants were kept unchanged for every training-size condition. The full-data condition was retrained under the same update-controlled protocol used for all learning-curve conditions and is therefore treated as a separate controlled retraining rather than a reuse of the primary MELD checkpoints. The training subsets preserve the original ClinVar labels and are nested such that every smaller subset is contained within the next larger subset; the resulting training-set compositions are re-ported in Supplementary Table S2. MELD and the same-backbone ISM baseline were trained independently at each data scale using five random seeds. To make optimization effort comparable across training-set sizes, train-ing was controlled by optimizer updates rather than by a fixed number of epochs. The effective batch size was 128, with a maximum of 150,000 optimizer updates. Validation was performed every 1,500 updates, with a minimum of 6,000 updates before early stopping and a patience of 10 validation events, corresponding to 15,000 updates without improvement. Model checkpoints were selected using validation AUPRC. For threshold-dependent classifica-tion metrics, the decision threshold was selected by maximizing validation F1 and was then applied unchanged to the held-out test set. The same training subsets, validation set, test set, and five seeds were used for MELD and ISM. 

4.14 Evaluation metrics and selection protocol 

We evaluate binary pathogenicity prediction using AUROC, AUPRC, F1, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), balanced accuracy, accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, false-positive rate (FPR), Brier score, and expected calibration error (ECE). AUROC and AUPRC are computed from continuous predicted probabilities and are indepen-dent of the classification threshold. F1, MCC, balanced accuracy, accuracy, precision, recall, speci-ficity, and FPR are computed using a classification threshold selected on the validation set by max-imizing validation F1 and then applied once to the held-out test set. These metrics are widely used for binary and imbalanced data classification as illustrated in. 27, 28, 37 

The optimal model configuration is selected using validation AUPRC. Validation data are used only for configuration selection, threshold selection, early stopping, and training monitoring. The held-out test split is never used for model checkpoint, hyperparameter, or threshold selection. Unless explicitly identified as a single-run sensitivity experiment, repeated-seed results use 

5 different random seeds and are reported as mean ± standard deviation. The hyperparameter sensitivity analysis uses fixed seed of 42 and is reported separately from the five-seed main-model and ablation results. For quantitative BRCA1 mutation-effect regression, performance is evaluated using Pearson correlation, Spearman rank correlation, root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination ( R2). Regression checkpoints are selected using valida-tion Spearman correlation. Regression outputs are continuous and unrestricted real-valued. 17

5.1 Base model and architecture capacity 

The MELD model was evaluated on the held-out ClinVar standard test split using 1,025-bp variant-centered sequence windows. This reference architecture contains 1,917,059 trainable parameters and reached a peak GPU memory usage of 10.22 GB during training. The reference MELD model reaches AUROC 0.9859 ± 0.0007 and AUPRC 0.9342 ± 0.0052 ,with strong balanced performance (F1 0.8598 ± 0.0047 , MCC 0.8358 ± 0.0050 ) and calibration (Brier 0.0472 ± 0.0009 , ECE 0.0711 ± 0.0014 ). Full held-out metrics, including balanced accu-racy, accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and false-positive rate, are reported in Supplementary Table S3. These results show that a compact shared CNN-Transformer model trained on paired WT/mutant windows can achieve high held-out pathogenicity prediction performance without re-quiring a large pretrained foundation-model backbone, achieving both high ranking performance (AUROC, AUPRC) and strong thresholded classification (F1, MCC). This MELD model is there-fore retained as a parameter-efficient reference architecture, while the architecture-capacity analy-sis evaluates whether increasing encoder depth improves performance. We evaluated whether increasing encoder depth improves mutation-effect prediction beyond the compact MELD architecture. All architecture variants use the same paired WT/mutant ob-jective, ClinVar standard split, input representation, and training protocol. The sweep varies the number of residual CNN blocks and Transformer encoder layers to assess whether additional local feature-extraction capacity or contextual modeling improves performance. Across the architecture sweep, MELD achieved the strongest mean performance across all reported predictive and calibration metrics (Supplementary Table S4). From Fig. 3D, we observe that the 5CNN+2T model achieved nearly identical AUPRC to the 5CNN+1T architecture despite using an additional Transformer layer and ≈ 10% more parameters. Increasing both convolutional and transformer depth further reduced mean performance and increased variability across seeds, indicating that additional encoder capacity may lead to overfitting and does not consistently benefit this task. The encoder controls also highlight the importance of convolutional feature extraction for variant-effect prediction. The CNN-only model remained competitive, although adding a single Transformer layer produced small relative improvements of 0.22% in AUROC, 0.35% in AUPRC, 0.64% in F1, and 0.87% in MCC, together with relative reductions of 11.9% in Brier score and 17.9% in ECE, as shown in Supplementary Table S4. In contrast, the Transformer-only model per-formed substantially worse across all metrics, suggesting that local convolutional representations are imperative for this task. Based on these results, MELD (5CNN+1T) is retained as the primary architecture because it provides the strongest overall performance while remaining compact and parameter-efficient.

5.4 Performance across variant types and molecular consequences 

We examined MELD’s performance across recorded variant types and transcript-aware molecular consequences in the 54,513-variant ClinVar held-out test set (Fig. 4). The recorded variant-type groups comprised 48,835 single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), 3,587 deletions, 1,993 insertions and 98 multinucleotide variants (MNVs). MELD achieved an AUPRC of 0.8916 ± 0.0045 for SNVs, 

0.9748 ± 0.0057 for deletions and 0.9710 ± 0.0107 for insertions (Fig. 4B). The corresponding pathogenic fractions were 10.2%, 62.4% and 56.4%, respectively, providing context for the high precision–recall performance within these groups. AUPRC was 0.8585 ± 0.0079 for MNVs, al-though this estimate was based on only 98 variants. Performance varied across molecular consequence categories (Fig. 4C). MELD distinguished pathogenic from benign variants within the missense group, which contained 6,190 variants, in-cluding 1,744 pathogenic examples, achieving AUROC 0.8300 ± 0.0034 and AUPRC 0.6371 ±

0.0115 . Discrimination was also observed among in-frame indels, comprising 153 variants with 63 pathogenic examples, with AUROC 0.8717 ± 0.0081 and AUPRC 0.7762 ± 0.0296 . Splice-region variants showed high AUROC ( 0.9442 ±0.0037 ) and AUPRC above the pathogenic fraction (0.3023 ± 0.0842 ), despite containing only 74 pathogenic examples among 5,708 variants (1.30%). Intronic variants exhibited a similar pattern of high AUROC ( 0.8748 ± 0.0062 ) but lower absolute AUPRC ( 0.0957 ± 0.0254 ), with 31 pathogenic examples among 10,080 variants. These findings indicate within-category discrimination across several molecular consequence classes, although precision-recall performance varied considerably. Class composition was important when interpreting the remaining groups. Pathogenic vari-ants accounted for 99.1% of stop-gained, 99.0% of frameshift and 98.3% of splice donor/acceptor variants. Although AUPRC approached unity in these groups, AUROC indicated more limited within-category discrimination, particularly for stop-gained variants, where performance was close to chance. The small numbers of benign examples further limited interpretation of these estimates. Conversely, the synonymous and intergenic groups contained only four and one pathogenic ex-amples, respectively, limiting interpretation of their high AUROC values. AUROC and AUPRC were not reported for the 3 ′ UTR and non-coding exon groups because these subsets contained no pathogenic variants. Overall, MELD showed discrimination across several major variant and con-sequence classes, while the stratified analysis revealed limitations not apparent from the aggregate performance alone.

5.6 End-to-end fine-tuning of DNA language models 

To complement the frozen-representation benchmark, we fully fine-tuned three relatively smaller foundation models, DNABERT-2, 22 Caduceus 23 and HyenaDNA, 24 end-to-end on the full Clin-Var training split and evaluated them on the same 54,513-variant held-out test set used for MELD (Table 3). Each backbone used the DeltaEmbedding head of the frozen baselines and was trained with five seeds, with checkpoints selected by validation AUPRC and classification thresholds se-lected by maximizing validation F1. DNABERT-2, the largest of the three ( ≈117M parameters), achieved a held-out AUROC of 0.9692 ± 0.0008 and AUPRC of 0.9066 ± 0.0006 , compared with 

0.9859 ± 0.0007 and 0.9342 ± 0.0052 for MELD. The corresponding values were 0.9561 ± 0.0018 

and 0.8636 ± 0.0041 for Caduceus, and 0.9271 ± 0.0042 and 0.7397 ± 0.0129 for HyenaDNA. None of the fine-tuned models outperformed MELD on AUROC, AUPRC, F1 or MCC, although fine-tuned DNABERT-2 was better calibrated (Brier 0.0423 vs 0.0472 ; ECE 0.0295 vs 0.0711 ; Sup-plementary Table S11). Taken together, these experiments extend the external-model evaluation to end-to-end supervised adaptation rather than frozen-representation transfer alone. 23

5.7 Comparison with precomputed genome-wide scores 

On the 48,795 held-out SNVs scored by all methods, CADD v1.7 achieved the highest performance on every metric (AUROC 0.9890, AUPRC 0.9400, F1 0.8780, MCC 0.8640), exceeding MELD (AUROC 0.9823 ± 0.0007 , AUPRC 0.8941 ± 0.0032 , F1 0.8041 ± 0.0028 , MCC 0.7830 ± 0.0031 ;Table 4). MELD ranked second, above GPN-MSA (AUROC 0.9604, AUPRC 0.8350) and both conservation scores, phyloP (AUROC 0.9199, AUPRC 0.6773) and phastCons (AUROC 0.8537, AUPRC 0.3345). The same ordering held on the shared-subset test set, where MELD trained on full ClinVar reached an AUROC of 0.9821 ± 0.0005 against 0.9896 for CADD. MELD trained on the 200k shared subset reached an SNV AUROC of 0.9573 ± 0.0010 , slightly below GPN-MSA (0.9626). Restricting to SNVs lowers MELD’s AUROC on the full test set from 0.986 to 0.982. The excluded insertions, deletions and MNVs cannot be scored by CADD or GPN-MSA from their precomputed files, and MELD efficiently classifies them with higher precision (Fig. 4B). CADD is not trained on ClinVar labels, but it integrates more than a hundred annotations, in-cluding cross-species conservation, gene and protein-level features, and protein language model scores. 32 Pathogenic ClinVar SNVs are concentrated at conserved coding and canonical splice positions, where these annotations are most informative. In addition, computational predictions are used as supporting evidence during clinical variant classification, which makes ClinVar labels partly circular with respect to such scores. 38 MELD, by contrast, uses only the 1,025-bp DNA sequence around the variant, without alignments or external annotations or prior knowledge incor-poration. These results show that aggregate ClinVar performance does not establish a significant advantage for MELD over integrative annotation-based scores, and that evaluation on label sources independent of clinical curation is needed to separate the two. 24

5.8 Data-efficiency and training-set scaling 

We next examined whether the advantage of directly learning the paired WT/mutant differential is maintained across different amounts of supervised training data. MELD and the same-backbone ISM baseline were trained using identical nested ClinVar subsets ranging from 10,000 examples to the complete 1,482,739-variant training set, while keeping the validation and held-out test sets fixed. As shown in Fig. 5, both models benefited from increasing amounts of training data, but MELD consistently achieved higher performance across all evaluated training-set sizes. Complete five-seed metrics are reported in Supplementary Table S12. With 10,000 training variants, MELD achieved an AUROC of 0.8090 ± 0.0031 and an AUPRC of 0.5448 ± 0.0068 , compared with 

0.5092 ± 0.0103 and 0.2388 ± 0.0112 , respectively, for ISM. ISM performance improved substantially as the training set increased, with the largest gains occurring between 50,000 and 200,000 variants. Using the complete training set, ISM reached an AUROC of 0.7963 ± 0.0048 , an AUPRC of 0.6973 ± 0.0049 , an F1 score of 0.6818 ± 0.0058 ,and an MCC of 0.6537 ± 0.0076 . MELD maintained a clear advantage at full scale, achieving an AUROC of 0.9861 ± 0.0004 , an AUPRC of 0.9367 ± 0.0037 , an F1 score of 0.8619 ± 0.0034 , and an MCC of 0.8379 ± 0.0036 .Notably, MELD trained on only 50,000 variants already exceeded full-data ISM in both AU-ROC ( 0.9034 versus 0.7963 ) and AUPRC ( 0.7279 versus 0.6973 ). These results indicate that explicitly learning the paired WT/mutant differential provides a considerable advantage in data efficiency, with MELD maintaining higher predictive performance across the complete range of training-set sizes. 25

5.10 Structural context of BRCA1 variant-effect predictions 

To relate the quantitative predictions to protein structure, we mapped held-out BRCA1 variants onto annotated functional regions and the experimental tandem BRCT structure (PDB: 1JNX; 39 ). The held-out coverage was confined to the BRCT regions (Fig. 6A). For the strict ClinVar-transfer model illustrated using seed 42, predicted and observed SGE damage scores showed a positive association across 466 variants, with Pearson r = 0 .518 and Spearman ρ = 0 .452 (Fig. 6B). Four illustrative missense substitutions were examined in structural context: V1687F and M1689R in BRCT1, and V1784E and A1789D in BRCT2 (Fig. 6C). All four had elevated mea-sured SGE damage scores and positive predicted damage scores, although MELD underestimated their measured effect magnitudes. These examples illustrate the correspondence between predicted effects and experimentally measured impairment, while also showing the remaining limitations of quantitative prediction. Inspection of the reference structure placed A1789 in a hydrophobic environment containing F1761, L1764, I1766 and V1784 (Fig. 6D). Replacing alanine with an acidic aspartate could per-turb local packing, providing a plausible structural interpretation of A1789D. Consistent with the importance of this position, a recent study identified A1789 as a BRCT hydrophobic-core residue and demonstrated impaired partner interactions and homologous recombination for A1789L. 40

6 Discussion & Conclusion 

This work tests a simple premise: that a variant’s effect is better learned directly than recovered after the fact. Our proposed approach, MELD, places the wild-type (WT) versus mutant logit differential ∆z inside the supervised objective, and under matched data, split, and backbone this change alone raised ClinVar AUROC from 0.800 (post-hoc ISM) to 0.986, and AUPRC from 0.700 to 0.934. Because the ISM baseline shares MELD’s encoder family and training data, this gap is attributable to the training formulation rather than to model capacity or data. The advantage was most pronounced in low-data regimes: with 10,000 training variants MELD reached an AUROC of 0.809 against 0.509 for ISM, and with 50,000 variants it already exceeded the full-data ISM baseline (AUROC 0.903 vs. 0.796). These findings indicate that the difference signal is a more efficient learning target than absolute single-sequence labels, from which sensitivity to nucleotide-level perturbation must be acquired indirectly. The results also show that this formulation does not require a large backbone. With ap-proximately 1.9M parameters and a 1,025-bp window, MELD outperformed frozen Enformer, 19 

Borzoi, 20 AlphaGenome, 7 and four DNA language models on a controlled shared-subset bench-mark (AUROC 0.967 vs. 0.936 for the strongest baseline, Nucleotide Transformer 21 ), despite these models having 3-1,300 × more parameters. This should be read as evidence for the value of mutation-specific supervision rather than as a claim of general superiority: the external mod-els were trained for absolute signal prediction or self-supervised modeling, and were evaluated through a lightweight head over frozen representations. Notably, fully fine-tuned DNABERT-2 22 

( 117M parameters), trained on the full ClinVar split, reached an AUROC of 0.969 against 0.986 for MELD on the same test set (Table 3), suggesting that supervised adaptation of a pretrained backbone can recover much of the same benefit, at far greater computational cost ( ≈ 60 x). The ablation and sensitivity analyses clarify which components matter. The mutation-mask boost, delta regularization, reverse-complement augmentation, and the full 1,025-bp context each improved performance, and convolutional feature extraction was essential, as the Transformer-only encoder performed markedly worse. Increasing encoder depth did not help and increased seed-to-seed variability, supporting the compact design. In contrast, adaptive amplification contributed modestly: the no-α ablation was nearly as strong (AUROC 0.983 vs. 0.986) and slightly exceeded MELD on thresholded F1 and MCC, indicating that α chiefly benefits ranking. Across 36 hyper-parameter configurations only very high learning rates degraded performance, indicating that the approach is not sensitive to fine-grained tuning. The BRCA1 saturation genome editing experiments indicate that the differential representation extends beyond binary pathogenicity. Under a protocol matched to the external baselines, frozen ClinVar-pretrained MELD achieved a Spearman correlation of 0.42 , exceeding Borzoi 20 (0.40 )and AlphaGenome 7 (0.38 ), and end-to-end transfer raised this to 0.45 . The gap between random initialization ( ρ = 0 .32 ) and ClinVar transfer shows that most of the quantitative gain derives from pretraining on large-scale pathogenicity labels, which supports the transferability of the learned representation. Absolute correlations remain moderate, however, and the BRCA1 test set is small. 28

The pattern of results across models is consistent with the biology of the benchmark. Pathogenic SNVs in ClinVar are concentrated in protein-coding sequence and at canonical splice sites, where a single substitution can introduce a premature stop codon, alter a constrained residue, or abol-ish exon recognition. 29, 41, 42 These effects are largely determined by sequence close to the vari-ant, which a compact model with a 1-kb window and convolutional feature extraction is well placed to capture. The ranking of the frozen sequence-to-function baselines may partly reflect the same mechanism. Enformer predicts regulatory and expression tracks at 128-bp resolution, 19 

Borzoi predicts RNA-seq coverage that partly reflects splicing, 20 and AlphaGenome 7 adds base-resolution splice-site and junction outputs, 7 and AUROC increased in this order (0.723, 0.845, and 0.924), although representation type also differs across these models. Nucleotide Transformer, the strongest frozen baseline, was pretrained on genomes from hundreds of species, 21 so its representa-tions may encode evolutionary constraint, which separates pathogenic from benign variants across the genome. 34 The precomputed scores support this reading: phyloP conservation alone reached an AUROC of 0.920 on held-out ClinVar SNVs, and CADD, which combines conservation with gene- and protein-level annotations, reached 0.989 (Table 4). Conversely, the strengths of the large sequence-to-function models lie in regulatory and long-range effects that ClinVar labels represent poorly, which is consistent with the limitations discussed below. The BRCA1 results can be read in the same way. The saturation genome editing assay mea-sured SNVs across the 13 exons encoding the RING and BRCT domains in HAP1 cells, where BRCA1 is essential, so the damage score is a direct readout of loss of function. 30 In that assay, nonsense and canonical splice-site variants were almost uniformly deleterious, damaging missense variants clustered within the RING and BRCT domains, and a subset of exonic variants acted through disrupted splicing. 30 Because ClinVar pathogenic labels largely encode the same loss-of-function mechanisms, a ClinVar-pretrained representation can transfer to BRCA1 even after overlapping training windows are removed, and the relatively strong performance of Borzoi 20 and AlphaGenome 7 is consistent with the contribution of splicing. Nucleotide Transformer, 21 by con-trast, ranked highly on ClinVar but correlated weakly with BRCA1 damage ( ρ = 0 .08 ). One possible explanation is that a representation of genome-wide constraint discriminates less well within a single gene, where most assayed positions lie in conserved coding exons. The moderate absolute correlations of all models are also expected for DNA-only inputs: the effect of a missense substitution depends on protein structure and residue-level constraint, which protein-level predic-tors model explicitly, 43, 44 so combining the paired differential objective with protein-level features is a natural future extension. Several limitations are readily addressable in future work. First, quantitative evaluation is lim-ited to one gene and 466 test variants; additional saturation mutagenesis and MPRA datasets would establish generality and tighten confidence intervals. Second, MELD scores single variants within a 1-kb window, so long-range regulatory effects, splicing, and multi-track outputs, which are the strengths of the foundation models, are not captured; longer windows or a hybrid with a pretrained backbone are natural extensions. Third, on the SNVs it can score, the integrative CADD v1.7 score exceeded MELD on ClinVar (AUROC 0.989 vs. 0.982; Table 4), so MELD’s advantage is estab-lished against sequence-only models rather than against annotation-based genome-wide scores. Finally, ClinVar labels are subject to ascertainment bias and label noise, and a few duplicated sequence windows were retained; evaluation on independent sets such as population-frequency-derived benign variants and deep mutational scanning data would strengthen the evidence. 30
