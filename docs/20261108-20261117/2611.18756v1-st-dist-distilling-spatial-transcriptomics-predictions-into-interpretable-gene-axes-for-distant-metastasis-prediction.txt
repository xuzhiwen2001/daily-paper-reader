ABSTRACT 

Distant metastasis at diagnosis determines the treatment of prostate cancer, but predicting it from routine biopsy slides is chal-lenging as the signs of metastasis are not clearly localized and are more subtle than in standard tumor detection and subtyping tasks. Multiple instance learning (MIL) on pathology foundation-model features has become the standard approach when labeled data are abundant, but such models remain hard to interpret. Distant metas-tasis is further challenging, because confirming the label requires clinical follow-up beyond the routine H&E slides. To this end, we propose ST-Dist, which distills the gene expression predicted by a spatial transcriptomics (ST) model into a linear head mapping each tile’s foundation-model features onto named gene axes. The slide’s projections along these interpretable gene axes are then used for distant metastasis prediction with a simple logistic regression. On 497 prostate needle biopsies (205 patients), ST-Dist reaches an AUROC of 91.37%, above nine MIL baselines. It remains com-petitive on the public TCGA-COAD and TCGA-KIRC cohorts, and it has the highest mean AUROC over the three cohorts. The abla-tions on the prostate biopsies show that the gene axes outperform a linear probe on encoder features, dimension-matched principal com-ponents, random gene panels and the ST model’s own predicted ex-pression, and the gain over the probe holds on all 13 pathology en-coder settings that we tested. ST-Dist thus offers a simple, accurate and interpretable route from H&E to distant metastasis prediction. 

Index Terms — Spatial transcriptomics, distillation, MIL 

1. INTRODUCTION 

A patient who already has distant metastasis when prostate cancer is diagnosed (stage M1 rather than M0) needs systemic therapy, not lo-cal treatment alone. Knowing whether metastasis is present is there-fore one of the most important questions at diagnosis, and one that the biopsy from the primary tumor alone does not yet answer. Un-like tumor detection or subtyping, which have abundant labels and a clearly localized signal, metastasis prediction relies on signs that are not confined to one region of the slide [1, 2]. Additionally, confirm-ing metastatic status requires further imaging and clinical follow-up, which makes labels expensive and cohorts small. Clinical risk groups and tissue-based genomic classifiers such as Decipher [3], the cell cycle progression score [4] and the Genomic Prostate Score [5] help to assess the aggressiveness of the cancer but cannot establish a clear metastatic status. These classifiers addition-ally need dedicated molecular assays, which are not always avail-able. In contrast, the routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slide exists for every patient, but whether this slide can predict M0/M1 status at diagnosis is largely unexplored. Recently, deep learning has shown the prognostic value of biopsy histology by combining slide images with clinical variables to predict long-term distant metastasis in phase III trial cohorts [6]. Standard approaches like multiple instance learning (MIL) on pathology foundation-model features [7, 8, 9] learn attention weights that pick out the most informative tiles, which suits localized sig-nals. With few labels, however, simple pooling without attention is often as good as complex MIL [10, 11]. Further, the explanations that MIL offers, mostly attention heatmaps, are not always accepted as interpretable [12]. Gene expression offers a different language for the same slide. Clinicians already reason about metastatic risk in terms of genes, and with models now capable of predicting expression from H&E, predicted expression can be leveraged for downstream tasks. Paired spatial transcriptomics (ST) resources such as HEST-1k [13] have enabled the development of foundation models such as STPath [14], which predicts tens of thousands of genes for every tile of a WSI. Similarly, virtual proteins predicted from H&E have been shown to improve prognostic prediction in lung cancer [15], further showing that predicted expression is useful for prognosis. In this work, we propose genes as a concept bottleneck [16]. Un-like a recent concept-based MIL method [12], which predicts its con-cepts with a vision-language model, we distill the concepts (genes) from an ST foundation model, STPath. A linear head on top of a frozen pathology encoder learns to reproduce the expression that the teacher, STPath, predicts for a small, pre-specified panel of metasta-sis related genes. A logistic regression trained on the outcome labels then predicts metastasis from the slide-averaged gene values. The head can sit on any pathology encoder, independent of the teacher’s backbone. We call this model ST-Dist. Every input of downstream classifiers using ST-Dist is thus a named gene rather than an un-named image feature. On 497 prostate needle biopsies, ST-Dist outperforms nine MIL methods, and we further evaluate it on two public cohorts of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), TCGA-COAD and TCGA-KIRC. 2. METHOD Problem setting. A slide s is represented by its Ns tissue tiles, with features xi = f (tile i) ∈ Rd from a frozen pathology encoder f .The task is to predict the slide label ys ∈ { 0, 1} (M0 or M1) from a small labeled cohort. ST-Dist addresses it as shown in Fig. 1. 

Tile encoder. Our primary pathology encoder ( f ) is Prov-Giga-Path [17], a vision transformer pretrained by self-supervised learn-ing on 1.3 billion tiles ( d = 1536 ). Any other pathology encoder can serve as f . The teacher, STPath, always receives GigaPath features, so a different encoder changes only the input of the gene head. 

Spatial transcriptomics teacher. STPath is a generative founda-tion model that predicts the expression of 38,984 genes from his-tology. It is a geometry-aware transformer, pretrained by masked gene-expression prediction on about 1,000 public slides with paired ST profiles from 17 organs. Each tile is one token, which com-bines the tile’s GigaPath feature, an expression input, an organ to-ken and a sequencing-technology token; tile positions enter through the geometry-aware attention. At inference, all expression inputs are masked, so the prediction rests on histology alone. We use the organ token of each cohort (Prostate, Kidney or Bowel) and no technology token. 3. EXPERIMENTS Cohorts. Our main cohort consists of 497 pretreatment prostate nee-dle biopsies from 205 patients (98 with M1), labeled M0 or M1 for distant metastasis from clinical reports and follow-up. The main co-hort data come from a large US hospital network. To test our ap-proach on other cancers, we use two TCGA resection cohorts [18] with the same endpoint: kidney clear-cell carcinoma (TCGA-KIRC, 509 slides from 505 patients, 76 with M1) and colon adenocarci-noma (TCGA-COAD, 387 slides from 380 patients, 60 with M1). The TCGA labels use the pathologic M stage (the clinical one when it is missing). Patients with MX or no M stage are excluded. Each cohort is split into three patient-level folds, each with training, vali-dation and test sets. 

Gene panels. Each cohort has its own panel. The prostate panel has 117 genes. Of these, 100 come from published signatures of metas-tasis and aggressiveness [19, 3, 4, 5]. Ten are prostate lineage genes, such as NKX3-1 and MSMB, and seven are genes often altered in metastases. The kidney (98 genes) and colon (113 genes) panels come from five and seven published sources. The panel files, with the source of every gene, will be released with the code. 

A second vocabulary. To test whether the result depends on our hand-built panel, we also use a vocabulary from a single stan-dard source, fixed by name before its result was computed: the 1,236 genes of 10 Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) Hall-mark pathways linked to metastasis [20]. These are epithelial– mesenchymal transition, TGF-β signaling, apical junction, hypoxia, angiogenesis, inflammatory response, IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, TNF α signaling via NF-κB, E2F targets and the G2M checkpoint. 

Encoders and baselines. Our main experiments use GigaPath fea-tures of 62.5 μm tiles, and robustness is tested on 13 settings of 9 pathology encoders (Sec. 4). The other settings use 125 μm tiles. For a 125 μm tile, the teacher’s latent is averaged over the four 62.5 μmtiles that it contains, and the gene values follow by the same linear readout. We compare ST-Dist with nine MIL methods. Seven use attention: ABMIL [7], CLAM [21], DSMIL [22], TransMIL [23], DTFD-MIL [24], ILRA [25] and RRT-MIL [8]. Two use simple pooling techniques: SiMLP [10] combines mean-pooling with MLP and Maxsoft [11] takes the highest tile score. Evaluation. We report the slide-level AUROC as the mean over the three test folds, with its standard deviation (SD) over runs (three folds for logistic models, 15 fold-and-seed runs for MIL). On the main cohort we also report average precision (AP) and balanced ac-curacy at a 0.5 threshold. All metrics are given in percent. Dif-ferences between models are tested with a paired bootstrap over patients (2,000 resamples), in which predictions are averaged over seeds and over each patient’s slides, and pooled over the three test folds. We call a difference significant when its 95% bootstrap confi-dence interval excludes zero. 

4. RESULTS 4.1. Accuracy, ablations and robustness Performance on the prostate biopsies. Table 1 compares ST-Dist with nine MIL methods on the main cohort. ST-Dist has the high-est AUROC (91.37%), average precision (92.29%) and balanced accuracy (82.12%), and its AUROC is 2.2 points above the best MIL method (ABMIL, 89.13%). Among the MIL methods, sim-ple mean pooling (SiMLP, 88.54%) comes within 0.6 points of the best attention-based method, in line with earlier findings for small cohorts [10, 11]. 

Results on the TCGA cohorts. We refit the same recipe on each TCGA cohort, with the cohort’s own gene panel (Table 2). These resection cohorts are harder than the biopsies: M1 patients are rarer (15–16%), and every method scores lower. On TCGA-COAD, ST-Dist (67.71%) outperforms all nine MIL methods, by 2.9 points over the best one (Maxsoft), significantly so against RRT-MIL, TransMIL and DTFD-MIL. On TCGA-KIRC, it ranks second (81.19%), 0.2 points behind ILRA. Without the gene head, the linear probe reaches 78.05% on TCGA-KIRC and 69.16% on TCGA-COAD. Overall, ST-Dist has the highest mean AUROC (80.09%, against 78.68% for the probe and 77.36% for the best MIL method, CLAM).

4.2. Which genes separate M1 from M0 Genes with the largest differences. ST-Dist outperforms its teacher (Table 3). To check that its gene values still carry the teacher’s bi-ology, Fig. 2 compares the ten genes with the largest M1–M0 dif-ferences in ST-Dist with the same genes in STPath, measured by Hedges’ g (the difference in patient means, divided by the pooled SD). ST-Dist’s differences closely follow STPath’s (Pearson r = 0.99 over all 117 genes) and are larger for 8 of the 10 genes. The student thus keeps the teacher’s gene-level signal and separates M1 from M0 slightly more strongly. 

Grounding in measured expression. On eight held-out prostate Vi-sium slides from HEST-Bench [13], which STPath did not see during training, STPath predicts 12 of the 115 panel genes that these slides measure with a mean correlation of at least 0.3 (0.32 to 0.52) against measured expression, computed per gene within each slide. These are mostly luminal and androgen-regulated genes (MSMB, AZGP1, SLC45A3, TMPRSS2, KLK2, KLK3, HOXB13, NKX3-1, SPDEF, ANO7, TPM2 and FOXA1). They include two of the genes with the largest M1–M0 differences in both models, MSMB and AZGP1 (Fig. 2), whose loss has been associated with aggressive prostate cancer [34, 35]. Recovery of known biology. Used alone in place of the panel, the E2F targets pathway (one of the 10 Hallmark pathways) reaches an AUROC of 91.43 ± 1.07%. 5. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

ST-Dist shows that distant metastasis, a label-scarce target, is well served by a simple model that reads pathology features through a small set of named genes rather than unnamed image features. The genes do more than make the model interpretable, they act as a prior that selects which visual directions are worth learning from few la-bels. A task-relevant vocabulary, whether our curated panel or the metastasis-related Hallmark pathways, keeps most of this benefit. In summary, distilling an ST foundation model into named gene axes turns any pathology encoder into a simple, interpretable and com-petitive predictor of distant metastasis from routine H&E slides. 

6. COMPLIANCE WITH ETHICAL STANDARDS 

As an analysis of de-identified images, the study is not human sub-jects research and was exempt from IRB review. The TCGA [18] and HEST-1k [13] data are publicly available, and their use did not require separate ethical approval.
