# Abstract 

Motivation: Single-cell perturbation prediction seeks to recover transcriptional responses to unseen interventions from unpaired control and perturbed populations. The Schr¨ odinger Bridge provides a principled framework for modeling these transitions as stochastic transport, but learning bridge dynamics directly from high-dimensional, sparse gene-expression profiles remains challenging. Results: We present PerturbBridge, a conditional latent Schr¨ odinger Bridge framework for single-cell perturbation response prediction. Aresponse-anchored autoencoder constructs a compact cellular representation that preserves dominant transcriptional response directions identified from training perturbations while encoding complementary cellular variation. In this latent space, PerturbBridge models conditional stochastic transport through a tractable approximation to the Schr¨ odinger Bridge. We introduce the Perturbation-Induced Bridge Transformer, which uses perturbation- and time-dependent slots to guide interactions among latent coordinates during drift estimation. Evaluations on Norman, Sci-Plex3, and Replogle RPE1 demonstrate leading performance in differential-expression direction recovery and perturbation-effect correlation, alongside competitive gene-expression prediction accuracy. Availability: The source code is available at https://github.com/echQoQ/PerturbBridge , with an archived version at https: //doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24272128 .

## 1. Introduction 

Single-cell perturbation technologies, including CRISPR-based screens and chemical treatment assays, enable systematic investigation of transcriptional responses to genetic and pharmacological interventions (Dixit et al., 2016; Srivatsan et al., 2020). Beyond advancing our understanding of gene regulation and drug action, these technologies provide valuable data for developing AI-driven virtual cell models that seek to predict cellular behavior under different conditions (Bunne et al., 2024). A central challenge toward this goal is predicting responses to unseen perturbations, as the vast space of possible interventions, dosages, and cellular contexts makes exhaustive experimental characterization impractical (Wei et al., 2026). This task is further complicated by the destructive nature of single-cell sequencing, which prevents direct observation of the same cell before and after perturbation. Consequently, control and perturbed cells are observed as unpaired populations (Bunne et al., 2023), requiring computational models to recover both transcriptional response patterns and population-level variability from these observations. A variety of computational methods have been developed for single-cell perturbation prediction. GEARS (Roohani et al., 2024) exploits gene relationships to predict transcriptional responses to genetic perturbations, while scGPT (Cui et al., 2024) leverages pretrained single-cell representations for perturbation modeling. Conditional generative models, such as chemCPA (Hetzel et al., 2022) and PRnet (Qi et al., 2024), predict responses to chemical perturbations, whereas PerturbNet (Yu et al., 2025) employs conditional normalizing flows to model perturbed cell distributions. Recent approaches have further explored generative dynamics for perturbation prediction. CellFlow (Klein et al., 2025) learns perturbation-conditioned transport through flow matching, while scLDM (Wu et al., 2026) uses conditional latent diffusion to generate post-perturbation cellular states. These advances have extended perturbation prediction toward modeling the distributional changes induced by interventions. The Schr¨ odinger Bridge (SB) provides a principled framework for stochastic transport between probability distributions, seeking a stochastic process that satisfies prescribed endpoint marginals while minimizing relative entropy with respect to a reference process (L´ eonard, 2013). For general endpoint distributions, however, the SB problem typically lacks a closed-form solution, and numerical methods often involve computationally demanding iterative procedures. Recent advances have developed practical approximations for learning SB dynamics (De Bortoli et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). However, applying SB directly to high-dimensional, sparse gene-expression data remains challenging. Departures (Chi et al., 2026) approaches this problem by constructing approximate endpoint couplings through minibatch optimal transport and jointly learning two coupled bridges for continuous gene expression and discrete gene activation states. This design explicitly accounts for expression sparsity, while requiring the learning of two stochastic processes at the gene level. An alternative is to learn bridge dynamics in a compact cellular latent space, potentially capturing coordinated transcriptional variation in fewer dimensions. The challenge then lies in preserving the perturbation-induced transcriptional changes needed for accurate population-level prediction. To address these challenges, we propose PerturbBridge, aconditional latent Schr¨ odinger Bridge framework for predicting transcriptional responses to unseen perturbations from unpaired single-cell observations. PerturbBridge first learns a compact cellular representation using a response-anchored autoencoder (RA-AE), which explicitly preserves dominant transcriptional response directions identified from training perturbations while encoding the remaining cellular variation. Within this representation, PerturbBridge learns a conditional latent SB using a tractable bridge-matching formulation. To model how cellular states and perturbation conditions influence the bridge dynamics, we develop the Perturbation-Induced Bridge Transformer (PIBT), which uses perturbation- and time-dependent representations to guide interactions among latent features during conditional drift estimation. During inference, the learned stochastic dynamics transport encoded control cells toward perturbation-specific latent populations, which are subsequently decoded into gene-expression profiles. We evaluate PerturbBridge on three benchmarks spanning genetic and chemical perturbations: Norman (Norman et al., 2019), Sci-Plex3 (Srivatsan et al., 2020), and Replogle RPE1 (Replogle et al., 2022). Across these datasets, PerturbBridge demonstrates strong performance in gene-expression prediction, differential-expression recovery, and perturbation discrimination. In particular, it achieves leading results in differential-expression direction recovery and perturbation-effect correlation across all three benchmarks, while maintaining high gene-expression prediction accuracy. Ablation studies further support the contributions of response-anchored representation learning and PIBT-based conditional drift modeling, including the benefits of condition-guided interactions among latent coordinates.

## 2.1. Overview of PerturbBridge 

Let x ∈ RG denote the gene-expression profile of a single cell,

Given a control population and a specified perturbation condition, our objective is to predict the corresponding perturbed cell distribution, including for perturbations unseen during training. PerturbBridge models the transition between control and perturbed populations as a conditional Schr¨ odinger Bridge (SB) in a compact cellular latent space, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The latent representation is constructed to preserve dominant transcriptional response directions identified from training perturbations while encoding the remaining expression variation. We achieve this through a response-anchored autoencoder (RA-AE), which provides a shared latent space for control and perturbed cells. Stochastic transport between the resulting latent distributions is then learned through a tractable bridge-matching formulation. To account for the dependence of bridge dynamics on cellular states, time, and perturbation conditions, we introduce a condition-guided interaction mechanism that coordinates information exchange among latent features. This mechanism is implemented in the Perturbation-Induced Bridge Transformer (PIBT) to parameterize the conditional drift field. Training proceeds in two stages. RA-AE is first optimized for gene-expression reconstruction, after which its encoder and decoder are frozen. The conditional bridge model is then trained using latent representations of unpaired control and perturbed cells. During inference, control cells are encoded into the latent space and transported under the learned stochastic dynamics conditioned on the target perturbation. The resulting latent states are decoded to generate the predicted perturbed cell population.

## 2.6. Datasets 

We evaluate PerturbBridge on three single-cell perturbation benchmarks spanning genetic and chemical interventions: Norman (Norman et al., 2019), Sci-Plex3 (Srivatsan et al., 2020), and Replogle RPE1 (Replogle et al., 2022). We use the datasets released with PerturbNet (Yu et al., 2025) for Norman and Sci-Plex3, and the preprocessed Replogle RPE1 dataset distributed through GEARS (Roohani et al., 2024). Norman comprises 109,738 cells and 2,279 genes, covering single-gene and combinatorial CRISPR perturbations, with 95 of 236 perturbation conditions held out for testing. Sci-Plex3 contains 648,737 cells and 5,087 genes, spanning 188 drugs across three cell lines and four dosage levels, with 38 drugs reserved for testing. Replogle RPE1 comprises 162,733 cells and 5,000 genes, covering 1,543 genetic perturbation targets, of which 145 are held out for testing. All splits are defined at the perturbation level, with separate validation subsets drawn from the training perturbations for representation and bridge learning. 

## 2.7. Implementation Details 

PerturbBridge is implemented in PyTorch and trained in two stages. RA-AE employs an encoder with hidden dimensions of 512 and 256 and a symmetric decoder, using GELU activations. The latent and response dimensions ( d, r ) are set to (24 , 4) for Norman and Sci-Plex3 and (64 , 8) for Replogle RPE1. For PIBT, the hidden dimension, number of slots, number of blocks, and affinity dimension (H, K, L, A ) are set to (128 , 4, 3, 32) for Norman and (256 , 8, 4, 64) for Sci-Plex3 and Replogle RPE1. RA-AE is trained using Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014) with a batch size of 4,096 and an initial learning rate of 5 × 10 −4, after which its parameters are frozen. PIBT is optimized using AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) with a weight decay of 10 −4. The batch sizes are 2,048 for Norman and Replogle RPE1 and 4,096 for Sci-Plex3, with initial learning rates of 10 −4 and 5 × 10 −5, respectively. Both stages are trained for 200 epochs using cosine learning rate schedules. For chemical perturbations, we use precomputed 384-dimensional embeddings from ChemBERTa (Chithrananda et al., 2020), derived from molecular SMILES representations, together with dose and cell-line covariates. For genetic perturbations, gene embeddings are constructed by applying PCA to Gene Ontology (GO) annotations (Aleksander et al., 2023). The resulting embeddings have dimensions of 256 for Norman and 384 for Replogle RPE1, with individual gene embeddings summed for combinatorial perturbations. The pretrained chemical representations and GO-derived embeddings remain fixed, while the downstream condition encoders are optimized jointly with PIBT. The reference diffusion scale is set to σB = 1 .0, and stochastic inference is performed using 100 Euler–Maruyama integration steps. All experiments are conducted in FP32 on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.

## 3.2. Main Results: Performance on Unseen Perturbations 

Overall performance across datasets. We evaluate PerturbBridge on three established single-cell perturbation benchmarks—Norman, Sci-Plex3, and Replogle RPE1—spanning both genetic and chemical perturbations. We compare PerturbBridge with representative baselines encompassing direct expression prediction and conditional generative modeling, using metrics that assess expression accuracy, differential-expression recovery, and perturbation discrimination. The overall results are presented in Fig. 3(a–c). Among the generative baselines, CellFlow, a flow matching-based approach, exhibits strong and relatively consistent performance across the three datasets, approaching PerturbBridge on Replogle RPE1. However, larger performance gaps emerge on Norman and Sci-Plex3, particularly in differential-expression recovery. PerturbNet, based on a conditional invertible neural network (cINN), performs competitively on Sci-Plex3 but shows weaker performance on Replogle RPE1, whereas the latent diffusion-based scLDM exhibits limited recovery of perturbation-induced transcriptional changes, particularly on Norman and Sci-Plex3. Departures provides a particularly relevant comparison, as it also builds on the Schr¨ odinger Bridge framework but employs dual bridges in gene-expression space with minibatch optimal transport for endpoint coupling. Despite this shared theoretical foundation, PerturbBridge achieves consistently stronger perturbation-response prediction, with particularly pronounced advantages in differential-expression recovery on Norman and perturbation discrimination on Replogle RPE1. These findings further support the effectiveness of latent-space bridge modeling. Overall, PerturbBridge achieves state-of-the-art performance across the three benchmarks, with particularly strong results in recovering perturbation-induced transcriptional changes. It consistently achieves the highest DirM and Pearson ∆ scores across all three datasets, indicating accurate recovery of differential-expression directions and expression changes relative to controls. Its leading SpLFC scores on Norman and Sci-Plex3 further demonstrate strong agreement in the relative ordering of gene-level log-fold changes. PerturbBridge also achieves the highest PDS scores across all three distance variants on Norman and Replogle RPE1, reflecting improved discrimination of perturbation-specific transcriptional signatures. Notably, these advantages are accompanied by the lowest MSE across all three benchmarks, indicating that improved perturbation-response fidelity is achieved without compromising overall expression prediction accuracy. 

Consistency across perturbation conditions. To assess the consistency of performance improvements across individual perturbation conditions, we compare PerturbBridge with CellFlow and Departures using condition-level DirM and SpLFC scores (Fig. 3d–e). DirM measures agreement in the direction of differential expression between predicted and observed responses, whereas SpLFC evaluates the Spearman correlation of gene-level log-fold changes, reflecting the recovery of relative transcriptional response patterns. On Norman, PerturbBridge outperforms both baselines across nearly all perturbation conditions on both metrics, with Departures exhibiting the largest performance gaps. These advantages are observed across different levels of target-gene novelty, including single-gene and combinatorial perturbations involving unseen targets. On Sci-Plex3, although the performance differences are generally smaller, PerturbBridge achieves higher scores for the majority of drug–dose conditions, with a particularly consistent advantage in SpLFC. Together, these results indicate that the improvements in differential-expression recovery extend across diverse perturbation conditions rather than being driven by a limited number of favorable cases. 

Transcriptional response fidelity. To further examine the fidelity of predicted transcriptional responses, we investigate PRPF31 perturbation in Replogle RPE1 (Fig. 3f–g). In the UMAP visualization, PerturbBridge-predicted cells show closer alignment with the observed perturbed population, whereas predictions from Departures and scLDM exhibit more pronounced spatial deviations (Fig. 3f). Comparison of the five most upregulated and five most downregulated differentially expressed genes further shows that PerturbBridge generally reproduces the observed expression distributions more closely than the competing methods (Fig. 3g). 

## 3.3. Ablation Study 

We conduct ablation experiments on Norman to assess the contributions of the key components of PerturbBridge (Table 1). Replacing RA-AE with a conventional autoencoder consistently reduces predictive performance, supporting the benefit of explicitly preserving perturbation-associated response directions in the latent representation. Replacing PIBT with DiT leads to performance degradation across all metrics, with particularly pronounced declines in perturbation discrimination and expression prediction accuracy, highlighting the effectiveness of PIBT for conditional drift modeling. To further examine the role of condition-dependent coordinate– slot interactions, we replace the dynamic slot keys with learnable static keys while retaining condition-dependent slot representations and the read–process–write mechanism. This modification degrades performance across all metrics, suggesting that directly incorporating perturbation and bridge-time information into the coordinate–slot affinity computation provides additional benefits beyond conditioning the slot representations alone. 

## 4. Discussion 

PerturbBridge provides a conditional latent Schr¨ odinger Bridge framework for predicting transcriptional responses to unseen perturbations from unpaired single-cell populations. By modeling perturbation-induced transitions as stochastic transport in a compact

RA-AE constructs aresponse-aware representation that explicitly preserves dominant perturbation-associated transcriptional directions while encoding complementary cellular variation. Within this latent space, PIBT parameterizes the conditional bridge drift through interactions among latent coordinates guided by perturbation conditions and bridge time. Experiments across three genetic and chemical perturbation benchmarks demonstrate strong performance in recovering perturbation-induced transcriptional changes, particularly in differential-expression direction recovery and perturbation-effect correlation. Condition-level comparisons further indicate that these improvements extend across diverse perturbations rather than being driven by a limited number of conditions. Ablation studies further support the contributions of response-anchored representation learning and PIBT-based conditional drift modeling. Despite these promising results, several aspects warrant further investigation. The response subspace is derived from training perturbations, and its ability to capture transcriptional response directions not adequately represented during training remains to be systematically evaluated. Moreover, the current bridge formulation adopts independent endpoint sampling as a tractable approximation, leaving the potential benefits of alternative coupling strategies unexplored. Future work will investigate more adaptive response representations and alternative coupling strategies, while extending evaluation to broader cellular contexts and pursuing prospective experimental validation.
