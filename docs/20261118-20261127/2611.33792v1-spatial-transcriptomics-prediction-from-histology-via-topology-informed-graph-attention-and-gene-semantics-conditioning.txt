Abstract 

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) profiles gene expression with spatial context but remains costly, motivating models that infer expression from H&E images. Existing approaches face three key limitations: (i) a single patch embedding is often insufficient—morphologically similar regions can exhibit distinct expression due to spatial context, while naive neighborhood aggregation can over-smooth bound-aries; (ii) conventional fixed, gene-indexed output heads treat genes independently and cannot directly support strict zero-shot prediction for unseen genes; and (iii) common metrics under-measure spatial pattern fidelity and bound-ary resolution. To address these challenges, we propose TIGEM, a topology-informed, gene-conditioned framework for histology-to-ST inference. TIGEM learns topology-aware spot representations by regularizing graph attention with tissue-level topology and morphology cues to avoid cross-compartment smoothing, and uses gene semantic modulation to replace gene-indexed heads, enabling zero-shot prediction for unseen genes. We also introduce Graph Spectral Correlation (GSC) to evaluate spatial pattern fi-delity and boundary resolution complement to PCC/MSE. Across three large-scale ST datasets, TIGEM achieves over-all state-of-the-art performance and significantly improved boundary prediction resolution, and further demonstrates the ability to predict expression patterns for genes unseen during training, extending prior zero-shot histology-to-ST efforts with topology-aware spatial propagation and PLM-based gene conditioning. The model is open-sourced at https://github.com/ZhangLabGT/TIGEM. 

1. Introduction 

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) profiles gene expression while preserving tissue architecture, enabling links between molecular activity, morphology, and spatial organiza-tion [16, 32]. Standard workflows partition hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained whole-slide images (WSI) into thousands of micrometer-scale spots and measure spot-level expression [22, 23], providing insights into cellular hetero-geneity and tissue microenvironments [24, 28]. However, ST remains costly and operationally complex, limiting clin-ical deployment. In contrast, H&E slides are inexpensive and routinely collected in clinical settings, motivating mod-els that infer spatial gene expression directly from histology. A common paradigm in histology-to-ST prediction is to extract a H&E patch-level embedding for each spot and then regress spot-level expression [8, 21, 39, 45], option-ally with graph-based neighborhood aggregation to incor-porate spatial context [4, 11, 12, 25, 30, 41, 44]. Al-though this formulation appears straightforward, it relies on a strong implicit assumption: that local patch appearance (or together with all spatial neighbors) is sufficiently infor-mative for downstream expression inference. In practice, however, this assumption is often invalid. Morphologically similar spots may still exhibit markedly different transcrip-tional programs when their surrounding tissue organization differs, for example across glandular lumens, compartment boundaries, stromal interfaces, or other microenvironmen-tal transitions [5, 46]. Some methods attempt to solve this problem by replacing deterministic regression with genera-tive modeling and sampling-based prediction. Yet the core input assumption remains largely unchanged: the model is still asked to infer expression primarily from a single patch-level representation without explicitly modeling the surrounding tissue context. Meanwhile, we should also no-ticed that naive neighborhood aggregation can mix distinct tissue contexts into overly similar representations, leading to over-smoothed predictions and reduced fidelity near spa-tial expression boundaries. These observations suggest that the missing ingredient is not merely a stronger regressor, but a context-aware propa-gation rule that adapts message passing to slide-specific tis-sue organization. In particular, spatial modeling should be guided by a learned notion of where information can plausi-bly travel in the tissue plane, instead of assuming that spatial location proximity alone implies expression coupling. A second limitation is that gene semantics remain under-1arXiv:2611.33792v1 [cs.CV] 25 Nov 2026 utilized in many histology-to-ST predictors. Conventional methods typically employ a fixed, gene-indexed output head of size |Gtrain |, restricting prediction to predefined training genes and preventing direct inference for unseen genes un-der a strict zero-shot split ( Gtest ∩ Gtrain = ∅) without mod-ifying or retraining the output layer. Recent studies[3, 40] have begun to explore semantic conditioning for unseen-gene prediction, but they largely focus on gene-level gen-eralization without explicitly modeling tissue topology or boundary-aware spatial propagation. This motivates gene-conditioned predictors that can transfer across genes while preserving spatial context. Together, these limitations not only limit prediction reso-lution and boundary accuracy but also hinder clinical gener-alization. In addition, existing evaluation metrics are often overly simplistic, relying primarily on Pearson correlation. As a result, the spatial fidelity of a model at the boundary-level is often overlooked, and such metrics do not quan-tify the extent to which the predictions preserve biologically meaningful and spatially localized heterogeneity. To address these limitations, we propose TIGEM 

(Topology-Informed Graph attention with g Ene semantic 

Modulation), a framework for predicting both seen and un-seen spatial gene expression from histology images (Fig. 1). TIGEM first introduces a Topology-Informed Graph atten-tion (TIG) module that learns a slide-specific continuous barrier field from morphology and topology cues to regular-ize graph attention, encouraging within-region propagation while suppressing message passing across potential tissue boundaries. TIGEM further replaces conventional gene-indexed output heads with a Gene Semantic Modulation unit (GSMu), where pretrained gene embeddings condition the readout and enable strict zero-shot inference by swap-ping in unseen-gene embeddings at test time. Together, these modules address both indiscriminate neighborhood aggregation and the fixed-output limitation of conventional predictors. We additionally introduce Graph Spectral Cor-relation (GSC), a graph-spectral metric that complements spot-wise measures such as PCC by evaluating spatial pat-tern fidelity. We provide its mathematical formulation and biological interpretation, and use it as a complementary cri-terion for benchmarking spatial expression patterns. We summarize our contributions as follows: • We present TIGEM , a topology-informed graph atten-tion framework for histology-to-ST prediction, which sig-nificantly improves both overall expression accuracy and boundary fidelity, and achieves state-of-the-art results on three diverse ST benchmarks. • By adopting pretrained pathology vision models (PVM) and protein language models (PLMs) [2, 17, 19] for the patch and gene embedding generation, TIGEM also sig-nificantly reduce the computational cost compared to ex-isting prediction models. • We extend zero-shot histology-to-ST prediction by in-troducing a PLM-conditioned GSMu decoder that sup-ports strict unseen-gene inference, and combine it with topology-informed graph attention to improve spatial fi-delity for clinically relevant biomarkers. • We propose GSC, a graph-spectral metric that com-plements conventional magnitude-based measures (e.g., MSE and PCC) by quantifying spatial pattern preserva-tion and boundary-resolving ability. 

2. Related works 

ST prediction from histology images. Predicting spatially resolved gene expression from H&E-stained WSIs has at-tracted growing interest in computational pathology. Ex-isting methods can fall into regression-based and genera-tive paradigms. Regression approaches range from spot-wise predictors such as ST-Net [8] to spatial-context models that aggregate neighborhood information and even multi-model information, such as HisToGene [25], Hist2ST [41], TRIPLEX [4], and newly published M2TGLGO [30]. Com-plementary lines improve robustness by aligning images and genes via contrastive-based designs (BLEEP [36], EGN [39]). Generative predictors are more recent, exem-plified by STEM [45] using conditional diffusion. Despite these advances, most methods still instantiate a single map-ping from morphology to expression and treat genes as in-dependent targets, which can blur region-specific variability and limit generalization, especially to unseen genes. 

Zero-shot gene expression prediction. Recent stud-ies have begun to explore gene-level generalization be-yond fixed gene-indexed prediction heads. SGN [40] intro-duces semantic gene information to enable zero-shot predic-tion of unseen genes from histology, while other methods such as M2TGLGO[30], stImpute[42], and HEIST [20] ex-plore gene relationships, imputation, or gene-generalizable representation learning in spatial transcriptomics. These studies highlight the value of semantic or relational gene information, but differ substantially in task setting and input modality. TIGEM builds on this direction by combining PLM-based gene conditioning with topology-informed, boundary-aware graph propagation, jointly mod-eling unseen-gene transfer and spatial tissue context. 

Pathology vision models (PVMs). Pathology vision models aim to learn general-purpose representations of his-tology image patches that can be transferred to downstream tasks such as gene expression prediction and prognosis analysis. Leveraging large-scale histology datasets, mod-els such as UNI [2], CONCH [19], Virchow[33], and H-Optimus [29] are trained with self-supervised or multi-modal objectives. These foundation models capture rich morphological information that implicitly reflects cellular organization and molecular context, making them valuable for computational pathology and spatial omics applications. 2Figure 1. We train the model to predict log-transformed expression for spot i and gene g. The main supervision is a mean squared error (MSE) over all observed spots and genes. In addition, we use the topology-guided KL term from Sec-tion 3.2 to align attention distributions with the topology prior. The overall loss is 

L = LMSE + λtopo Ltopo , (23) where λtopo controls the strength of topology guidance. 

4. Experiments 

4.1. Datasets 

We selected three publicly available spatial transcriptomics datasets, named HEART, COAD, and MEND datasets, to do the benchmarking. Specifically, HEART [15] consists of 9 human heart tissue sections from independent samples, COAD [9] contains 13 human colorectal cancer samples, and MEND [6] includes 14 human prostate cancer sections. These datasets are also available in HEST-1k[13]. 

4.2. Experiment Setting 

All H&E slides are cropped into 224 ×224 patches centered at each spot. For all benchmark comparisons, TIGEM anal-yses, and ablation studies, we adopt leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) over all slides. In each fold, one slide is held for testing and the remaining slides are used for train-ing. We report the mean and standard deviation across folds. Across baselines, we select the top 500 genes from the intersection of highly expressed and highly variable genes (HEHVG) as targets, and apply log1p to all counts. Hyper-parameters are reported in the Supplementary ; we initialize from official settings and tune key hyperparameters on the validation split for fair comparison. 

4.3. Evaluation Metric 

We report mean squared error (MSE) and Pearson correla-tion coefficient (PCC), following prior work [4, 45]. PCC is evaluated at two scales, PCC(50) and PCC(200), calculating the average of top 50 and top 200 PCC values. Meanwhile, we also notice that MSE and PCC primarily measure vector-level agreement and may overlook spatial fidelity. In practice, predicted expression maps are often smoother than the ground truth [36, 45], making PCC less sensitive to gene spatial pattern mismatches, while MSE is dominated by magnitude differences and does not ex-plicitly assess spatial structure. To explicitly evaluate spa-tial pattern fidelity, we design Graph Spectral Correla-tion (GSC) in collaboration with biological experts. GSC is a graph signal processing metric that compares predic-tions and ground truth in the graph Fourier domain. Given the spot adjacency graph with the normalized Laplacian 

L = I − D−1/2AD −1/2 and eigenbasis U , we compute the cosine similarity between the truncated spectral coefficients that retain a proportion p of cumulative spectral energy: 

GSC( p) = ⟨ˆx(p)true , ˆx(p)pred ⟩∥ˆx(p)true ∥2 ∥ˆx(p)pred ∥2 + ε . (24) We report GSC@90% in the main results as a resolution-oriented metric. Take Fig. 2 as a justification, GSC@90% faithfully measures spatial pattern than PCC. Although Stem model achieves noticeably higher PCC than ST-Net and BLEEP, its predictions are overly smoothed, resulting in blurred boundaries and failure to recover the spatial pat-tern of KLK3. Thus it does not exhibit a corresponding ad-vantage under GSC@90%, highlighting the metric’s abil-ity to penalize spatial mismatches that PCC overlooks. 4.4. ST Expression Prediction 

Comparing with H&E-based ST prediction models. We first benchmarked our method with 11 published ST pre-diction models. As shown in Tab. 1, TIGEM consis-tently outperforms all baseline models across all evaluation metrics and achieves state-of-the-art performance on three datasets spanning distinct tissue types and pathological con-ditions. Benefiting from the incorporation of cell-level ge-ometric topology, TIGEM more effectively preserves spa-tial expression structure, leading to substantially higher GSC@90% scores compared with competing methods. Im-portantly, TIGEM also achieves the highest SSIM on all three datasets, independently corroborating the spatial im-provements observed with GSC@90% using an established spatial-structure metric. Since all histology sections orig-inate from different patients or sequencing batches, these results also suggest that TIGEM is robust to technical noise and generalizes well to unseen query slices. 

Evaluated TIGEM against recent pathology vision models (PVMs) . To assess whether TIGEM perfor-mance comes merely from strong histology embeddings, we included several open-source PVMs (Virchow2, UNI-2, CONCH, and GigaPath) as well as their combinations. We followed the HEST-1K protocol by training a random forest regressor on their patch embeddings. Across all datasets, TIGEM still surpasses these models and their combina-tions on all metrics, with a particularly large margin on the spatial-pattern metric GSC@90% (Tab. 1). This demon-strates that TIGEM predicts expression in a higher resolu-tion than using PVM embeddings alone, and that its frame-work substantially enhances the spatial organization aware-ness of existing pathology encoders. 

4.5. Biomarker Recovery 

One key application of spatial gene expression prediction is to uncover the relationship between disease progression and tissue morphology, which requires accurate biomarker estimation. We therefore evaluated TIGEM and the top eight baseline methods on four biomarker genes across both healthy H&E sections (HEART dataset) and cancer tissue (MEND dataset, prostate cancer). In MEND, we selected two established prostate cancer markers, KLK3[26, 31] and AZGP1[35]; in HEART, we evaluated the cardiomyocyte marker ANKRD1[1] and the epicardium marker DNC[38]. As shown in Fig. 2, TIGEM’s predictions exhibit strong alignment with the ground truth and closely overlap with pathology-annotated regions, producing sharply defined ex-pression boundaries. These results demonstrate TIGEM’s strong ability to capture spatially coherent biomarker pat-terns under topology-informed guidance. Additional visu-alizations are shown in Supplementary Material .

5. Conclusion 

We present TIGEM , a topology-informed graph attention framework with gene semantic modulation for histology-to-spatial transcriptomics prediction. TIGEM regularizes graph message passing with a topology-guided prior distribution to reduce cross-region over-smoothing, and decodes expression through a gene-conditioned GSMu head, enabling strict zero-shot inference for unseen genes by swapping in gene embeddings at test time. We also introduce GSC , a complementary graph-spectral metric for evaluating spatial-pattern fidelity beyond conventional spot-wise metrics. Across diverse ST datasets, TIGEM consistently outperforms existing methods under MSE, PCC, and GSC, while improvements in the established SSIM metric independently corroborate the spatial fidelity captured by GSC. Ablation studies further demonstrate consistent contributions from the topology barrier field, TopoKL, and GSMu, while zero-shot experiments show the potential to recover biologically relevant expression patterns for genes absent from training. Together, these results establish TIGEM as a generalizable framework that integrates topology-aware spatial modeling with gene-semantic conditioning for histology-to-ST prediction. 8References

11. Evaluation Metrics 

To assess the quality of spatial expression prediction, we employ three established metrics: the Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and Struc-tural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), together with our proposed Graph Spectral Correlation (GSC). 12.2. Biological Interpretation 

In spatial transcriptomics, expression level is not indepen-dent but spatially autocorrelated, forming morphologically distinct clusters of high or low expression. This spatial co-expression phenomenon often leads to different features at the tissue level. Spatial expression pattern refers to the sys-tematic distribution of a single gene across the spatial do-main of a tissue or sample. It stresses the regional feature of gene expression in space. This concept is used to describe the overall spatial organization of gene expression shaped by tissue structure, functional compartments, or local mi-croenvironments. Spatial expression patterns are largely shaped by tissue-scale spatial organization such as anatom-ical compartmentalization and developmental or patholog-ical boundaries, and thus reflect a global property of gene expression rather than a point-wise characteristic. Considering the overly strict point-wise matching im-posed by the definition of PCC across individual sequencing spots, its adaptability to spatial expression patterns is eas-ily weakened by local outliers. With reduced pattern infor-mation loss caused by local mismatches, our GSC stresses 7more in the quantification of spatial gene express pattern in sample level. Therefore, GSC more accurately reflects the global characteristics of spatial gene expression.
