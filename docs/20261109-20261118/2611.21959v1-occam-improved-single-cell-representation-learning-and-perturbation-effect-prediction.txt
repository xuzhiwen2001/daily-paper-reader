## Abstract 

Predicting the effects of genetic perturbations requires models that generalize be-yond observed cell contexts and gene combinations. We introduce O CCAM , a mul-tilayer perceptron (MLP) framework for representation learning and perturbation-effect prediction. Occam combines cell representations learned through denois-ing with gene interaction, text, and protein-sequence features. It achieves the highest aggregate representation scores for perturbation information and cell-type preservation with batch integration. In cross-context few-shot prediction, a per-turbation and a cell context are each observed during training, but never together. Occam better captures the measured response patterns for these unobserved com-binations than four retrained competitors or direct transfer of the same perturba-tion’s measured effects from other training cell lines. Without fine-tuning, Occam best distinguishes perturbations among the evaluated models and simple baselines in cell lines held out from predictor training, across seen and unseen assays. Pre-dicting expression levels accurately in these settings remains challenging. For double perturbations, which target two genes simultaneously, Occam composes learned single-gene responses to better capture the combined response and reduce expression error compared with competing models and summed single-gene ef-fects. These gains extend to both strongly additive and weakly additive responses. Together, these results support simple MLPs for cell representation learning and perturbation-effect prediction across contexts and gene combinations. 4

## 1 Introduction 

Predicting how cells respond to genetic perturbations could help prioritize experiments and charac-terize gene function. Single-cell perturbation screens measure RNA expression after targeted gene perturbations across many cells (Replogle et al., 2022; Nadig et al., 2025). Yet the space of possible experiments grows with the number of genes, cell types, and perturbation combinations. A useful predictor must therefore generalize to conditions not measured during training: a perturbation in another cell line, or a double perturbation that targets two genes simultaneously in the same cell. An encoder compresses an expression profile into features that support tasks such as identifying cell types or comparing perturbations. Predicting a response additionally requires learning how a specified perturbation changes that state. This relationship must be learned from separate control and perturbed populations, because RNA sequencing destroys the cell and prevents a before–after

Transformer models provide reusable cell representations (Cui et al., 2024). However, pretrained em-beddings and complex predictors do not consistently outperform simple alternatives for perturbation-effect prediction (Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025; Wenteler et al., 2025). We therefore examine a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that processes a fixed gene-expression vector and learns relationships among genes through reconstruction. OCCAM combines representation learning and perturbation-effect prediction in two stages. First, swap-in denoising reconstructs profiles after replacing selected gene values with measurements from another cell. Second, a predictor keeps the encoder frozen and uses gene interaction, text, and protein-sequence features to condition the encoded state on a perturbation. Its training objective supervises expression levels and the direction of change relative to controls. To predict double perturbations, we apply the same predictor successively to each target gene and average predictions from both orders. Across our benchmarks, Occam achieves the strongest overall representation performance for re-taining perturbation information and integrating cells across batches while preserving cell types. In few-shot perturbation-effect prediction, a perturbation and a cell line are each seen during training, but never together. Occam exceeds experimental reproducibility in mean agreement with reference delta profiles, which describe expression changes relative to controls. For double perturbations, Oc-cam improves agreement with reference delta profiles and prediction of mean expression levels over competing models and the additive baseline, which sums the measured effects of the two single-gene perturbations. In cell lines held out from predictor training, Occam distinguishes perturbations better than the evaluated models and simple baselines, across both seen and unseen assays, while expression-level accuracy remains limited. 

## 2 Related Work 

Cell representations. Transformer models, including scGPT, TranscriptFormer, STATE, AIDO.Cell, and TxFM, learn reusable cell features from expression data (Cui et al., 2024; Pearce et al., 2026; Adduri et al., 2026; Ho et al., 2024; Kenyon-Dean et al., 2026). Multilayer perceptron (MLP) approaches offer another route: scVI uses a variational model of expression, while scMAE reconstructs corrupted profiles and predicts which entries were replaced (Lopez et al., 2018; Fang et al., 2024). Occam builds on this denoising approach: replaced values come from a single donor cell, and reconstruction is supervised in normalized count space. We compare these nonlinear en-coders with principal component analysis (PCA), ridge-regularized PCA, and a linear autoencoder. These baselines test whether nonlinear features improve on linear representations, complementing studies of simple representations and predictors (Bendidi et al., 2024; Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025; Wenteler et al., 2025). 

Perturbation-effect prediction. Perturbation-effect predictors estimate the expression response to a specified intervention. TxPert uses gene interaction structure as prior biological informa-tion (Wenkel et al., 2026), while STATE learns a transformer-based transition between cellular states (Adduri et al., 2026). CellFlow and PerturbDiff model response distributions through con-ditional flow matching and functional diffusion, respectively (Klein et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026). We retrain these four methods for our prediction tasks. Occam conditions a frozen cell representa-tion on perturbation features, combining network neighborhoods with GenePT gene-text and ESM2 protein-sequence embeddings (Chen and Zou, 2025; Lin et al., 2023). The transfer baseline reuses the same perturbation’s measured effects from other training cell lines; sum of singles adds measured single-gene effects.

Swap-in denoising. We replace a random subset of gene values with measurements of the same genes in another cell. For replacement probability ρ, a binary mask mi ∼ Bernoulli( ρ)⊗G and a permutation π of cells within the minibatch define ˜xi = mi ⊙ ¯xπ(i) + ( 1 − mi) ⊙ ¯xi, where ⊙

denotes elementwise multiplication. All replaced entries come from the same donor cell. The model must therefore recover the recipient’s profile from a mixture of two measured profiles. An MLP encoder produces zi = fθ (˜ xi). A sigmoid head estimates the replacement mask, ˆmi = hψ (zi), and a decoder reconstructs expression from their concatenation, [zi; ˆ mi].

Reconstruction criterion. A softplus decoder output keeps predicted log-expression nonnegative. We invert the log transform with expm1 for both predicted and target profiles, then compute the negative-binomial (NB) reconstruction loss in normalized count space. Encoder pretraining com-bines this loss with binary cross-entropy for mask prediction. Appendix C.2 gives the training schedule, and Appendix B.1 derives the output parameterization. 

3.2 Perturbation-effect prediction 

The perturbation-effect predictor maps a control cell and a perturbation p to perturbed log-expression in context c, here a cell line. We freeze the pretrained cell encoder and train a perturbation encoder, modulation module, and expression decoder on independently sampled control and perturbed cells from that line. 

Perturbation features. We describe each target gene using neighborhoods in Gene Ontology (GO) and STRING networks, GenePT text embeddings, and ESM2 protein-sequence embeddings. Denot-ing these features by ap, tp, and sp, a shared MLP produces ep = qη ([ ap; ˜ tp; ˜ sp]) . Tildes denote normalization and scale matching of text and sequence features. Network neighbors remain binary adjacency features; the model performs no graph message passing. Appendix C.1 specifies prepro-cessing and missing-feature handling. 3Conditioning the cell state. The perturbation embedding modulates the frozen representation through multiplicative feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) (Perez et al., 2018). Let z = fθ (¯ xctrl ),let γ(e) be a sigmoid projection, and let ¯e be the mean perturbation embedding over the fixed target-gene vocabulary. We compute ˆz = z + [ γ(ep) − γ(¯ e)] ⊙ z. Subtracting the projection of the mean embedding centers the modulation on this reference; there is no additive FiLM shift. A trainable residual decoder with a softplus output predicts expression, Fp(¯ xctrl ) = dω (ˆ z). For non-targeting controls, we bypass modulation and decode z directly.

be training-population means of perturbed and control log-expression in context c. We minimize

denotes cosine similarity and λ∆ weights direction supervision. SmoothL1 compares the prediction with a sampled single-cell target; the cosine term aligns its change from controls with the measured mean effect. Both losses operate on decoded expression. Appendix C.2 gives the loss settings. 

Predicting double perturbations. To predict simultaneous perturbation of genes a and b, we compose the single-perturbation predictor on a log-expression input ¯x: Fa,b (¯ x) = 12 [Fb(Fa(¯ x)) + 

Fa(Fb(¯ x))] . Each step decodes an expression profile and re-encodes it for the next perturbation. Both orders use the same weights; averaging makes the prediction independent of gene order. Pa-rameter fitting uses single-gene perturbations. We use validation double perturbations to select checkpoints and a disjoint set of test double perturbations to assess generalization (Section 4.1; Appendix E.1). 

## 4 Experiments 

Representation evaluation measures information in embeddings of observed cells. Perturbation-effect prediction evaluates expression profiles generated for specified interventions. Prediction tests cover unobserved perturbation–cell-line combinations, cell lines held out from predictor training, and double perturbations that target two genes simultaneously. 

4.1 Training setup and data splits Representation pretraining. For the controlled representation comparison, encoders are pre-trained on DiverseRNA (Kenyon-Dean et al., 2026): approximately 1.4 million single-cell and bulk RNA-seq profiles from nine datasets. Occam reserves approximately 10% of profiles for valida-tion; studies and cell types can appear in both splits. Competitors use the same corpus with their own preprocessing and objectives. We also evaluate public checkpoints pretrained on other corpora. Appendices C.2 and D.1–D.5 detail training. 

Perturbation-effect prediction. We train separate predictors on two CRISPR perturbation datasets. RHJ combines CRISPR interference screens, which knock down target-gene expression, in RPE1 (retinal epithelial), HepG2 (liver-derived), and Jurkat (T-cell-derived) cell lines (Replogle et al., 2022; Nadig et al., 2025). We use 103 held-out HepG2 gene perturbations for validation and a disjoint set of 611 for testing. The model trains on other perturbations in HepG2 and on the held-out perturbation identities in Jurkat and/or RPE1. Thus, both the perturbation and the target cell line are observed during training, but their specific combination is not. We call this the cross-context few-shot setting; no training cells receive a held-out perturbation in HepG2. 

Norman profiles CRISPR activation in the leukemia-derived K562 cell line (Norman et al., 2019). Single perturbations activate one gene; double perturbations activate two genes simultaneously in the same cell. Training uses 105 single-gene perturbation conditions (47,269 cells) and 9,702 non-targeting control cells. We reserve 20 double perturbations (5,276 cells) for validation and a disjoint set of 111 double perturbations (28,921 cells) for testing. Both constituent single-gene perturbations are present in training. Double-perturbation cells are used only for checkpoint selection and testing, with no gradient updates. On each perturbation dataset, Occam keeps the pretrained cell encoder frozen and trains the pertur-bation modules and expression decoder with five random seeds. Each run retains the epoch with the 4highest validation Pearson ∆, averaged over validation conditions. For Norman, we predict each validation and test double perturbation by applying the single-gene predictor in both orders (gene a

then b, and b then a) and averaging the two resulting expression profiles (Section 3.2). Appendix C.2 gives schedules and loss weights. 

4.2 Cell-representation evaluation 

The perturbation representation benchmark measures information retained in frozen cell embeddings using HepG2, Jurkat, and RPE1 (Bendidi et al., 2024). All three cell lines and their benchmark data are absent from DiverseRNA (Kenyon-Dean et al., 2026) and are therefore entirely unseen during encoder pretraining in the controlled comparison. We keep the model frozen and use linear probing for some metrics. Tasks assess perturbation identity, gene relationships, and expression information across batches. Each cell-line score averages six tasks; the overall score averages the three cell lines. The scIB cell-integration benchmark (Luecken et al., 2022) tests whether embeddings mix cells from different technical batches while preserving cell types. We use pancreatic cells (Pancreas) and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC-68k) (Segerstolpe et al., 2016; Grün et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2017). Each dataset score weights biological conservation by 0.6 and batch integration by 0.4; the overall score averages the two datasets equally. Appendices H.1–H.3 define metrics and preprocessing; Appendix H.4 describes an additional gene-embedding phenotype benchmark. 

4.3 Perturbation-effect prediction tasks Generalization across cell lines and assays. We evaluate three settings to test generalization to new perturbation–cell-line combinations and to new cell lines, and to assess prediction performance when the assay also changes. Unseen cell line, seen assay: K562. We evaluate 528 perturbations in the Replogle K562 leukemia-cell knockdown screen (Replogle et al., 2022), which shares the assay family used for RHJ RPE1. 3. Unseen cell line and assay: H1. We evaluate 80 perturbations in the Virtual Cell Challenge H1 human embryonic stem-cell screen (Arc Institute, 2025). We apply the RHJ-trained predictors to K562 and H1 without fine-tuning. We also evaluate K562 on the same 80 gene perturbations as H1. This tests generalization across cell lines and assays without differences in which genes are perturbed (Appendix E.1). 

Held-out double perturbations. We evaluate all 111 held-out double perturbations in the Norman activation dataset. For each, we compare the measured effect with the sum of the two measured single-gene effects. We group double perturbations by how strongly they change gene expression, then select responses closest to and furthest from this sum within each group. This yields 36 strongly additive and 36 weakly additive double perturbations, with small and large expression changes in both subsets. 4.4 Perturbation-effect prediction: comparators and scoring 

We retrain TxPert (Wenkel et al., 2026), STATE (Adduri et al., 2026), CellFlow (Klein et al., 2025), and PerturbDiff (Yuan et al., 2026) on RHJ and Norman with five seeds, using the same evaluation conditions, genes, control draws, and scorer. 

Simple baselines. Control cell returns an observed control-cell profile; control mean returns the average control-cell profile. Mean perturbation adds the average training effect to a control profile, regardless of the target gene. Transfer adds the same perturbation’s effect measured in other training cell lines to a control profile from the evaluation cell line. For double perturbations, sum of singles 

adds the two measured single-gene effects to a control profile. Appendices E.2–E.3 detail baselines and training. 

Split-half scoring for perturbation-effect prediction. Within each test perturbation, we divide measured cells into disjoint, equally sized halves A and B, stratified by batch; controls are split separately. Half A is the common reference. Comparing measured perturbed cells in B with those in A gives experimental reproducibility . Models receive B controls and the perturbation identity, and generate as many perturbed profiles as there are measured cells in each half. These predictions are also compared with A, not with B’s perturbed cells. For both model evaluation and experimental reproducibility, an expression-change profile, or delta 

(∆), is the mean perturbed profile minus the mean control profile. The common reference uses A’s control mean. Measured B profiles and model predictions share the same measured B-control mean, keeping their scoring conditions matched. We use separate A and B controls because subtracting the same estimated control mean from both delta profiles introduces shared sampling noise that can inflate Pearson ∆ (Nicol et al., 2026; Fukutani et al., 2026). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are likewise identified against A controls for the reference, and against B controls for both measured B cells and predictions. DEG scoring then compares each resulting gene list with A’s reference list. With more cells, smaller effects can become statistically significant, changing which genes are called DEGs. Matching cell counts and scored genes therefore prevents sample-size differences from be-ing mistaken for differences in prediction quality. ## 5 Results 

5.1 Cell-representation quality 

Occam leads both aggregate representation scores in Table 1: 39.31 for perturbation information and 53.72 for scIB cell integration. Across all evaluated encoders and baselines, it leads on three of the five datasets: RPE1, HepG2, and Pancreas. TxFM leads on Jurkat; on PBMC-68k, scVI leads among models trained on DiverseRNA, while the public scGPT checkpoint has the highest score overall. Public checkpoints use different pretraining corpora containing approximately 33 to 167 million cells (Cui et al., 2024; Adduri et al., 2026), compared with DiverseRNA’s 1.4 million expression profiles, so the public-checkpoint comparison reflects differences in both data and architecture. 7Simple representations also perform well. Ridge-PCA reaches 35.51 on perturbation representa-tion, ahead of scGPT, scMAE, STATE-SE, and scVI trained on DiverseRNA. Occam improves on Ridge-PCA by 3.80 points and exceeds TxFM’s 38.78; the perturbation-score improvement over each comparator is significant ( p < 0.01 , n = 5 ). On scIB, the linear autoencoder is the closest comparator at 52.67, only 1.05 points behind Occam. Occam’s margin over the strongest simple baseline is therefore larger on the perturbation benchmark. Tables 19–23 give task-level scores. 

5.2 Perturbation-effect prediction across cell lines Consistent recovery of perturbation-specific responses. Across all three cell-line settings, Oc-cam has the highest mean Pearson ∆ among the five trained models and the highest mean L1 discrim-ination among all nine methods, including the four simple baselines (Figure 2). Occam therefore better captures the relative expression changes across genes than the other trained models and better distinguishes perturbations than all other models and simple baselines. Across the four metrics, Oc-cam leads in 8 of 12 comparisons among trained models and 5 of 12 including simple baselines. All model and baseline rankings exclude experimental reproducibility. 

Unobserved combinations in a familiar cell line. On RHJ/HepG2, Occam leads the trained mod-els and simple baselines on Pearson ∆ and L1 discrimination. Its Pearson ∆ is 0.3999 ± 0.0035 ,compared with 0.3438 ± 0.0092 for TxPert and 0.2332 for transferring the same perturbation’s mea-sured effects from other training cell lines. The discrimination gain is particularly clear: 0.761 versus 0.668 for STATE, the next-best method. Overlap@N is close to the best score (0.086 versus STATE’s 0.089), while MAE is 0.0269 compared with TxPert’s best value of 0.0228. Together, these results support stronger recovery of perturbation-specific response patterns with competitive DEG recov-ery and mean-expression accuracy. Its mean Pearson ∆ also exceeds experimental reproducibility (0.3999 versus 0.3722). 

Cell lines held out from predictor training. Without fine-tuning, Occam leads trained models on three metrics in both K562 and H1. On K562, where the assay is familiar, it leads on Pearson 

∆, L1 discrimination, and DEG recovery. On H1, where the assay is also unseen, it leads on Pear-son ∆, MAE, and discrimination, with DEG recovery close to TxPert’s best score (0.325 versus 0.339). Occam’s discrimination exceeds every simple baseline: 0.749 on K562 and 0.644 on H1, compared with 0.709 and 0.569 for transfer of the same perturbation’s measured effects from other training cell lines. This transfer baseline has higher Pearson ∆ in both settings, while control mean has lower MAE. All five deep learning models have high H1 MAE (0.1423–0.1996 versus 0.0137 for control mean), consistent with difficulty predicting expression levels under the combined cell-line and assay shift. Their positive Pearson ∆ scores nevertheless indicate partial recovery of the relative expression changes across genes. Occam captures these response patterns best among the trained models and most clearly distinguishes perturbations, despite the shared difficulty predicting expression levels. Matching target genes helps interpret the difference between K562 and H1. On the separate 80-perturbation K562 panel, Occam’s Pearson ∆ is 0.2033 ± 0.0138 , versus 0.1071 ± 0.0057 for the same targets in H1. With the perturbation list held fixed, the lower H1 score is consistent with difficulty applying the predictor to an unseen assay, although differences in cell line, measured genes, and assay conditions prevent isolating the assay effect (Table 5; Appendix E.1). 

5.3 Perturbation-effect prediction for double perturbations Consistent gains in Pearson ∆ and MAE. On Norman K562 double perturbations, Occam leads the trained models and simple baselines on Pearson ∆ and MAE in the full test set and both additivity subsets (Figure 3). It has the best mean in 8 of 12 comparisons among trained models and 6 of 12 including simple baselines. The full set contains both subsets, so these are descriptive counts over overlapping evaluations. Across all 111 test doubles, Occam improves on sum of singles in Pearson

## 6 Conclusion 

Occam establishes simple MLPs as a strong foundation for cell representation learning and perturbation-effect prediction. More complex architectures should be assessed by the gains in gen-eralization they provide across biological conditions. The central challenge is to learn how pertur-bation effects depend on cellular context, so that predictions remain useful beyond the experimental systems used for training. Broader coverage of cell contexts and perturbations should be a priority in both training and eval-uation. Measuring the same perturbations across diverse contexts would help distinguish shared responses from context-specific effects. Matching perturbations and cell contexts across assays would help distinguish biological effects from technical variation. Training on this diversity should be accompanied by tests on unseen contexts, target genes, and perturbation combinations, includ-ing across assays. The next step is to establish how reliably models generalize across this wider experimental space, and whether their predictions can guide the choice of new experiments. 9References

12 A Model limitations 

Occam’s encoder uses a fixed gene vocabulary. Applying it to another dataset requires matching gene identifiers and setting unmeasured genes to zero. The perturbation-effect predictor receives individual control cells, but the reported results mainly assess population-level effects. They do not establish accurate predictions of how a particular cell would respond or how responses vary be-tween cells. GenePT, ESM2, and the gene networks supply prior information beyond the expression training data, and their coverage varies between genes. Norman evaluates double perturbations whose constituent single-gene responses are observed during training. We do not evaluate unseen target genes, perturbations of more than two genes, or chemical perturbations. K562 and H1 differ in both cell line and measurement conditions, so their comparison cannot isolate the effect of either factor. Transferring measured effects from other training cell lines gives higher Pearson ∆ than Occam in held-out cell lines, and no model leads on every metric. The five training seeds measure variation for the datasets and protocol studied here; the five evaluation splits per seed are not independent training runs. Validation in new prospective experiments remains future work.

## C Implementation details 

C.1 Perturbation features and cell-state modulation 

The perturbation-effect predictor maps a control cell and a target gene to perturbed log-expression. The pretrained cell encoder is frozen and has layer widths 44 ,728 → 2048 → 1024 → 512 . The perturbation encoder represents 18,216 target genes. For each gene, we retain its top 20 neighbors in the Gene Ontology (GO) and STRING networks, make the connections symmetric, and encode each connection as a binary value. We add no self-connections and do not normalize by the num-ber of neighbors. We concatenate these network indicators with a 3,072-dimensional GenePT text embedding and a 1,280-dimensional ESM2 protein-sequence embedding. The source embeddings remain fixed; the perturbation encoder and the vectors used for missing embeddings are trainable. 14 Source preprocessing. We align GenePT and ESM2 embeddings to the target-gene vocabulary and standardize each embedding dimension over genes with an available embedding. We then scale each source so that its average squared vector norm matches that of the network indicators. Let EA

be the mean squared norm of nonzero network vectors and Ek the mean squared norm of available, standardized embeddings from source k. We multiply those embeddings by sk = √EA/E k when both quantities are positive; otherwise, sk = 1 . This keeps the sources on comparable scales while preserving differences in the number of network neighbors. Genes without an embedding receive a shared trainable vector for that source, initialized to zero. Their network indicators remain available. An MLP maps the concatenated vector through Linear–ReLU–Dropout–Linear layers, with hidden width 128, output width 128, and dropout 0.2. It uses the network indicators directly, with no graph message passing. Let ¯e be the mean perturbation embedding over all target genes in the fixed vocabulary. A projection g with widths 128 → 256 → 512 and an identity hidden activation modulates the cell representation as 

ˆz = z + [ σ(g(ep)) − σ(g(¯ e))] ⊙ z. (13) Here, σ is the sigmoid function. This multiplicative feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) has scale one and no additive shift. Subtracting the projection of the mean perturbation embedding centers the modulation on that reference. Non-targeting controls bypass modulation. The expression decoder has hidden width 2048, four residual blocks with squeeze-and-excitation (SE) channel gating, SE reduction factor 16, dropout 0.3, and a softplus output. Reconstruction pretraining initializes its weights, which are then updated during perturbation-effect prediction training. 

C.2 Training stages and objective Corpus and validation split. DiverseRNA (Kenyon-Dean et al., 2026) contains 1,434,299 ex-pression profiles from nine public datasets, including seven single-cell datasets and bulk RNA-seq from GTEx and TCGA. Table 1 and Section 3 of Kenyon-Dean et al. (2026) describe the sources and curation. Occam uses a 44,728-gene input vocabulary. HepG2, Jurkat, and RPE1 and their representation-benchmark data are absent from DiverseRNA and are therefore unseen during en-coder pretraining. The encoder remains frozen throughout representation evaluation. For encoder pretraining, we divide the ordered corpus into groups of at most 8,192 profiles, shuffle the groups with seed 42, and reserve ⌊0.1K⌋ of the K groups for validation. This assigns approx-imately 10% of profiles to validation. Training and validation contain different profiles, but can include the same studies and cell types. Validation measures reconstruction, separately from down-stream perturbation evaluation. Representation baselines use their own preprocessing and training splits (Appendices D.1–D.5). 

Pretraining and reconstruction-decoder initialization. We normalize each DiverseRNA profile to a total count of 2,000 and apply log(1 + ·). Encoder pretraining combines reconstruction in normalized count space with binary cross-entropy (BCE) for mask prediction. The encoder receives 100 epochs of negative-binomial (NB) pretraining with swap probability 0.1, learning rate 4 × 10 −4,batch size 1024, and mask-loss weight increasing from 0.7 to 0.9. This stage uses Adam and a cosine learning-rate schedule. It is followed by 100 epochs of Poisson reconstruction training at learning rate 6 × 10 −4, with gradually less input corruption and a mask-loss weight decreasing from 0.7 to zero. To initialize the perturbation-effect decoder, we keep the encoder frozen and train a separate expression decoder with NB reconstruction, learning rate 5 × 10 −5, batch size 1024, and a 200-epoch schedule. We use its epoch-124 checkpoint.

The control cell is the input; the perturbed cell is the reconstruction target. Predicting double perturbations. A double perturbation targets genes a and b simultaneously. Each single-perturbation predictor Fp includes encoding, modulation, and decoding. We apply the two single-perturbation predictors in both orders and average the resulting expression profiles: 

Fa,b (x) = 12 [Fb(Fa(x)) + Fa(Fb(x))] . (17) After the first prediction, the decoder output is passed back to the encoder as log-expression, without a second count normalization. Each order therefore uses two encoder and decoder passes. Both or-ders share the same weights. Averaging their final expression profiles makes the double-perturbation prediction independent of gene order.

The model uses a bidirectional transformer encoder with L = 16 layers, hidden dimension dmodel = 512 , feed-forward dimension dffn = 3584 , and 8 attention heads, giving a head dimension of 64 . The output embedding dimension is dout = 512 . Each transformer block uses post-normalization, with residual connections followed by LayerNorm, and GELU activations in the feed-forward sub-layer. Strongly and weakly additive double perturbations. We compare each measured double-perturbation effect with the sum of the two measured single-gene effects. E.2 Split-half scoring for perturbation-effect prediction 

This protocol evaluates perturbation-effect prediction against experimental reproducibility using the same cell counts, genes, and reference populations. Cell-representation evaluation is separate: the encoder stays frozen, and linear probes are trained for some metrics (Appendices H.1–H.3). With more cells, smaller effects can become statistically significant, changing which genes are called dif-ferentially expressed genes (DEGs). Comparing many generated profiles with fewer experimental profiles could therefore change DEG scores simply because of sample size. We match the reference cells, the number of profiles per perturbation, the scored genes, and the scoring method. This re-moves the difference in sample size; it does not establish that model outputs reproduce biological variability or are independent experimental replicates. 

Splitting measured cells into A and B. The training, validation, and test assignments determine which perturbations are evaluated. We then use five random seeds to split the measured cells of each test perturbation into two halves. For perturbation p with np cells, each half contains mp = ⌊np/2⌋

cells. We shuffle cells within each batch and assign them alternately to A and B, continuing the alternation across batches. If np is odd, we randomly remove one cell from the larger half. Controls are split separately using the same procedure. A and B share no cells within a split, although different splits reuse cells. Splitting within batches reduces batch imbalance, but cannot guarantee identical batch proportions when few cells are available. Each perturbation has the same cell count across splits and methods. 

Common reference for model evaluation and experimental reproducibility. Half A contains the measured perturbed cells and controls used as the common reference. Experimental reproducibil-ity compares measured B perturbed cells with this reference. Models receive B controls sampled uniformly with replacement, with one control per predicted profile. Their predictions are also com-pared with A, not with measured B perturbed cells. Both measured B and each model provide exactly mp perturbed profiles for perturbation p, matching A’s cell count and scored genes. Individ-ual cells are not paired across halves. All methods use the same evaluation splits and random seeds for sampling controls. For scoring, both measured B perturbed cells and each model’s predictions are evaluated using the complete set of measured B controls. This set can differ from the sampled controls given to a model as inputs. Model evaluation and experimental reproducibility therefore share exactly the same B controls and the same A reference. 

Delta profiles and differential expression. An expression-change profile, or delta ( ∆), is a mean perturbed profile minus its corresponding mean control profile. Write ¯xA,p and ¯xA, 0 for the mean profiles of perturbation p and controls in A, with analogous notation for B. The common reference delta is ∆A,p = ¯ xA,p − ¯xA, 0. For experimental reproducibility, the measured B delta is ∆B,p =

23 ¯xB,p − ¯xB, 0. For a model, the predicted delta is ̂ ∆B,p = ˆ xB,p − ¯xB, 0. Pearson ∆ compares either 

∆B,p or ̂ ∆B,p with ∆A,p across genes. A’s control mean is therefore part of the common reference in both comparisons; B’s control mean is shared by measured B and model predictions. We identify DEGs by comparing perturbed profiles with the corresponding controls. The reference compares A perturbed cells with A controls. Experimental reproducibility compares B perturbed cells with B controls; model evaluation compares predicted profiles with those same B controls. Both resulting DEG lists are scored against A’s DEG list. Cell-eval 0.6.0 (Arc Institute, nd), using pdex 0.1.24 for differential expression, computes these comparisons in log-normalized expression without post-hoc calibration. A’s DEG results are computed once and reused for every comparison within that split. 

Why the two halves use separate controls. Experimental reproducibility measures agreement when both perturbed cells and controls are independently sampled into disjoint halves. Subtracting one estimated control mean from both delta profiles introduces the same sampling noise into both vectors. This shared noise can inflate Pearson ∆ even when predictions contain little perturbation-specific information (Nicol et al., 2026; Fukutani et al., 2026). We use separate A and B controls to avoid this source of spurious correlation, while reusing B controls between model evaluation and experimental reproducibility. Both comparisons use the same A reference; within each comparison, the two deltas use distinct control samples. 

Interpretation of experimental reproducibility. Experimental reproducibility measures agree-ment between two samples of cells receiving the same perturbation, at the cell counts used for eval-uation. Both measured means contain sampling noise, while a model’s estimate of the population mean can have lower variance. Experimental reproducibility is therefore not a strict performance ceiling. Exceeding it on one metric does not establish that a model is more accurate than the ex-periment. Scores should be compared within each evaluation set: changing the perturbations or measured genes can also change the scores. 

Control mean and control cell. 

Control mean returns the mean B-control profile for every perturbation. Control cell returns a sampled B-control profile. Neither baseline adds a perturbation effect. 

Mean perturbation. 

Add the mean training perturbation profile minus the B-control mean to each sampled B control. Training profiles are averaged with equal weight per perturbation. For K562 and H1, we first add the mean HepG2 training delta to the mean of all controls from the evaluation cell line. We then subtract the B-control mean and add the result to each sampled B control. The prediction therefore includes the mean training delta and a small difference between the full control mean and the B-control mean. It does not depend on which gene is perturbed. 

Transfer. 

Add the same perturbation’s measured effect from other training cell lines to each sampled B control from the evaluation cell line. The source effects are weighted by their cell counts. This baseline uses perturbation identity but does not learn how the effect changes between cell lines. 

Sum of singles. 

Add the two observed single-gene effects to each sampled B control; this is the additive baseline for double perturbations. 

Experimental reproducibility. 

Compare measured half-B perturbed cells with the common half-A reference, using B and A controls, respectively. This is a measured comparison, not a predictive model or a performance ceiling. E.4 Metrics and uncertainty 

A mean profile here averages log-normalized expression over cells receiving the same perturbation. Its delta subtracts the corresponding mean control profile, as defined in Appendix E.2. For model evaluation, Pearson ∆ compares the predicted delta with A’s reference delta across genes. For experimental reproducibility, it compares the measured B delta with A’s reference delta. Pearson ∆

measures agreement in the relative expression changes across genes, not calibrated effect magnitude. Mean absolute error (MAE) compares mean log-normalized expression levels with A; lower values are better. L1 discrimination ranks A’s perturbations by L1 distance to the predicted or measured B delta and scores the rank of the correct perturbation. Chance performance is 0.5. Overlap@N measures recovery of A’s reference DEGs. Cell-eval identifies DEGs using Wilcoxon tests with a false-discovery-rate threshold of 0.05. For each perturbation, N is the number of signif-icant genes in A. Significant genes in the measured B or model comparison are ranked by absolute fold change; the top N are compared with A’s N DEGs. The score is the number of shared genes divided by N . If fewer than N genes are significant in the measured B or model comparison, only those available genes can contribute. If either list is empty, the scorer returns zero. The same rule applies to model evaluation and experimental reproducibility. Statistical significance depends on both cell count and variation between cells. Matching cell counts makes the comparison fairer, but Overlap@N alone does not establish that predictions reproduce the full distribution of measured responses. 31 G Training compute and inference throughput 

This section estimates training compute for cell-representation models and measures cell-encoder inference throughput. 

G.1 Estimating training compute 

We estimate training compute analytically from each model architecture and training configuration. We count each multiplication and addition as one floating-point operation (FLOP), following the convention used in neural scaling-law analyses (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). For a linear layer with input dimension din and output dimension dout , the forward cost is 

Clinear = 2 din dout (34) FLOPs per input vector. For token-based models, this cost is multiplied by the sequence length S

when the same projection is applied independently to each token. Thus, the FLOPs of input projec-tions, MLP/feed-forward blocks, output projections, and decoder heads are obtained by summing the corresponding linear-layer costs over all layers. For transformer-based models, we additionally include the quadratic self-attention cost. In a trans-former layer with sequence length S and hidden dimension dmodel , the attention matrix computation 

QK ⊤ costs approximately 2S2dmodel FLOPs per sample, and the attention-weighted value aggre-gation costs another 2S2dmodel FLOPs. Softmax and normalization have lower computational cost than these matrix multiplications and are not included. The attention contribution is therefore esti-mated as 

Cattn = 4 S2dmodel (35) FLOPs per transformer layer and per sample. The total forward cost per sample, denoted Cfwd , is obtained by summing all linear, attention, and decoder-head contributions in the forward pass. We approximate the backward pass as twice the forward-pass cost, accounting for gradients with respect to both activations and parameters. The total training cost is therefore 

Ctrain = 3 Cfwd D, (36) where D is the total number of training examples processed over all epochs, i.e. 

D = nprofiles × nepochs . (37) Using more GPUs can change training time, but does not change this estimated number of operations. For MLP-only models without attention, the same estimate reduces approximately to 

Ctrain ≈ 6P D, (38) where P is the number of trainable parameters involved in dense matrix multiplications. This follows because the forward pass of a dense MLP costs approximately 2P FLOPs per sample, and forward plus backward training costs approximately three times the forward pass.

Perturbation-effect prediction datasets. Appendix E.1 describes the RHJ, K562, H1, and Nor-man datasets, including which perturbations and cell lines are held out and which genes are scored. The representation benchmarks above assess information in observed-cell embeddings, separately from prediction of perturbed expression. 

H.2 Perturbation representation benchmark 

We evaluate six tasks on frozen cell embeddings from each of the three perturbation datasets. Tasks 1 and 2 use Typical Variance Normalization (TVN), task 3 uses embeddings centered and scaled using controls, and tasks 4–6 use the encoder outputs directly. Within each benchmark run, stochastic probes use seeds 42 and 43, and their scores are averaged. These probe seeds are separate from the five repetitions summarized in the main representation table. 

Embedding post-processing. Raw embeddings are the encoder outputs. To center and scale them, we estimate the mean and standard deviation of each embedding dimension from control cells within each batch, then apply that transformation to all cells in the batch. TVN adds three steps: PCA fitted on controls, a second centering and scaling in PCA space, and CORAL whitening within each batch. The whitening transformation uses the inverse square root of the regularized control covariance matrix to adjust correlations between embedding dimensions. 

Task 1: Gene relationship recall. We average cell embeddings within each perturbation and compute cosine similarities between the resulting perturbation embeddings. We then compare these similarities with known gene relationships in CORUM, HuMAP, Reactome, SIGNOR, and STRING. For each database, recall is the fraction of known gene pairs whose similarity falls in the extreme tails of the full similarity distribution. The score averages recall across the five databases and is scaled to [0 , 100] .

Task 2: Perturbation consistency. This task asks whether cells receiving the same perturbation have more similar embeddings than expected by chance. We compare each cell with the mean embedding of the other cells receiving that perturbation, using cosine similarity. Randomly sampled cells provide the comparison expected by chance. The score is the fraction of perturbations with significant consistency at p ≤ 0.01 , scaled to [0 , 100] . Non-targeting controls are excluded from the final score. 34 Task 3: iLISI batch integration. For each perturbation with sufficient cells, iLISI measures batch mixing on the k-nearest-neighbor graph of cell embeddings. Higher values indicate that cells receiv-ing the same perturbation have neighbors from several batches. The score averages over qualifying perturbations and is scaled to [0 , 100] .

Task 4: KNN separability. We train a weighted k-nearest-neighbor (KNN) classifier to predict perturbation labels from frozen cell embeddings. The test-seen set contains the same perturbation labels in held-out batches. The score averages top-1 and top-5 classification accuracy. 

Task 5: Linear separability. We train a linear classifier to predict perturbation labels from frozen cell embeddings. Control cells are excluded from training and evaluation. The score averages top-1 and top-5 classification accuracy on the test-seen set. 

Task 6: Linear invertibility. We train a decoder to reconstruct gene expression from frozen cell embeddings and evaluate it on held-out perturbations in held-out batches. The score averages two measures: per-gene Spearman correlation between reconstructed and measured expression, and structural transcriptome distance, which assesses preservation of expression structure after center-ing within batches. The combined score is scaled to [0 , 100] .

Aggregation. Each task produces a score in [0 , 100] . For each dataset, the perturbation represen-tation score is the arithmetic mean of the six task scores. The overall perturbation representation benchmark score is the arithmetic mean of the three per-dataset scores. 

H.3 scIB cell-integration benchmark 

We compute ten metrics from the scIB framework (Luecken et al., 2022) on each of the two cell-type datasets. We construct a k-nearest-neighbor graph from the embeddings using Euclidean distance. Leiden clustering is performed at the resolution that maximizes agreement with reference labels. 

Biological conservation metrics. The biological conservation score averages six metrics. ASW label measures cell-type separation using average silhouette width. Normalized mutual infor-mation (NMI) and adjusted Rand index (ARI) measure agreement between Leiden clusters and reference cell-type annotations. Isolated-label ASW and isolated-label F1 evaluate whether rare or batch-isolated cell types remain distinguishable. cLISI measures cell-type neighborhood purity. 

Batch correction metrics. The batch correction score averages four metrics. ASW batch measures batch mixing within cell types. Graph connectivity measures whether cells of the same type re-main connected across batches. iLISI measures batch mixing in the neighborhood graph. PCR batch 

measures the reduction in batch-associated variance explained by principal components. 

Aggregation. The biological conservation score is the arithmetic mean of the six biological met-rics, and the batch correction score is the arithmetic mean of the four batch metrics. The per-dataset scIB score is

H.4 Gene-representation evaluation with phenotype probes 

We evaluate gene embeddings using the phenotype probes of Cole et al. (2026). Each probe is trained to predict a gene’s knockdown phenotype from its embedding. The probe receives only the gene embedding, which may include information from control cells. This differs from the full perturbation-effect predictor, which receives a control cell and a perturbation. The gene benchmark uses five-fold cross-validation over target genes, separately from split-half evaluation of perturbation-effect prediction. 35 Experimental data. The benchmark uses CRISPR-based single-gene knockdown experiments across three human cell lines: RPE1, HepG2, and Jurkat. For each cell line, approximately 2,386 

genes are individually perturbed, and transcriptomic responses are measured with single-cell RNA sequencing. Non-targeting guide cells serve as the unperturbed control population for each cell line.
