Abstract 

Virtual cell models aim to predict the effects of genetic, environmental, chemical and other perturbations in silico, reducing the dependency on complex and costly experiments. The Virtual Cell Challenge 2026 motivates the field toward this goal, tasking models with zero-shot prediction of the transcriptional responses to CRISPRi knockdowns in six human cell lines. The faithfulness of a model to the experiment is assessed by metrics that compare its predictions with the real data, and designing such metrics is not trivial. We developed the metrics of the 2026 Challenge around three principles: a metric should (i) reflect the biological information extracted from experiments, (ii) be calibrated on a repeat of the experiment, and (iii) resist shortcut solutions that raise the score without predicting the response. Here we present the six resulting metrics, the reasoning behind each of them, and their evaluations on the Challenge data. Two of the metrics compare the pseudobulk expression profiles of predicted and measured cells, while four assess the genes that respond to each perturbation, the central readout of a Perturb-seq experiment. The metrics are implemented in cell-eval2, an open-source Python package. On the Challenge data, all six metrics score a repeat of the experiment above the mean baseline, and the shortcut solutions that the metrics of the 2025 Challenge left open can no longer raise the score. The new metrics are therefore more robust than the previous ones. 

# 1. Introduction 

Virtual cell models aim to predict how a cell responds to a perturbation where this response was never measured, especially where such measurements are not yet feasible. They become valuable to human health once they predict the response in humans at least as well as the best current preclinical models do. While cellular responses to perturbations include changes in many biological layers (for example, in the epigenome, transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome), here we focus on the changes in the transcriptome, as measured by Perturb-seq. Perturb-seq combines pooled CRISPR perturbations with single-cell RNA 1

sequencing and measures the responses to thousands of perturbations in one experiment (Replogle et al., 2022; Nadig et al., 2025). Such data provide both the training data and the ground truth for these models. The Virtual Cell Challenge was launched to compare these models within one evaluation framework, on datasets generated for this purpose, and to accelerate their development (Roohani et al., 2025). For the 2026 Challenge, participants had to provide zero-shot predictions of the responses to CRISPR-interference (CRISPRi) knockdowns in six human cell lines (Arc Institute, 2026a; Arc Virtual Cell Initiative Team and Goodarzi, 2026). Assessing predictive models depends heavily on the metrics that compare the predictions with experimental measurements, and designing these metrics is not trivial. Single-cell measurements are noisy, so even a repeat of the experiment does not reproduce the measured profiles exactly. A metric that ignores this noise can therefore rank a no-change prediction (i.e. the control cells) above a repeat of the experiment. In addition, a metric can respond to properties of a prediction that are unrelated to the biology of the response, such as the scale of the predicted values. In a competition, such weaknesses matter even more, since the participants optimize the score as part of their design goals. Any part of the score that can be gained without predicting the response invites what we call “shortcut solutions”. We borrow the term from the “shortcut learning” of machine learning models, which can learn decision rules that perform well on standard benchmarks but fail to transfer to more challenging conditions (Geirhos et al., 2020). Recent benchmarks showed how strongly the conclusions about these models depend on their evaluation. Deep-learning and foundation models of single cells did not outperform deliberately simple baselines, such as the mean of the training examples or a linear model, in predicting the responses to genetic perturbations on conventional metrics (Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025; Csendes et al., 2025). Common metrics also overestimate performance because they are susceptible to the systematic differences between perturbed and control cells (Viñas Torné et al., 2026). Other studies introduced rank-based metrics (Wu et al., 2024) and showed how the distance and the scaling of the data change the perturbation discrimination score (Liu et al., 2025). Proposed evaluation principles include calibrating metrics against empirical reference points (Vollenweider and Bühlmann, 2026), such as a lower reference and a positive reference made by splitting the cells of the experiment (Schäfer et al., 2026). The 2025 Challenge scored the predictions with three metrics: the perturbation discrimination with the L1 distance, the mean absolute error of the expression profiles, and the overlap of the predicted and measured differentially expressed (DE) genes (Roohani et al., 2025). However, these metrics left room for shortcut solutions (Section 2.3), and the mean absolute error scores a repeat of the experiment worse than a no-change prediction (Section 4.2). For the 2026 Challenge, we redesigned the metrics, guided by three principles: a metric should (i) reflect the biological information extracted from experiments, (ii) be calibrated on a repeat of the experiment, and (iii) resist shortcut solutions. We present the six metrics that score the 2026 Virtual Cell Challenge, the reasoning behind each of them, and their values on the Challenge datasets. Two of them compare expression profiles (perturbation discrimination and expression error), and four compare the DE genes (fold-change error, significant-set agreement, direction fidelity yield and direction reach). We implemented these metrics in cell-eval2, an open-source Python package (Supplementary Note 1). 2

# 2. Data and task 

2.1 The task 

The 2026 Challenge asks for zero-shot prediction across cell lines. The responses to CRISPRi knockdowns were measured by Perturb-seq in six human cell lines from different tissues of origin and were all withheld as the ground truth (Arc Institute, 2026a; Arc Virtual Cell Initiative Team and Goodarzi, 2026). For each line, the participants received only the expression profiles of cells carrying non-targeting guides, which describe the unperturbed state of the line, and the list of genes whose knockdown they had to predict. Importantly, there was no Challenge-specific training set, and the participants could train their models on any other data, public or their own. This is the main change from the 2025 Challenge, when the prediction stayed within one cell type, and a training set measured in that same cell type was provided. Cell lines A/B/C formed the validation phase, scored on a live leaderboard, while test lines D/E/F decided the final ranking. Each submission received a single score, the mean of the six metric scores averaged over the cell lines (Section 3.2). 

2.2 Data and panel 

The six lines were screened with the same pooled CRISPRi guide library, and profiled with 10x Genomics Flex, a probe-based single-cell assay (Arc Institute, 2026a, 2026b; Swinderman et al., 2026). All released and scored data share one axis of 18,533 genes. Each line is scored on a panel of 300 perturbations, one per target gene. The three validation lines (A/B/C) share one panel, while the three test lines (D/E/F) share another, with 129 genes in both panels. We excluded all genes targeted in any of the four essential-gene Perturb-seq screens of Replogle et al. (2022) and Nadig et al. (2025), so that no measured response to a panel perturbation could be carried over from these public datasets. Supplementary Fig. S1 shows the cumulative distributions of the target genes’ expression in the control cells and of their knockdown. Each panel gene is represented by one dual-guide construct, which carries two guides against that gene (Replogle et al., 2020), the same in every line in which the gene is scored. The six lines differ in their native depth (median ~21,500–31,600 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) per cell) and in the number of cells per construct (median 445–590). From the cells of each panel construct, we subsampled exactly 400 cells per line. The control pool of every line contains the same 46 non-targeting constructs, also with 400 cells each, i.e. 18,400 control cells per line. To match the sequencing depth across the lines, we thinned the counts of every cell binomially, so that each line has a median of ~20,000 UMIs per cell. We fixed the number of cells and the depth because every metric depends on both. More cells and reads add power to the Wilcoxon test that selects the genes of the DE metrics and reduce the noise in the pseudobulk profiles of the expression metrics. 

2.3 Submissions: single cells, counts and measured controls 

We require predicted single cells (cell × gene count matrix, 400 cells per perturbation) rather than a summary of the response (pseudobulk expression, list of DE genes, etc.) for two reasons. First, single cells yield every Perturb-seq result (distances, differential expression, pathway enrichment, gene signatures, etc.), while collecting each result separately would invite a different model for each outcome, which is less likely to 3

In the 2025 Challenge, the metrics used the control cells of the submission and shortcut solutions exploited this freedom as well. 

# 3. Shared design of the six metrics 

3.1 Target genes: excluded from every metric 

Every knockdown lowers the expression of its own target gene, and the participants were given the list of targets. Predicting this drop requires no model, so we exclude the target gene of each perturbation from all six metrics. The perturbation-discrimination metric compares the prediction for each perturbation with the measured profiles of all perturbations, which show the knockdowns of their own targets. For this metric, we remove all 300 target genes of the panel from every profile, so that all comparisons use the same genes. In the 2025 Challenge, the target gene was excluded only from this metric. 

3.2 Scores: anchored at the mean baseline and at a repeat of the experiment 

The raw values of the six metrics cannot be compared with each other. They differ in range (Fig. 3.1), and the values that can be reached differ between the cell lines. To place the metrics on one scale, we anchor each of them at two values measured on the same reference data: a mean baseline b, the value of a prediction without any perturbation-specific information, and a replicate r, the value of a repeat of the experiment. Like the measured responses to the perturbations, the per-line values of the two anchors were not released to the participants. A submission’s value u of a metric becomes the score 

s = ( u − b)/(r − b),

so that 0 is the mean baseline and 1 is a repeat of the experiment. The score of a line is the equal-weight mean of its six scores, and the scores of the lines are averaged. We weight the six scores equally, since the anchoring places them on one scale and each of them scores a different property of the prediction. Sections 4 and 5 give the values of the metrics before this scaling. The mean baseline is the mean response of the line: the equal-weight average of the mean count profiles of all constructs screened in the line with at least 300 cells that pass a knockdown filter (4,497–6,010 constructs per line, most of them outside the panel). It is assigned to every perturbation as 400 identical cells and scored as an ordinary submission, except that its counts are fractional. The replicate compares the reference with itself. We split the cells of every perturbation and of the control into two disjoint halves, scored one half against the other, each with its own control cells, and averaged the result over five random splits. For the fold-change error, only the fold changes came from the halves, while the significant genes and the denominator came from the full reference. We note that Schäfer et al. (2026) discuss the same normalization, between a lower reference and a positive reference made by splitting the cells of a perturbation into two disjoint subsets (see also Vollenweider and Bühlmann, 2026). As Schäfer et al. point out, such a 4

Since every half holds only 200 cells per perturbation, the replicate is noisier than the full reference, and a prediction equal to the reference scores above 1. A model that predicts the response exactly can therefore also score above 1, by a margin that differs between the metrics. We do not clamp the scores above 1 or the negative scores of submissions worse than the mean baseline. The two exceptions are the two error metrics, which have no upper bound, so that one badly scaled prediction could otherwise dominate the mean score. The score of the fold-change error is floored at −6. The score of the expression error is clamped to the range from 0 to 1, since its corrected error can also fall below that of the replicate, and even below zero, without a better prediction. In the 2025 Challenge, the upper anchor was the ideal value of each metric (zero for an error metric, one for the others), and scores below the mean baseline were set to zero. We replaced the ideal by the replicate because no prediction can be expected to reach the ideal value: even a repeat of the experiment falls short of it, owing to the measurement noise. At the lower end, clamping at zero hid how far below the mean baseline a submission fell, which is why negative scores are now included for five of the six metrics. 

# 4. Expression metrics 

Both expression metrics (perturbation discrimination and expression error) compare the pseudobulk profiles of predicted and measured cells. In the 2025 Challenge, the pseudobulk profile was calculated as the mean over the cells of their log-normalized expression. Since the logarithm is concave, this mean (log-norm) depends on how the counts are spread over the cells (Jensen’s inequality), and a prediction with the correct expression but with less variation between its cells than the measured cells cannot reach the measured profile, however accurate it is. # 5. Differential-expression metrics 

The main readout of a perturbation experiment is the list of genes that respond to each perturbation: which genes change their expression, in which direction and by how much. These differentially expressed genes are the biologically relevant information that we want to extract from the experiment, since they point to the function of the perturbed gene and to the pathways and programs that it controls. A model that predicts the outcome of the experiment therefore has to reproduce them, and four of the six metrics compare the DE genes of a submission with those of the measured data. In the 2025 Challenge, a single metric scored the DE genes: the overlap between the measured significant genes and the predicted significant genes with the largest absolute fold changes (at most as many as the measured ones), relative to the number of measured ones (Roohani et al., 2025). We replaced it with four metrics, which score the size of the changes, the set of significant genes and the directions of the changes. To find the DE genes, we compare the cells of each perturbation with the measured control cells, gene by gene, by the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The test makes no assumption about the distribution of the counts, which are sparse and overdispersed, and an outlying cell affects it only through its rank, not through its value. We apply the test to the counts normalized per cell, and take its p-values from the normal approximation with tie and continuity corrections, which is accurate at the sizes of the groups (400 cells per perturbation, 18,400 control cells). We correct the p-values for multiple testing within each perturbation by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) and call a gene significant if its adjusted p-value is below 0.05, i.e. at a false discovery rate of 5%. The test reports only whether a gene changed. The direction and the size of the change come from the log 2 fold change,

We apply the same procedure, with the same parameters and against the same measured control cells, to the submission and to the reference. Compared with the 2025 Challenge, we correct for multiple testing within each perturbation rather than across all perturbations together, so that the threshold has the same meaning for a perturbation with a strong response as for one with a weak response. We also compute the fold changes from arithmetic rather than geometric means. For the DE metrics, we filter out the genes with low expression, testing a gene only if its mean expression in the control cells exceeds 5 counts (UMIs) per million (CPM). This filter keeps 9,537–10,124 of the 18,533 genes, depending on the cell line. To avoid shortcut solutions, we apply the filter to the control cells alone. 5.4 Direction reach: the depth to which the ranking of the genes is correct 

Whether the submission calls a gene significant depends on the calibration of its adjusted p-values, i.e. on how much its cells vary within a perturbation. Correct calibration is a hard task, since it requires accurate modeling of the experimental noise. The direction reach requires no calibration of the false discovery rate, only a correct ranking of the genes by their significance. We order the genes that the reference calls significant for perturbation p by the submission’s own significance (by its adjusted p-value, then by its unadjusted p-value, then by its absolute fold change), and find the deepest prefix of this ranking in which at least 90% of the predicted directions are correct:

# 6. Discussion 

In this work we present the six metrics that score the 2026 Challenge, together with the reasoning behind each of them and their values on the data of the Challenge. Two of the metrics compare the pseudobulk 13

expression profiles of the predicted and measured cells, while four compare the genes that respond to each perturbation. Every metric is reported on a scale from a mean baseline to a repeat of the experiment, and every definition includes safeguards against shortcut solutions. The first lesson of this work is that the details of a metric decide which predictions win. With the L1 distance of the 2025 Challenge, the perturbation-discrimination metric rewarded or penalized a prediction for the scale of its effects (Supplementary Note 2). Without a correction for the sampling noise, both the squared error and the absolute error score a repeat of the experiment worse than a no-change prediction (Section 4.2). Such properties are easy to overlook in the definition of a metric. They become obvious when the metric is applied to predictions whose quality is known in advance. Metrics therefore have to be designed as carefully as the experiments whose predictions they evaluate. The most informative of these predictions is a repeat of the experiment, which we made by splitting the cells of every perturbation and of the control into two halves. A repeat predicts the measurement as well as the experiment itself can. Thus, a metric that ranks it below a trivial prediction does not measure what it is meant to measure. The values of the repeat also show how much of the range of a metric the measurement noise leaves within reach. Two halves of the same experiment share fewer than half of the genes in the union of their significant sets (Section 5.2), while 93–95% of the significant calls of one half have the direction measured in the other (Section 5.3). We therefore anchored every score at the repeat (Section 3.2), and we recommend evaluating any new metric on half-splits of the data before it is used to rank models. Another lesson is that the metrics should follow the experiment. Four of the six metrics compare the DE genes, the main readout of a Perturb-seq experiment: which genes change, in which direction and by how much (Section 5). A prediction that scores well on these metrics reproduces the result that an experimentalist would report, rather than a summary statistic that is convenient to compute. We believe that the evaluation of virtual cells should be built on the quantities that biologists measure in their experiments. The same principle guides the choice of metrics in biomedical image analysis (Maier-Hein et al., 2024). Finally, the metrics of a competition have to be designed against shortcut solutions, since a competition rewards whatever raises the score. The tested genes are chosen from the control cells alone, and the target genes are excluded. The predictions are submitted as counts of single cells, and compared with the measured control cells. The expression error caps the credit for sampling noise, and the direction fidelity yield penalizes a submission that calls too few genes. The new set of metrics improves upon those used in the 2025 Challenge in three ways. First, the DE genes are scored by the size and the direction of their changes, and not only by the overlap of the significant sets (Section 5). Second, a repeat of the experiment scores above the mean baseline on all six metrics and all six cell lines (Section 3.2), while the mean absolute error of the 2025 Challenge scored it below a no-change prediction (Section 4.2). Third, the shortcut solutions that the metrics of the 2025 Challenge left open can no longer raise the score (Sections 2.3, 3.1 and 4.1). However, there is still room for improvement to prevent shortcut solutions. On the final validation leaderboard of the 2026 Challenge, eight of the ten leading submissions emitted cells whose summed counts, for many genes, varied less than sampling allows. Nevertheless, the expression error credited them with 91–100% of the full correction for sampling noise (Section 4.2). The direction fidelity yield penalizes under-prediction more than over-prediction (Section 5.3), and the significant-set agreement of a repeat of the experiment is low, because the power of the test is limited (Section 5.2). In addition, all six metrics compare the prediction with the measurement within each cell line, and they are not designed to measure how the response to a perturbation depends on the cell line. We find that the leading submissions reproduced only a small part of 14

We will continue this work for the next Challenge in two directions: metrics that capture more of the biology that experimentalists measure, and designs that close the shortcut solutions that remain open.
