ABSTRACT
Understanding gene regulatory relationships at scale can help identify interven-
tion points and anticipate unintended effects. However, connecting a black-box
model’s learned computation to biological knowledge remains difficult, particu-
larly for unfamiliar cellular states. We investigate whether a sparse, typed gene
network can generate inspectable hypotheses for steering malignant transcrip-
tional programs toward expression patterns associated with favorable prognosis.
Using LINCS L1000 CRISPR/Cas9 profiles paired with matched plate controls,
we train a graph neural network to recover the perturbed gene from baseline and
endpoint expression. Its nodes combine ProTrek-derived protein representations
with expression features; its edges encode signed regulatory relationships and
other gene interactions. We use this inverse formulation to nominate interventions
for a desired multigene expression change and examine the biological relation-
ships supporting each nomination. We query the model in a specified cancer-cell
context with endpoints that upregulate favorable prognostic markers and down-
regulate unfavorable ones. To examine the sensitivity of nominations with respect
to structures such as gene states, typed messages, and residual connections, we
use integrated gradients and conductance along a path from the cell baseline to-
wards the requested expression endpoint. In A549, the model nominates TTK
although its expression is unchanged in the request. The account assigns support-
ing credit through the checkpoint-associated MAD1L1 relationship and opposing
credit through the E2F4 transcriptional-regulatory channel, distinguishing their
contributions within the same nomination. Across 333 screened nominations in
nine held-out cell lines, request-path attribution shows positive line-averaged cor-
respondence with finite message-deletion effects at every transition. Existing TTK
knockout measurements show requested-direction agreement across other markers
at the 98.2nd percentile (10 wells); ten of seventeen measured cases in the illustra-
tive screen meet the predefined support criterion. Together, these analyses make
competing biological accounts examinable through their distinct contributions to
a nomination, with evidence from perturbation measurements and experimental
literature.

INTRODUCTION
Recent interest in the virtual cell has focused attention on perturbation experiments and modeling
(Bunne et al., 2024). A central goal is to predict the change in transcriptional state following a ge-
netic or small-molecule intervention and generalize to unseen cell contexts. Perturb-seq made such
measurements scalable by combining pooled genetic screens with single-cell expression profiling
(Adamson et al., 2016; Dixit et al., 2016), later extending to genome-scale surveys (Replogle et al.,
2022).
Forward models predict expression responses to specified interventions (Klein et al., 2025; Roohani
et al., 2024; Lotfollahi et al., 2023; Adduri et al., 2025); inverse methods nominate interventions for
a desired state (Gonzalez et al., 2026). Both can learn from the same observations. We choose the in-
verse formulation for two main reasons. First, its input lets us specify a desired multigene expression

change. Here we request changes in prognostic biomarkers. Other possible requests could represent
movement toward a treatment-sensitive cancer-cell state (Bell et al., 2019), lineage maturation in
immature cancer cells (Sykes et al., 2016), or a functional beta-cell expression program (Pagliuca
et al., 2014). Second, we want a window into the biology supporting a nomination for that change.
For treatment sensitivity, we could examine support associated with treatment-response features and
broader proliferation changes; for differentiation, lineage-maturation and cell-cycle features; and
for beta-cell function, cell-identity, secretion-related, and stress-response features. Gradient-based
attribution separates supporting and opposing contributions from requested changes and named re-
lationships, making the model’s support for different biological explanations examinable. These
broader applications would require appropriate perturbation data and functional testing in the rele-
vant cellular context. Forward prediction followed by response matching is also a valid approach;
our choice concerns the nomination we want to interrogate, without assuming a ranking or compu-
tational advantage.
A recent perturbation-prediction benchmark found that the evaluated deep learning methods did not
outperform simple linear baselines (Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025), while subsequent work showed
that conclusions depend on metric calibration (Miller et al., 2026). We evaluate predictive perfor-
mance alongside a separate question: how does a requested expression program contribute to an
intervention nomination through named biological relationships?
Logistic regression offers a familiar starting point: its coefficients connect named inputs to a logit.
In an interacting model, however, contributions depend on other inputs and on the comparison being
made. We therefore ask whether the representation can preserve the scientific identities needed to
inspect those dependencies. We retain the graph’s named relationships while allowing their contri-
butions to depend on the surrounding expression program through nonlinear updates. This provides
a model in which to examine how combinations of requested changes support or oppose an inter-
vention nomination.
To make these dependencies inspectable, we combine protein features, paired expression, and typed
graph messages at gene/protein nodes (Figure 1). We use semantic attribution to quantify how re-
quested changes contribute to a nomination through those identities and relationships. The graph
supplies available relationships; the fitted account quantifies their contributions for a particular re-
quest, including opposing contributions from different relationship types connecting the same genes.
With 33 distinct neighbors per gene on average, corresponding to approximately 52 incoming typed
messages per node excluding self-loops, the graph offers many possible relationships to examine.
Their relative contributions can help prioritize hypotheses for subsequent wet-lab investigation. We
illustrate this account with TTK in A549, a nomination whose score rises under the requested pro-
gram and falls when that program is reversed (Section 6), test request-path attributions against finite
message deletion across every screened nomination in 9 held-out lines (Section 6.2), and compare
the requested directions with measured knockout responses (Section 7). Predictive evaluation pro-
vides context for the learned representations we inspect. The original PYGL and CCDC86 examples
are retained as contrasts between own-edit-dependent nomination and measured other-marker sup-
port (Appendix J).

RELATED WORK
Perturbation prediction and inverse design. PDGrapher couples perturbagen discovery with an
intervention-conditioned response model (Gonzalez et al., 2026). Causal differential networks re-
cover intervention targets from observational and perturbed distributions (Wu et al., 2025). Our
direct inverse classifier learns on a fixed communication graph.
Biologically structured representations. DCell and DrugCell show how biological entities can
organize neural computation (Ma et al., 2018; Kuenzi et al., 2020). We build on this principle using
gene-indexed states and relation-specific message passing (Bronstein et al., 2021; Schlichtkrull et al.,
2018) to inspect individual inverse nominations.
Attribution and graph explanations. Integrated gradients (IG) allocate a scalar difference relative
to a reference (Sundararajan et al., 2017), while conductance follows it through hidden represen-
tations (Dhamdhere et al., 2019). For graph explanations, GNNExplainer optimizes subgraph and
feature masks (Ying et al., 2019), whereas GNN-LRP attributes predictions to higher-order relevant

walks (Schnake et al., 2022). Our signed allocation through gene states, typed messages, and residu-
als precedes display selection. Routes then organize marginal transitions without higher-order route
scores. Our evaluation concerns signed accounting and finite message effects; visual plausibility
alone is not validation (Adebayo et al., 2018).

LEARNING CONTEXT-DEPENDENT GENE REPRESENTATIONS
We learn these representations by identifying knockouts from paired expression, allowing graph
neighbors to shape each gene’s state.
3.1
OBSERVED PAIRS AND GENE-INDEXED STATES
For training, each LINCS L1000 observation pairs a CRISPR/Cas9 knockout endpoint with matched
plate controls and labels the targeted gene (Subramanian et al., 2017; Broad Institute, 2017). The
Level-3 cohort contains 269,769 eligible pairs across 17 lines and 11 tissues. LINCS BING supplies
10,073 measured and inferred expression coordinates (Appendix A). Training-only means and stan-
dard deviations normalize baseline and endpoint expression. We test new cell contexts by holding
out whole lines in multi-line tissues; singleton tissues permit only physical-replicate holdouts. At
each graph depth, we fit three models, H1/H2/H3, on different training/held-out partitions with a
shared seed. Their training data overlap. Whole-plate validation selects checkpoints (Appendix A).

neighbors in STRING and CollecTRI (Szklarczyk et al., 2025; Müller-Dott et al., 2023). Directed
families have separate forward/reverse transport channels; computational reversal does not reverse

3.2
SHARED DECODING AND PREDICTIVE CONTEXT
The shared decoder ranks 17,225 nodes with mapped ProTrek vectors; 83 others remain in the
computation but are ineligible candidates. Genes without positive training examples remain eligible;
unseen-target accuracy is untested. Training uses global cross-entropy (CE) plus a margin against
the hardest 20 incorrect candidates (Appendix B). Genetic labels keep the intended target explicit;
multitarget chemical action is examined later through annotations (Keiser et al., 2009).
We compare the GNN with a protein-informed node-local predictor and an expression-only global
multilayer perceptron (MLP). Predictive performance depends on the setting: GNN versus node-
local mean reciprocal rank (MRR) is 0.1344 versus 0.1150 for held replicates and 0.1433 versus
0.1499 for unseen cells (three-seed tissue-balanced means; Table 21). These measure observed-
knockout recovery, not intervention recommendation quality. The node-local candidate’s raw score
cannot depend on other genes’ edits; the global MLP can use those edits and support input attri-
bution. We use the GNN to examine named, typed relationships that participate in the cross-gene
computation producing the score. The fixed-budget comparison does not establish best-achievable
graph-free performance. Depth comparisons likewise do not establish a uniformly preferred depth
(Appendix B).

FROM A DESIRED PROGRAM TO A NOMINATION
After training, we freeze the weights and replace the observed endpoint with a requested profile
to nominate interventions. Human Protein Atlas (HPA) survival associations supply expression di-
rections, not causal effects (Uhlen et al., 2017; Yuan et al., 2025; Human Protein Atlas, n.d.). For
A549, validation-tier markers in Lung Adenocarcinoma (validation) specify 2 increases and 155 de-
creases, including decreases in the unfavorable markers MAD2L1, BUB1B and PLK1. Each edit
has amplitude 2 H1-reference SDs; other coordinates remain unchanged. HPA supplies the direc-
tions; PRECOG provides a separate concordance check, not a requirement for selecting markers
(Appendix K).
One native baseline/endpoint pair is transformed through each model’s normalizer. Four-hop parti-
tion models H1 and H3 both held A549 out of training and validation; their mean raw logit defines
the ranking. Unfavorable markers requested to decrease, such as the originally illustrated PYGL
and CCDC86, remain eligible knockout candidates. Directly reducing an unfavorable marker agrees
with its requested direction; a single-gene perturbation may also affect other coordinates. Candi-
dates are ranked against the complete multigene request. Of the top 50 nominees, 20 have edited
own inputs. We therefore test whether nominations persist when their own requested edit is removed
(Section 6).

6
A PROGRAM-DRIVEN NOMINATION
We first ask whether a nomination is supported by the other requested changes, beyond the requested
decrease in the nominated gene itself. To test this, we restore only that gene’s endpoint expression
to its baseline value and keep the rest of the request unchanged. PYGL and CCDC86 lose their high
rankings under this control, showing that their own requested decreases strongly influence their nom-
inations. Their measured knockouts provide separate evidence about effects on the other requested
markers (Table 1). We therefore screen for candidates whose scores rise under the remaining re-
quest and fall under its reversal in every eligible model. Requiring candidate-specific masked rank
≤200 and mean (akf −bkf)/2 ≥0.5 yields 19 cases in six of fourteen lines. We use TTK/A549
as a worked example because its own expression was never edited. This choice is retrospective; the
population test includes every nomination passing without the illustrative 0.5 floor.
6.1
TTK IN A549: FROM REQUESTED EDITS TO AN UNEDITED INTERMEDIARY
The A549 request does not ask the model to decrease TTK. Nevertheless, the checkpoint kinase
TTK/MPS1 (Zheng et al., 2019) ranks 2/3 under the candidate-specific masked screen in H1/H3.
In this screening procedure, each competing candidate is scored with its own requested change
removed. TTK’s input does not change, but its competitors’ scores can change, explaining the
difference from its 18/17 ranks in Table 1. The requested changes raise its score by +1.517/+1.329;
reversing them lowers it by −1.834/−1.216. Both models held A549 out, so we can ask what in the
other genes’ requested changes supports this nomination.
At the input, decreasing MAD2L1 supplies the strongest support in both models (+0.251/+0.236).
Decreases in BUB1B and PLK1 also support both fits. Other edits behave differently: BRCA1 down
opposes in H1, and TK1 down opposes in H3. The model therefore does not assign the same sign to
every decrease in this program (Table 10).

TESTING THE REQUESTED PROGRAM AGAINST MEASUREMENTS
Measured responses. The signed account makes a hypothesis about TTK worth examining, but
the computation alone cannot show what a knockout does. We therefore compare existing LINCS
knockout wells with the requested marker directions, excluding the target’s own marker. Measured
TTK knockout agreement reaches the 98.2nd percentile among 5,115 A549 targets (10 wells). The
input-attribution profile also corresponds to measured requested-direction marker displacement (ρ =
0.492; 98.8th percentile against 4,654 other knockouts with at least three wells; Appendix O.1). The
first statistic measures program agreement; the second asks whether the more strongly credited
markers move further in the requested direction.
The full 19-case computational screen gives a mixed picture: ten cases meet the pre-set measured-
support criterion (≥75th percentile), seven are at or below the 50th percentile, and two are unmea-
sured (Table 15). These are retrospective, case-specific results. TTK’s high percentile corresponds
to a mean signed displacement of 0.418 reference SD, far below a uniform two-SD shift. For com-
parison, CKS2/HS944T reaches the 95.0th percentile with a mean displacement of 0.074 SD (six
wells). The measurements support directions for particular nominations, not attainment of the whole
requested vector.
Request size. Because two-SD edits exceed typical knockout displacements, we also query sizes
anchored to the measurements. At 0.527 SD in A549, TTK retains a positive request response
and negative reversed response, with masked ranks 14/7; MAD2L1 remains its strongest input.
CKS2/HS944T weakens at 0.275 SD: its rank falls from 10 to 54 and response from +0.768 to
+0.050 (Appendix M). Even TTK’s persistent nomination does not imply an unchanged internal
account: H3’s 2 →3 message-field cosine is −0.172 across sizes (Table 12).
What existing signatures already tell us. We include a retrospective reference that scores each
knockout’s measured responses in other lines for agreement with the requested marker directions
(4,970 common A549 targets; Table 16). It asks how informative those existing responses are for
the query. It is not a trained forward model and does not reproduce each inverse fold’s information
boundary, so it does not establish comparative ranking performance.

The explorer links nominees to source-reported drug annotations (Ap-
pendix G). These do not establish efficacy; an inhibitor need not reproduce a knockout.

LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION
Observed-pair recovery does not establish intervention efficacy. Survival associations specify di-
rections, not causal effects or amplitudes, and bulk patient cohorts only coarsely match cell-line
contexts. L1000 averages cell populations, so differences may reflect within-cell changes or shifts
in cell-state proportions; these accounts do not isolate phase-specific mechanisms. Training does not
filter by technical quality control (QC). The original cases’ baseline-sensitivity checks do not extend
to TTK or CKS2 (Appendix H). Protein references and interpolation are computational compar-
isons; biological priors and suppression checks do not establish that a displayed route is a necessary
mechanism.
The resulting account connects a quantitative expression request to biological relationships that
can be examined.
TTK separates recurring checkpoint-associated support from transcriptional-
regulatory opposition; SORL1 and CAMSAP2 show why even the same partner cannot be assigned
one contribution sign across relationship types. Population suppression assesses computational cor-
respondence, while measured knockout responses provide complementary, case-specific evidence.
Together, these results make intervention hypotheses more specific and open to experimental chal-
lenge. The biological mechanism responsible for the measured response remains to be tested.

DATA, GRAPH, AND VARIABLE DEFINITIONS
A.1
ASSAY AND RETENTION
The pinned inputs are CLUE’s LINCS2020 beta Level-3 CRISPR-treated and control GCTX ma-
trices, sample metadata, and gene annotations. L1000 measures expression abundance rather than
transcription rates. The selected trt_xpr modality denotes CRISPR/Cas9 knockout, not CRISPR
interference. Level-3 values are processed expression, not raw counts or Level-5 differential signa-
tures. All retained treated wells are measured at 96 hours; dose is missing and is not interpreted as
zero.
Retention requires physical replicate X1, X2, or X3, a nonmissing target label, and matched controls
on the same plate. The target vocabulary is the intersection of labels present in X1/X2 and X3
before hybrid splitting. This produces 270,331 paired wells on 915 plates, with 21,687 matched
controls. Of 5,119 retained target identities, 5,108 map to eligible positive labels, yielding 269,769
supervised pairs. Protein-covered candidate nodes form a broader set; a never-positive candidate
receives competition-based negative supervision but is not an established biological negative or a
validated zero-shot prediction.
LINCS supplies expression estimates inferred from the 978 measured landmarks; we use these sup-
plied values without fitting a new inference model. The BING (Best Inferred Genes) space combines
landmarks with genes whose inferred expression passed the provider’s validation against RNA-seq
(Subramanian et al., 2017). In the pinned LINCS annotation, 10,174 genes belong to BING and
10,086 of these are annotated protein-coding. The historical identifier intersection retains 10,073
coordinates: all 978 landmarks and 9,095 inferred genes. This ordered feature table is preserved
as a contract; no GTEx expression enters this study. Extending expression coverage lets more gene
nodes combine cellular context with protein features and typed relationships. Inferred coordinates
remain dependent estimates, and we make no claim of exact variance preservation. Native control
values are averaged with float64 accumulation and stored in float32 before normalization.

M.1
FIELD SIMILARITY AND MULTI-HOP INPUT DEPENDENCE
For two aligned signed fields v, w, define shared signed mass by separately dividing each by its
absolute L1 norm and summing min(|vi|/∥v∥1, |wi|/∥w∥1) only where signs agree. This measures
shape overlap, not a significance test or invariance of the raw magnitudes. Table 12 retains all
message transitions. TTK H3’s negative 2 →3 cosine rules out describing its internal account as
unchanged. Conversely, MAD2L1 remains its strongest absolute input-IG marker in both models at
both amplitudes. The nominee-only final node is architecturally concentrated and is not a stability
test.
Absolute input-IG shares outside the complete one-hop incoming prior neighborhood are
30.2%/28.0% for TTK H1, 26.3%/17.3% for TTK H3, and 58.6%/61.8% for CKS2 H3 (origi-
nal/empirical). The exported neighbor sets exactly match the complete prior: 92 distinct neighbors
for TTK and 34 for CKS2. With node-local encoding, normalization within each node, fixed input
normalizers/proteins, and nominee-local decoding, non-neighbor expression influence requires at
least two graph-message hops. The denominator is total absolute input IG; these percentages are
neither fractions of net score, sums of layer credits, nor selected-drawing coverage. They establish
multi-hop input dependence, not a particular attributed route.

SEPARATING PATH ALLOCATION FROM FINITE-DELETION REMAINDERS
Pairwise deletions show increasing non-additivity with depth, but a bounded follow-up does not
establish normalization as a general explanation of the disagreement.
Follow-up designed after the initial results. After recognizing the frozen-statistics arm’s mis-
match, we froze a repaired implementation and analysis on the same saved two-SD panels: 37
member tasks, 26 nominations, 9 held-out lines. This is a bounded follow-up, not an independent
replication or a replacement for the 333-nomination population study. The earlier pairwise measure-
ments and frozen-statistics outputs above remain unchanged.
Let ge multiply one typed directed message, with all gates initially one, and write Se(x) =
∂sk(x; ge)/∂ge|ge=1 for endpoint-local sensitivity. For no-change and request endpoints x0, x1,
define Ae = Se(x1) −Se(x0), De as the original response drop under full deletion, and Ce as the
saved request-path message conductance. In plain terms, Ae is the local linear approximation to the
request-response drop, while De is the drop after completely removing the message. Then, exactly,
D −C = (A−C)+(D −A),
(D −C)2 = (A−C)2 +(D −A)2 +2(A−C)(D −A). (27)
The first term separates path allocation from endpoint sensitivity; the second is the finite-deletion
remainder. Their signed cross-term can cancel, so squared components are not additive fractions of
discrepancy explained. Saved conductance retains its original quadrature error.
