# SUMMARY 20 

Single-cell foundation models (scFMs) are increasingly used for transcriptomic analysis, yet it 21 

remains unclear what biological information their embeddings preserve and when they outper- 22 

form dataset-fitted methods. Here, we present CellBench, a unified benchmark of frozen scFM 23 

representations across tissues, species, and genetic perturbation screens. We find that different 24 

models capture distinct aspects of cellular biology, while classical baselines remain competitive 25 

across tasks. Human-pretrained representations transfer to supervised mouse analyses, but un- 26 

der a shared decoder they fail to improve genome-wide absolute error over predicting no change 27 

for unseen perturbation targets. Neither model size nor reported pretraining data volume reliably 28 

predicts performance. Excluding cohorts with sample or donor identifier matches to pretraining 29 

records substantially reduces annotation advantages without changing model rankings appre- 30 

ciably. Together, these findings show that the value of scFM representations depends on the 31 

biological task and the strength of the evidence supporting their performance. 32

# THE BIGGER PICTURE 40 

Foundation models are becoming common tools for analyzing single-cell data, but choosing the 41 

right model remains challenging. Researchers often rely on benchmark rankings or model size 42 

to guide their decisions, even though these measures may not reflect the biological information 43 

a model preserves. CellBench shows that different models capture different aspects of cellu- 44 

lar biology and that simpler methods can perform just as well on some tasks. It also reveals 45 

an important distinction between representing observed cellular states and predicting how cells 46 

respond to genetic interventions. Human-pretrained representations can transfer to mouse tis- 47 

sues, but their success in observational tasks does not ensure accurate prediction of unseen 48 

perturbations. Moreover, stable model rankings can hide small or dataset-dependent advan- 49 

tages, especially when pretraining data are not fully documented. These findings highlight the 50 

need to evaluate foundation models according to specific biological questions rather than overall 51 

rankings. Strong classical baselines, appropriate controls, and transparent pretraining records 52 

are essential for determining when these models provide reliable benefits. Ultimately, better 53 

evaluation can guide the development of models that are not only more powerful but also more 54 

useful for biological research. 55 

# IN BRIEF 56 

CellBench compares 13 frozen single-cell foundation models with classical baselines fitted on 57 

training data across 12 human and mouse cohorts and four genetic perturbation screens. Dif- 58 

ferent models capture different biological information. Human-pretrained models transfer well to 59 

mouse tissues, yet with a shared decoder none beats predicting no change on unseen pertur- 60 

bations. Model size and training data volume poorly predict performance, and removing human 61 

studies overlapping pretraining data erases the human annotation gain over baselines. 62 

# INTRODUCTION 63 

Large-scale single-cell RNA-sequencing atlases have provided extensive data from human or- 64 

gans, diseases, and model organisms 1–3 . These resources have supported the development 65 

of more than a dozen single-cell foundation models (scFMs) 4–18 . Some models have been pre- 66 

trained on tens to hundreds of millions of cell profiles to learn representations that can be used 67 

across tissues, datasets, and biological tasks. In practice, researchers often use released check- 68 

points as frozen feature extractors to avoid the computational cost of fine-tuning 19–22 . The result- 69 

ing cell embeddings can be used directly for clustering and batch assessment or combined with 70 

prediction heads for cell-type annotation and gene-expression reconstruction. However, these 71 

models differ in architecture, input representation, parameter count, pretraining objectives, exter- 72 

nal biological priors 23,24 , and pretraining species coverage. It is therefore difficult to know which 73 

model is best suited to a particular task. It also remains unclear when pretrained representations 74 

offer an advantage over methods fitted directly to the target dataset, such as principal component 75 

analysis (PCA) 25 , uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) 26 , and scVI 27 . 76 

Previous benchmarks have compared scFMs across several downstream tasks, but their 77 

conclusions differ across models and evaluation settings 19–22 . Some studies report benefits 78 

from pretraining, while others find that PCA and scVI remain competitive. These comparisons 79

Specialized methods such as GEARS, scGen, and CPA have been developed for perturbation 85 

prediction 30–32 , but the ability of general-purpose frozen scFM embeddings to support this task 86 

has not been systematically compared with their performance on tissue datasets. Other stud- 87 

ies have examined how tokenization and pretraining data diversity affect individual model fam- 88 

ilies 33,34 . However, it remains unclear whether model size and pretraining data volume predict 89 

performance across different released checkpoints. Another concern is the possible reuse of 90 

public datasets in both pretraining and evaluation. Without detailed pretraining records, it is 91 

difficult to establish whether benchmark datasets are independent of the training data. 92 

These gaps raise four connected questions about the value and reliability of frozen scFM 93 

representations. First, what biological information do different models preserve, and how does 94 

this information affect their performance across tasks? Distinguishing broad cell-type identity 95 

from rare populations, gene-expression patterns, and continuous transcriptional programs may 96 

help explain why models perform differently across biological applications. Second, how well do 97 

pretrained representations generalize beyond their original training settings? Applying human- 98 

pretrained models to mouse tissues tests whether learned cellular information can be reused 99 

across species, while predicting unseen genetic perturbations tests whether these representa- 100 

tions can also support the prediction of responses to interventions. Third, do larger models and 101 

larger pretraining datasets consistently produce better representations? Comparing released 102 

checkpoints with different architectures can help establish whether parameter count and pretrain- 103 

ing data volume are useful indicators of performance and how performance differences relate to 104 

model design. Finally, how reliable are model rankings when pretraining data are not fully docu- 105 

mented? Understanding the effects of incomplete training records and potential dataset overlap 106 

is necessary to judge whether reported advantages over classical baselines are robust. 107 

To address these questions, we developed CellBench, a unified benchmark for evaluating 108 

frozen scFM representations across biological tasks and datasets. We compared 13 released 109 

scFM checkpoints with PCA, UMAP, and scVI fitted strictly on training data, covering 12 human 110 

and mouse tissue cohorts and four CRISPR Perturb-seq screens. Using consistent data splits 111 

and downstream evaluation methods, we assessed unsupervised clustering, native batch struc- 112 

ture, supervised cell-type annotation, and gene-expression reconstruction. We also used linear 113 

probes to examine how well different embeddings preserve rare cell populations and continuous 114 

cellular programs. To investigate generalization, we evaluated human-pretrained checkpoints 115 

on mouse tissues and tested frozen representations for genetic perturbation prediction using 116 

a shared residual decoder. Perturbation predictions were additionally compared with a zero- 117 

change baseline to determine whether they improved on predicting no transcriptional response. 118 

We further examined model parameter composition, reported pretraining corpus size, and the 119 

relationship between checkpoint characteristics and downstream performance. Finally, we au- 120 

dited pretraining data provenance and tested the sensitivity of model rankings and performance 121 

advantages to the exclusion of cohorts with identified sample or donor matches in pretraining 122 

records. Our results show that different models preserve different biological information and that 123 

no single representation consistently leads across tasks. Classical baselines remain competi- 124 

tive, while representations that transfer to supervised mouse tasks do not necessarily support 125 

accurate prediction of unseen genetic interventions under the shared decoder. We also find 126 

that model scale does not reliably predict performance across heterogeneous checkpoints and 127 

that stable rankings can conceal substantial changes in performance advantages over classical 128 

methods. Together, CellBench provides a systematic assessment of what scFM representations 129

# RESULTS 131 

CellBench enables systematic benchmarking of scFMs and dataset-fitted 132 

representations 133 

A major challenge in benchmarking single-cell foundation models (scFMs) is separating the util- 134 

ity of pretrained representations from the effects of model-specific fine-tuning strategies. To 135 

mitigate this challenge, we developed CellBench, a unified evaluation framework that compares 136 

pretrained cellular representations using standardized donor-level data splits (Supplemental Ta- 137 

ble S1), consistent downstream probe architectures (Supplemental Table S2), and rank-based 138 

performance aggregation across datasets and tasks ( Figure 1 ; Supplemental Table S3). We 139 

evaluated 13 publicly released scFM checkpoints, including CellFM 13 , CellPLM 14 , GeneCom- 140 

pass 12 , Geneformer 5, LangCell 17 , Nicheformer 10 , Tabula 18 , TranscriptFormer 9, UCE 8, scFoun- 141 

dation 7, scGPT 6, scLong 11 , and scPRINT2 15,16 , alongside three dataset-fitted baselines, includ- 142 

ing PCA 25 , UMAP 26 , and scVI 27 . 143 

CellBench assesses four complementary aspects of representation quality through unsu- 144 

pervised clustering 35 , batch-effect assessment in the native embedding space 37 , supervised 145 

closed-set cell-type annotation, and supervised reconstruction of highly variable gene (HVG) ex- 146 

pression. We evaluated these tasks across 12 cohorts organized into three biological panels, en- 147 

compassing healthy human organs, human diseases, and anatomically matched mouse organs. 148 

The healthy human panel included bone marrow mononuclear cells (BM) 38 , the Human Lung 149 

Cell Atlas (HL) 1, adult heart (AH) 39 , and adult kidney (AK) 40 . The disease panel comprised mul- 150 

tiple sclerosis brain (MS) 41 , ulcerative colitis epithelium (UC) 42 , lethal COVID-19 lung (C19) 43 , 151 

and triple-negative breast cancer (BC) 44 . The mouse panel included bone marrow (MM), lung 152 

(ML), heart (MH), and kidney (MK) from Tabula Muris Senis 2 (Supplemental Table S4). To en- 153 

sure a fair comparison and avoid information leakage during baseline fitting, the dataset-fitted 154 

baselines selected up to 3,000 HVGs and learned 256-dimensional representations exclusively 155 

from the 70% donor-partitioned training set for each random seed, without using cell-type labels 156 

or batch covariates. The fitted baselines were then applied to held-out validation (10%) and test 157 

(20%) donors through out-of-sample transformation or inference (Supplemental Table S1). 158 

To examine whether representation quality observed in cellular atlases extends to experimen- 159 

tal perturbations, we evaluated the same 16 methods on an independent panel of four CRISPR 160 

Perturb-seq datasets, comprising Norman 45 , Adamson 46 , Replogle K562, and Replogle RPE1 47 . 161 

Perturbation results were analyzed separately and excluded from both static representation rank- 162 

ings and cross-checkpoint attribute correlation analyses, so that observational and perturbation 163 

performance could be assessed independently. 164 

Pretrained models preserve distinct aspects of biological information 165 

This section examines whether pretrained embeddings from different models capture a common 166 

dimension of biological representation quality or distinct biological properties across four static 167 

tasks. We evaluated unsupervised clustering and native batch effects directly in the embed- 168 

ding space without training task-specific heads, while using standardized task-specific prediction 169 

heads for closed-set annotation and HVG reconstruction. We further applied Ridge classifica- 170 

tion and regression probes for rare-class and gene-program information, without using an MLP. 171

Unsupervised clustering. This task tests whether embeddings recover major tissue lin- 175 

eages and cell-state neighborhoods without labels. Composite rankings differed across biologi- 176 

cal panels, with protein-language UCE 23 leading healthy human organs at mean rank 2.604 and 177 

human diseases at 3.771 , whereas a 256-component PCA fitted only on training-split HVGs led 178 

mouse organs at mean rank 2.500 versus 3.188 for UCE and outperformed UCE on the multi- 179 

donor HL dataset at 2.667 versus 4.750 . That overall ordering cannot distinguish agreement with 180 

annotated cell-type partitions from continuous separation between cell types ( Figure 2 ; Supple- 181 

mental Figures S1–S4). We therefore considered two biological interpretations, scoring Louvain 182 

partition agreement with annotated types by three metrics, including the unsupervised adjusted 183 

Rand index (uARI), normalized mutual information (NMI), and Hungarian matching accuracy, 184 

while scoring continuous cell-type separation by cell-type average silhouette width (ASW). How- 185 

ever, the two interpretations identified different leading models. PCA reached the highest 12- 186 

cohort NMI of 0.706 , ahead of UCE at 0.701 , and uARI and matching accuracy followed the same 187 

order. These results suggest that PCA on HVGs can recover major cell-type partitions without 188 

requiring pretrained representations. By contrast, continuous separation told a different story. 189 

Dataset-fitted UMAP had the largest 12-cohort ASW of 0.578 , with UCE next at 0.572 . These 190 

results suggest that UCE preserves competitive continuous cell-type separation without consis- 191 

tently improving discrete clustering over dataset-fitted baselines. 192 

Native batch assessment. This task tests whether embeddings can mix cells from different 193 

donors without collapsing cell-type structure. Composite ranks placed PCA first on human or- 194 

gans at 6.042 , CellPLM first on human diseases at 7.594 , and TranscriptFormer and UCE jointly 195 

first on mouse organs at 6.917 . That overall ordering cannot tell whether a model mixes donors 196 

or conserves cell types ( Figure 3 ; Supplemental Figures S5–S8). We therefore considered 197 

two biological interpretations, scoring donor mixing by scaled iLISI 48 and kBET 49 , while scoring 198 

cell-type structure conservation by scaled cLISI 37,48 and graph connectivity 37 . However, the two 199 

interpretations revealed different model strengths. On human organs, CellPLM showed greater 200 

donor mixing than UCE, with iLISI 0.310 versus 0.215 , and kBET followed the same order at 0.325 201 

versus 0.180 . By contrast, cell-type conservation told a different story. UCE conserved cell types 202 

better than CellPLM, with scaled cLISI 0.989 versus 0.974 , and graph connectivity followed the 203 

same order at 0.906 versus 0.782 . Furthermore, high donor mixing coincided with poor clustering 204 

performance in several models. Tabula, CellFM, and mean-gene-pooled scLong showed high 205 

iLISI scores of 0.359 , 0.303 , and 0.297 , respectively, yet showed some of the lowest clustering 206 

uARI scores across 12 cohorts, at 0.016 , 0.052 , and 0.051 (Figure 2 ). This pattern suggests that 207 

elevated donor mixing in these unconditioned embeddings may reflect broad loss of biological 208 

structure rather than selective removal of technical batch effects. 209 

Supervised cell-type annotation. This task tests whether embeddings preserve fine-grained 210 

cell-type identity, including rare populations whose performance may be obscured by abundant 211 

lineages in overall accuracy. Full-panel scFoundation led all three panels on composite rank, 212 

with mean ranks of 2.854 on human organs, 2.156 on human diseases, and 1.979 on mouse or- 213 

gans. That overall ordering cannot distinguish gains in overall accuracy from improvements in 214 

rare-sensitive classification performance ( Figure 4 ; Supplemental Figures S9–S12). We there- 215 

fore considered two biological interpretations, scoring overall classification performance by over- 216 

all accuracy, while assessing class-balanced performance using macro-F1, balanced accuracy, 217 

macro-precision, and macro-AUPRC. However, the two interpretations revealed different magni- 218 

tudes of improvement. Relative to training-split PCA, scFoundation achieved only a 0.0074 ad- 219 

vantage in 12-cohort overall accuracy, at 0.8816 versus 0.8742 . By contrast, its macro-F1 advan- 220 

tage was more than three times larger, at 0.0234 , with scores of 0.8334 versus 0.8099 . Balanced 221

# DISCUSSION 545 

The central finding of CellBench is that the value of a cellular representation depends on the bi- 546 

ological information required by the downstream task, rather than on a single measure of model 547 

performance. Cell-type identity, continuous transcriptional programs, gene-expression patterns, 548 

and donor-related variation represent different aspects of cellular biology that are not necessar- 549 

ily captured equally well by the same embedding. This helps explain why previous benchmarks 550 

have sometimes reached different conclusions about the benefits of single-cell foundation mod- 551 

els 19–22 . A model that performs well in supervised annotation may not be equally effective at 552 

unsupervised clustering or expression reconstruction, while strong donor mixing can coexist with 553 

substantial loss of cell-type structure. The continued competitiveness of dataset-fitted PCA and 554 

scVI further suggests that pretraining is not inherently advantageous for every biological task. 555 

Rather than identifying a universally superior checkpoint, benchmarking should therefore es- 556 

tablish which biological properties different representations preserve and under what conditions 557 

those properties provide an advantage over simpler alternatives. 558 

Model scale alone is also insufficient to explain what makes a cellular representation useful. 559 

Although larger models and broader pretraining datasets are often expected to yield more trans- 560 

ferable features, this expectation does not translate into a consistent performance advantage 561 

across heterogeneous released checkpoints. This distinction is important when interpreting con- 562 

trolled studies that demonstrate the benefits of particular tokenization strategies or greater pre- 563 

training data diversity within individual model families 33,34 . Such experiments address the effect 564 

of specific design choices under relatively controlled conditions, whereas comparisons across 565 

released scFMs involve simultaneous differences in architecture, input representation, training 566 

data, and learning objectives. Moreover, total parameter count does not consistently describe 567 

the capacity of the cell encoder because checkpoints differ in how they allocate parameters to 568 

frozen lookup tables, trainable components, and other stored information. Our observations that 569 

embedding width was more strongly associated with annotation performance than parameter 570 

count, and that closely related Geneformer and LangCell encoders performed differently, further 571 

illustrate the limitations of using model size as a proxy for representation quality. These associa- 572 

tions do not establish which architectural or training choices cause better performance. Instead, 573 

they motivate more controlled investigation of how input representations, pretraining objectives, 574 

and embedding design influence the biological information available to downstream tasks. 575 

The ability to represent cellular states should not be equated with the ability to predict how 576 

those states will respond to genetic interventions. The competitive performance of several 577 

human-pretrained checkpoints on supervised mouse tasks suggests that useful cellular informa- 578 

tion can transfer across species, even when the pretraining data do not explicitly cover the target 579 

organism. However, this transfer remains task-dependent, as dataset-fitted representations con- 580 

tinued to perform well in unsupervised mouse clustering. More importantly, the success of a 581 

frozen representation in observational tasks did not ensure accurate prediction of responses to 582 

unseen genetic perturbations. In our shared decoder framework, models could recover aspects 583

analyses that reveal how conclusions depend on the evaluation data. 609 

Together, these findings suggest that progress in single-cell foundation modeling should be 610

scale or leaderboard position alone. For model users, this means selecting checkpoints accord- 612

der the same data restrictions. For benchmark developers, it means evaluating complementary 614 

biological properties, distinguishing donor mixing from biological conservation, and using mean- 615 

ingful controls such as the zero-change predictor in perturbation studies. For model developers, 616 

it calls for greater attention to what pretraining objectives and input representations encourage 617 

models to preserve, alongside controlled studies of backbone adaptation, cross-species transfer, 618 

and perturbation-target information. Transparent, privacy-conscious documentation of pretrain- 619 

ing datasets is equally important for establishing credible evidence of generalization. Ultimately, 620 

the goal should not be to develop a single foundation model that leads every benchmark, but 621 

to build and evaluate representations whose biological strengths, limitations, and conditions of 622 

successful transfer are clearly understood. 623

Perturbation prediction framework and evaluation 754 

To prevent leakage of post-intervention expression into model inputs, each perturbed cell was 755 

randomly paired with an observed unperturbed control cell from the same cell-type stratum, us- 756
