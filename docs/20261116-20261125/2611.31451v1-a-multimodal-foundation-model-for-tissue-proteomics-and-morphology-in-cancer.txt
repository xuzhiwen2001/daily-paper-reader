Abstract
Cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment decisions increasingly rely on integrating tissue morphology
with specific molecular readouts of the tumor and its microenvironment. H&E histopathology provides scalable
morphology, whereas multiplex spatial proteomics quantifies dozens of proteins in situ with spatial context, but
remains selectively deployed, often with different marker panels across cohorts. This creates a translational
bottleneck: most patients lack high-depth molecular profiling, and existing models rarely integrate morphology
and spatial molecular data in a single framework. We present VirTues-M2, a multimodal foundation model that
jointly learns from H&E and spatial proteomics using a novel vision transformer-based architecture. By encod-
ing marker identity during pretraining, VirTues-M2 is robust to missing modalities, imperfect modality pairing
or registration, and variable marker panels, enabling unified inference for tissue phenotyping, panoptic cell
segmentation, virtual staining, annotation-free biomarker discovery, and reinforcement learning-based adaptive
marker panel selection to reduce assay burden. As a concrete demonstration of translational utility, in a newly
collected cohort of 1,053 treatment-naive non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with paired spatial pro-
teomics and H&E, VirTues-M2 discovers high-risk and low-risk tissue signatures that stratify overall survival in
both the discovery cohort and an independent validation cohort, outperforming current state-of-the-art strat-
ification schemes; all enabled by VirTues-M2’s ability to jointly analyze both modalities within a single model.

Introduction
Cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment decisions increasingly integrate evidence from multiple, potentially
complementary technologies, including tissue profiling, targeted molecular assays, and genomic testing, among
others. Within this landscape, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) histopathology remains the most broadly available
tissue readout in clinical practice, capturing morphological and architectural information at scale1. In parallel,
highly multiplexed spatial proteomics (Method of the Year 2024 by Nature Methods2) extends H&E by quantifying
dozens of proteins in situ while preserving spatial context. It enables molecularly specific readouts of cell identity,
functional state, and microenvironment organization that complement morphology, including a more detailed
characterization of the tumor immune microenvironment that impacts on immunotherapy response and resistance.
Despite rapid methodological progress and clear translational promise3,4,5, multiplex spatial proteomics is not yet
part of routine clinical oncology due to cost, tissue requirements, operational complexity and the lack of a clear
clinical framework for deployment. In addition, substantial variation in marker panels and acquisition protocols
across studies and sites limits assay standardization and cross-site comparability, further hindering broad clinical
deployment. Consequently, most patients are evaluated with H&E alone, and spatial proteomics is typically
reserved for selected research cohorts, despite its complementary molecular specificity. This asymmetry creates
a clear need for frameworks that can learn from cohorts with deep spatial readouts and transfer molecular and
microenvironmental insight to routine H&E under the heterogeneous and partially observed conditions of clinical

increasingly requires integrating complementary modalities: H&E captures morphology and architecture, while
spatial proteomics reveals molecular composition and cell state in their spatial context. Second, any clinically
useful approach must translate insights from high-depth assays into tools that remain effective even when only
routine histology is available.
A natural way to meet these challenges is to learn general-purpose tissue representations from large, unlabeled
data that can transfer across cohorts, tasks, and input availability. In this context, recent AI advances in
self-supervised learning have led to the development of foundation models (FMs) pretrained on large corpora of
unlabeled data6,7. These models learn general tissue representations that support diverse downstream tasks with
minimal annotation, including prediction, stratification, and quantitative tissue phenotyping8,9,10. However, most
2


existing FMs are built for a single modality (unimodal), either H&E8,9,11 or specific multiplex assays10,12, which
limits their ability to learn how morphology relates to molecular cell states and microenvironment organization.
Other non-foundational approaches13,14 use, due to previous unavailability of large multimodal patient cohorts,
virtual staining to infer multiplex signals for a fixed panel of protein markers and then tailor the downstream
pipeline to particular tasks, rather than training a generalist representation jointly across modalities. This
motivates a single multimodal FM that jointly learns from routine histology and spatial proteomics, so that
specific molecular insights can inform standard analysis of slides while enabling unified inference when both
modalities are available.
To this end, we introduce the multimodal Virtual Tissues platform (VirTues-M2), a novel FM for tissue biology
that jointly learns from routine histopathology and multiplex spatial proteomics within a single architecture. We
first address a primary bottleneck in the field concerning insufficient scale and diversity of multimodal training data
by assembling the largest open-source multiplex spatial proteomics collection with partially paired H&E images,
spanning 5,849 patients across 37 studies, 17,275 multiplex tissues, acquired with multiple spatial proteomics
technologies including cyclic approaches such as PhenoCycler (formerly CODEX)15 and one-shot high-plex
approaches such as Orion16, as well as imaging mass cytometry (IMC)17 and multiplexed ion beam imaging
(MIBI)18 technologies, covering diverse cancer indications and tissue preparations (Fig. 1a-e). VirTues-M2 is
enabled by a new vision transformer-based architecture that can embed heterogeneous tissue modalities into a
unified representation space, while remaining robust to incomplete pairing, imperfect alignment, and substantial
variability in spatial proteomics marker panels (Fig. 1f). By incorporating protein marker identity directly into
the model and pretraining across diverse cohorts and marker panels, VirTues-M2 harmonizes heterogeneous
spatial proteomics assays into a common representation, enabling cross-study comparability while learning tissue
features that connect morphology to molecular phenotype.
These capabilities translate into practical advances that are difficult to achieve with unimodal models8,9,11,10,12
or late fusion schemes19,20,21 (Fig. 1g). VirTues-M2 supports a single workflow across H&E-only, spatial
proteomics-only, and joint multimodal inference. It improves one-shot cell segmentation, typing, and downstream
prediction by capturing both tissue architecture and molecular cell state. In addition, it enables cross-modal
inference, including virtual staining from H&E and prediction of unmeasured markers from partial panels, reducing
assay burden when tissue, cost, or turnaround time constrain profiling. Coupled with a reinforcement learning
(RL)-based marker-selection strategy, the model adaptively recommends which markers to measure starting from
an H&E slide, guiding clinicians and translational laboratories toward minimal, informative assays that approach
full-panel performance. A systematic scaling law analysis shows continued gains with increasing pretraining data
volume, while larger models do not consistently improve performance at the current data scale. The analysis
further demonstrates that the design of the VirTues-M2 transformer enables learning from unpaired observations
and paired but unregistered measurements, extending the usable training corpus beyond precisely registered
multimodal data and informing priorities for future data generation. Finally, VirTues-M2 enables annotation-free
discovery of spatial biomarkers that capture complementary prognostic information in tissue morphology and
protein expression within a single pretrained model. In a large treatment-naive NSCLC cohort with paired
IMC and H&E22, combining these signatures improves patient stratification over either modality alone and
established clinical predictors. The learned signature classifiers transfer without retraining to an independent
NSCLC cohort, with the strongest performance arising from joint analysis of both modalities within VirTues-M2’s
native multimodal representation.

Results
A multimodal foundation model architecture for tissue biology
Most tissue foundation models remain unimodal: H&E models capture relevant molecular variation only
insofar as it is reflected in the staining9,8,23,12, while spatial proteomics models learn from measured protein
expression without access to the complementary morphological information in H&E10,24,25. Neither learns the
correspondence between these modalities during pretraining; a relationship so far mostly investigated through
downstream analyses26. Existing pipelines that consider both modalities impute a fixed marker panel from
H&E or fine-tune modality-specific encoders for individual endpoints13,14. These approaches resemble early
vision-language systems that combined separately pretrained components27,28,29, rather than learning shared
representations through native multimodal pretraining and token-level fusion as done in state-of-the-art vision-
language models30,31. Tissue biology, however, presents an additional challenge: the molecular modality is a
3


complementary spatial measurement rather than a natural language description. Integrating it with morphology
requires accounting for its spatial structure and substantial heterogeneity while learning cross-modal relationships
from predominantly unpaired data.
VirTues-M2 closes this gap by enabling joint multimodal pretraining across H&E and multiplex spatial proteomics
within a single architecture, so that cross-modal relationships are learned as part of the representation itself rather
than added as a late-stage fusion step. To enable such joint multimodal pretraining, we assembled the largest
open-source multiplex (mx) dataset with partially paired H&E images, focusing on cancer tissues (Methods).
This corpus encompasses 5,849 patients across 37 cohorts, comprising 17,275 multiplex and 2,948 paired H&E
tissue samples (Fig. 1a), including a newly collected large patient cohort from the University Hospital Zurich
(USZ). These tissues were processed into ∼933,000 multiplex and 212,000 H&E crops with varying levels of
alignment: pairs registered pixel-wise, unregistered but paired samples (from the same tissue or patient), or
unpaired H&E or multiplex crops. This heterogeneous alignment structure reflects data availability within clinical
workflows, where perfect registration between modalities is often infeasible or impractical. Further, the multiplex
data spans four acquisition technologies, more than thirteen primary cancer types, and two tissue formats, i.e.,
WSI and region-of-interest (ROI), enabling generalization across heterogeneous experimental settings (Fig. 1b-d).
Also, to strengthen unimodal histopathology coverage, we augmented our multimodal pretraining corpus with
three million additional unpaired H&E crops, ensuring robust performance when only H&E is available (Fig. 1b).
Because large H&E collections contain many near-duplicate regions, we reduced redundancy by grouping crops
by visual similarity and sampling across groups to prioritize diverse, informative morphologies, thereby broadening
the histologic coverage seen during pretraining (Fig. 1c)32.
On the learning side, VirTues-M2 introduces a single-encoder, early-fusion architecture that jointly learns from
multiplex spatial proteomics and H&E histology (Fig. 1f). In contrast to approaches that pretrain separate
unimodal backbones and fuse embeddings only downstream, VirTues-M2 maps each modality to patch tokens
and processes all tokens jointly throughout the network. This enables cross-modal relationships to be learned
during pretraining and yields a single backbone for H&E-only, multiplex-only, or joint inference (Methods). To
initialize this backbone, we used Hibou, a large pretrained H&E model that captures common morphological
patterns in routine slides23, followed by multimodal continual pretraining (see Fig. S4d and Fig. 2b for ablations
on the size and choice of initialization model). A key technical barrier to early fusion is the heterogeneity
of multiplex assays. VirTues-M2 was pretrained on 314 distinct protein markers across 37 integrated clinical
studies. Marker panels vary substantially across cohorts in both composition and channel order, requiring the
model to identify which protein each channel measures to learn biologically meaningful representations. Existing
approaches often assume fixed marker dictionaries12,25,13 or enforce a specific ordering25, limiting robustness to
alternative panel configurations. VirTues-M2 addresses this with marker-aware multiplex tokenization (Fig. S1).
Following Wenckstern et al. 10, we condition multiplex patch embeddings on marker identity using protein
sequence embeddings from ESM-233, enabling channels to be interpreted by biological meaning rather than index
position. This design supports learning across heterogeneous panels and accommodates markers not observed
during pretraining by embedding their protein sequences and incorporating them as additional channels.
The challenge of training VirTues-M2 encoder then requires tailored self-supervised objectives that learn both
local, spatially resolved features and global, slide-level semantics under missing modalities and imperfect alignment.
To this end, we combine complementary objectives at two scales adapted from DINOv234 (Fig. 1f). At the patch
level, we utilize a masked-patch prediction objective (multimodal iBOT35) to learn stable local representations
that support spatial tasks. While at the crop level, we adopt a self-distillation objective that matches global
representations across augmented views and modalities (multimodal DINO36), supplemented by a kernel density
estimation (KDE) objective to encourage well-structured global embeddings for patient-level prediction. To
accommodate fully registered pairs, loosely matched pairs, and unimodal samples, we incorporate modality dropout
so training remains effective when one modality is absent, while joint token processing in the shared encoder
promotes integration when both modalities are available (Methods, Fig. S2). After training, we benchmark
VirTues-M2 by freezing the pretrained backbone and training only task-specific heads across a suite of clinically
and biologically motivated tasks, including diagnostic and prognostic prediction, panoptic cell segmentation,
virtual staining, spatial biomarker discovery, and minimal marker set design (Fig. 1g).

On H&E, we benchmarked VirTues-M2 across nine external cohorts, none of which appear in the pretraining corpus,
spanning diverse cancer types and clinical tasks39,40,41,37,42,43. In colorectal cancer (CRC) tissue classification,
distinguishing between four colorectal tissue categories, VirTues-M2 achieved the highest performance (macro-
average F1 of 0.79) compared with established H&E foundation models including UNI8 and Hibou23 across
model sizes, outperforming UNI2-H (macro-average F1 of 0.75) despite fewer parameters (Fig. 2b). Across all
nine H&E benchmarks, VirTues-M2 ranked first overall and achieved comparable average performance to larger
state-of-the-art models (p = 0.685) (Fig. 2c,d; Fig. S3; Tab. S1; Tab. S2).
On multiplex spatial proteomics, we evaluated two breast cancer cohorts including triple-negative breast cancer
(TNBC) and diverse breast cancers (BC) across six clinical prediction tasks (Fig. 2e,f; Tab. S3; Tab. S4)44,45.
We compared VirTues-M2 against multiplex foundation models (VirTues10 and the channel-agnostic masked
autoencoder (MAE), CA-MAE25) and supervised baselines (ResNet5024 and SPACE-GM46). VirTues-M2
matched the leading multiplex foundation model on most tasks, outperforming it on response prediction at both
the pre- and on-treatment time points (p = 0.006 and p = 0.012).
To evaluate multimodal performance, we benchmarked models on an NSCLC cohort with paired but unregistered
tissue cores, assessing grading (Fig. 2g) and subtyping (Fig. 2h; Tab. S5). We compared VirTues-M2 receiving
only an H&E or multiplex input versus having access to both modalities. In addition, we include a late-fusion
baseline that combines embeddings from independently pretrained H&E and multiplex state-of-the-art unimodal
foundation models (UNI + VirTues), reflecting common strategies for multimodal integration with disjoint
encoders. In unimodal settings, H&E and multiplex models achieved comparable performance for both tasks, and
VirTues-M2 matched these baselines when restricted to a single modality. When provided with both modalities,
VirTues-M2 outperformed its unimodal counterparts as well as the late-fusion baseline on NSCLC grading
(p = 3.40e−4 versus H&E-only; p = 5.00e−4 versus multiplex-only) and outperformed late fusion (p = 0.0267).
On NSCLC subtyping, VirTues-M2 using both modalities outperformed VirTues-M2 using multiplex alone
(p = 2.86e−4).
Because comprehensive, well-registered spatial proteomics data remain costly and scarce, we systematically
examined how VirTues-M2’s performance depends on pretraining data volume, composition, and model capacity
to determine where future gains are best invested: The pretraining scaling law analysis showed that performance
scales consistently with data volume (Fig. S4a). However, balanced sampling of H&E and multiplex images
is important to overall downstream performance (Fig. S4b). The analysis further confirms that the current
model size is optimized to the training data available, i.e., Fig. S4d shows that model capacity is capped by the
amount of multiplex data available. Lastly, the multimodal scaling law analysis underlines VirTues-M2’s strength
of learning robust representations from a heterogeneity of multimodal data typical of clinical cohorts: The model
exhibits continual performance increase even when training only on non-registered and unpaired H&E-multiplex
data instead of perfectly registered H&E and multiplex images (see Supplementary Information for details).
Multimodal embeddings recover complementary morphologic and molecular tissue
structure
To characterize the information captured by VirTues-M2 representations across modalities, we performed
unsupervised tissue segmentation using patch embeddings from H&E, multiplex, or joint inputs. We embedded all
patches from a ROI and visualized the spatial organization of the learned features by projecting embeddings onto
the first three principal components, mapped to RGB, and by clustering them with k-means (k = 6) (Fig. 2i;
Fig. S5).
H&E-derived embeddings primarily separated morphologic compartments, including tumor regions, vasculature,
and fibrotic or stromal areas (Fig. 2i, top), whereas multiplex-derived embeddings emphasized molecular defined
single-cell phenotypes, conditioned on specifically characterized neighborhoods (Fig. 2i, middle). Several broad
structures, such as the tumor compartment, were delineated by both modalities. In contrast, joint multimodal
embeddings preserved the compartment-level organization captured by H&E while simultaneously resolving
the finer-grained, molecularly defined states highlighted by multiplex, effectively integrating complementary

VirTues-M2 enables one-shot cell phenotyping and segmentation
Cell instance segmentation and phenotyping are foundational steps in quantitative tissue analysis for multiplexed
imaging, yet in clinical and research practice they are typically performed with multi-stage pipelines that separate
7


boundary detection from phenotype assignment. In multiplex imaging, segmentation is commonly driven by
generic image processing or nucleus-based heuristics, followed by marker-intensity quantification and gating to
assign cell types (Fig. 3a), resulting in the use of separate task-specific models50,51. These staged workflows
require assay-specific tuning, propagate errors from segmentation to phenotyping, and make it difficult to exploit
the complementarity between morphology and molecular specificity when both modalities are available.
VirTues-M2 enables one-shot panoptic inference that predicts cell instances and phenotypes jointly within a
single model, incorporating both morphology from H&E as well as molecular information from multiplex data. To
evaluate this setting, we used VirTues-M2 as a frozen backbone within a fused CellViT52 and InstanSeg-style53
architecture and fine-tuned only the decoder head to jointly segment cells and assign phenotypes (Methods,
Fig. S6).
We evaluated cell phenotyping on the registered Schürch et al. 54 cohort, which was excluded from training
all segmentation decoders, thereby testing transfer to a separate cohort without decoder retraining. Per-class
F1 scores were obtained by convolving the predicted phenotyping mask with the ground truth cell instance
segmentation mask (Fig. 3b), which allowed us to benchmark the segmentation model across several modality
configurations. The strongest macro-averaged performance came from VirTues-M2 with both modalities,
suggesting an advantage over the corresponding unimodal variants. For cell instance segmentation, we measure
the F1-Score across different Intersection over Union (IoU) thresholds (Fig. 3c). VirTues-M2 alone not only
outperforms stardist55 and InstanSeg53 in the H&E setting and matches their performance in the unimodal
multiplex case, but performs best in a multimodal setting with an F1-score at an IoU at 0.5 of 0.650 versus
0.452 using only H&E and 0.349 using only multiplex. Qualitative examples highlight why multimodality matters
for one-shot panoptic inference (Fig. 3d; Fig. S7). Multiplex-only inference recovered phenotype-specific signal
but showed reduced accuracy in delineating individual cell boundaries or missing cells entirely. H&E-only inference
delineated boundaries accurately but more frequently confused phenotypes. Joint multimodal inference combined
accurate boundary delineation with improved phenotype assignment, yielding more coherent panoptic maps
consistent with the quantitative results (Fig. 3b-d).
To assess the cross-cohort panoptic segmentation capabilities of our segmentation decoder, we further bench-
marked cell phenotyping and instance segmentation on TNBC patient cohort imaged with IMC44. Compared to
the phenotype segmentation performance of VirTues10, VirTues-M2 matches the performance across all cell
phenotypes (Fig. 3e) and outperforms the instance segmentation baselines (Fig. 3f). Similarly, using only H&E
as the input, the VirTues-M2 segmentation head outperforms stardist55 and InstanSeg53 (Fig. 3h) and is able
to detect small cells that where not detected in the original authors annotations (Fig. 3i).
Virtual staining and marker completion from H&E and multiplex panels
Multiplex spatial proteomics enables molecularly specific tissue profiling but remains constrained by cost,
turnaround time, and limited tissue availability, often necessitating reduced marker panels or selective deployment.
VirTues-M2 addresses these constraints by enabling virtual staining and marker completion from a shared
multimodal representation, allowing prediction of protein markers either from routinely available H&E or from a
complementary subset of multiplex channels without additional staining. We evaluated VirTues-M2’s virtual
staining abilities in two settings (Fig. 4a): multiplex panel completion, which predicts a held-out target marker
from a complementary subset of markers, simulating the setting of reduced panels, and H&E virtual staining,
which predicts marker expression from H&E images alone. For multiplex panel completion, we compared against
VirTues, a multiplex FM which includes the capability of marker reconstruction and can incorporate all 148
markers we evaluate. For evaluation, we use three established metrics: Pearson correlation (PC), structural
similarity index measure (SSIM)56, and MSE as all of them measure different positive properties of a virtual
staining (Methods).
We froze the VirTues-M2 encoder and trained only a decoder (Fig. S9) across 18 cohorts spanning three major
cancer types, i.e., breast (n = 9), CRC (n = 6), and lung (n = 7), some cohorts include several cancer types, as
well as multiple technologies and organs57,22,58,59,60,61,62,45,63,64,65,66,67,68,69,16,54,70 (Methods). Using a decoder
that supports arbitrary marker sets and cohort-specific panels, VirTues-M2 matched the virtual staining quality
of VirTues across all cohorts and markers, showing no significant difference in average Pearson correlation
(Fig. 4c) despite keeping the backbone frozen while VirTues was pretrained end-to-end for this task. The
multimodal representation therefore retains the information that multiplex inpainting requires without being
explicitly optimized toward it. Per-marker, cohort and metric results are reported in Tab. S17, Tab. S18 and

Minimal diagnostic tests and tissue-efficient marker panels
Although virtual staining and marker completion can reduce reliance on exhaustive panels, clinical and translational
workflows still require principled choices about which markers to measure experimentally and which information
can be recovered computationally. This choice is particularly consequential in spatial proteomics, where additional
markers increase assay complexity and consume limited tissue74,75. Therefore, we integrated VirTues-M2 with
the reinforcement learning framework MAVIS (Multi-modal Active VIew Selection)74, that uses foundation model
embeddings to sequentially select informative measurements in a tissue-dependent manner (Fig. 5a; Methods).
We benchmarked this setting in a clinically realistic workflow that begins with an H&E slide and adaptively
queries a minimal set of markers, while targeting the performance achieved with a full multiplex panel. For
evaluation, we used one-shot cell phenotyping as the evaluation task. We compared this MAVIS-based approach
with three baseline selection strategies: random selection with a fixed budget, random selection with an adaptive
stopping rule, and importance ranking that adds markers based on their marginal utility estimated individually
(Fig. 5b). Because the VirTues-M2 segmentation decoder requires a joint embedding that incorporates H&E
and multiplex inputs for multimodal segmentation, unimodal foundation models that cannot jointly encode both
modalities were not directly applicable as baselines.
MAVIS coupled with VirTues-M2 matched full-panel performance using only eight selected markers, significantly
reducing the required panel size (Fig. 5b) and outperforming all baseline strategies. The selection trajectories
were interpretable (Fig. 5c, Fig. S8b): because cell instance segmentation was already supported by H&E
morphology, early queries prioritized markers whose cellular identities H&E cannot resolve, T cell compartments
(CD3e, CD4, CD8a), endothelium (CD31) and B cells (CD20), which are morphologically indistinguishable from
surrounding stroma or from one another. CD31 contributed the largest single gain, as endothelial cells cannot
be assigned at the single-cell level in H&E despite vascular structures being visible (Fig. S8a), whereas later
selections such as Pan-CK, SMA and Ki67 added little once lineage identity was established. These results
demonstrate that VirTues-M2 can be coupled to sequential decision-making to identify compact, task-relevant
marker panels from an H&E-first starting point.

Foundation model-derived risk signatures enhance patient stratification
Current spatial biomarker discovery is largely driven by expert-defined phenotypes and hand-crafted features
of tissue architecture44,62,65. Beyond being labor-intensive, this ties discovered signatures to a specific marker
11


panel and annotation scheme, so applying them to a new cohort requires the panel to be reproduced and
the phenotypes re-annotated, which limits transfer to the cohort in which they were defined. Foundation
model embeddings capture cellular phenotypes and their local tissue context without requiring cell type or
region annotations, enabling systematic discovery of outcome-associated signatures and their evaluation in new
cohorts without re-annotation. To exploit these properties for biomarker discovery, we developed an automated,
annotation-free pipeline that uses VirTues-M2 embeddings to identify survival-associated cell populations and
locate their counterparts in independent cohorts (Fig. 6a; Methods).
In a cohort of 1, 053 treatment-naive NSCLC patients with paired IMC22 and H&E tissue cores, we used this
pipeline to discover survival-associated cell populations. For each core, we extracted VirTues-M2 cell tokens
from both modalities, capturing cellular phenotype and local microenvironmental context (Fig. S14 validates
the inclusion of contextual information). We then performed high-resolution unsupervised Leiden clustering to
define fine-grained cell states and computed each patient’s relative abundance of cells per cluster. Clusters were
evaluated by Cox regression on overall survival, adjusted for demographic and diagnostic covariates, and the
most significant clusters with negative and positive coefficients were defined as a low-risk (LRS) and high-risk
signature (HRS), respectively (Methods). On the held-out test set (n = 316), both signatures combined into
a single abundance-based score stratified patients using pre-specified tertiles without cutoff optimization (mx:
C = 0.634, HR = 2.55; H&E: C = 0.642, HR = 2.96), exceeding demographic (C = 0.577), diagnostic
(C = 0.605), H&E morphology (C = 0.553) and immune cell ratio (C = 0.570) baselines (Fig. 6b). The two
modality scores agreed only weakly (r = 0.19), and their combination improved stratification further (C = 0.646,
HR = 3.09, p = 5.1e−10), indicating complementary rather than redundant information.
To interpret the tissue programs captured by these signatures, we examined their cellular composition and spatial
organization. In the multiplex embedding, LRS occupied immune-rich and HRS tumor-dominated neighborhoods,
while in H&E, LRS occupied highly dense regions of tissue (Fig. 6c; Fig. S15), indicating that both signatures are
defined by microenvironmental context rather than by cell identity alone. Consistent with this, LRS was enriched
for B and T cells and depleted of tumor relative to the cohort (Fig. 6d), and within the T cell compartment
specifically enriched for stem-like TCF1/7+ CD4 and CD8 populations (Fig. 6e). In LRS niches, every cell
type contacted B and T cells 1.3- to 7.1-fold more often than in HRS (Fig. 6f; signature-specific neighborhood
compositions in Fig. S16), and all cell types were more tightly aggregated (nearest-neighbor distance 0.53-0.79
of random expectation), which is consistent with organized lymphoid aggregates (Fig. 6g). HRS niches showed
the reverse: contact was shifted toward tumor and hypoxic tumor cells, and all cell types approached random
spatial mixing (0.78-0.91), consistent with more disorganized tumor tissue. The H&E signatures recovered a
similar relationship from morphology alone (LRS nuclei rounder and densely packed, HRS elongated and sparse),
with cells of both modalities more spatially aggregated in LRS (Fig. 6h,i), indicating that tissue organization is
where the two signatures converge.
Finally, we tested whether the biomarkers transfer to an independent cohort. We annotated LRS and HRS
signatures using random forest classifiers trained on the Cords et al. 22 discovery split to recover signature
membership from cell tokens alone, applied unchanged to the cell tokens of 39 NSCLC patients from Monkman
et al. 59. Patients were again stratified by predicted population abundance, using a median rather than a tertile
split given the small cohort size. The mx and multimodal signatures separated survival in the expected direction
(mx: HR = 3.02, C = 0.645, p = 0.029; multimodal: HR = 2.87, C = 0.636, p = 0.039), and the H&E
signature showed the same direction of effect without reaching significance at α = 0.05 (HR = 2.38, C = 0.596,
p = 0.095). By contrast, the annotated T cell and immune cell ratios provided by the original study did not
separate survival at all (p = 0.91 and p = 0.42; Fig. 6j). The transferred H&E populations also preserved the
LRS-versus-HRS morphological contrast observed in discovery (Fig. 6i), indicating that their relative phenotype
is retained across cohorts. These results show that VirTues-M2 reveals complementary prognostic information in
tissue morphology and protein expression through annotation-free discovery of interpretable cellular signatures,
while supporting transfer to independent cohorts.
3
Discussion
VirTues-M2 brings routine histology and multiplex spatial proteomics into a single foundation model, enabling
morphology and molecular phenotype to inform tissue analysis through native multimodal pretraining. Alongside
the model, we assembled the largest open-source multimodal tissue dataset to date, spanning 5,849 patients,
37 studies, four acquisition technologies, and 314 protein markers. Learning across incomplete pairing and
heterogeneous panels allows diverse clinical studies to contribute to a shared encoder that supports H&E-only,
12


multiplex-only, and joint analysis without task-specific backbone fine-tuning. Multimodal learning need not
improve every endpoint: its value lies in retaining strong performance where one modality captures the relevant
information and improving prediction where the modalities contribute complementary evidence.
Most prominently, the learned multimodal representations of VirTues-M2 open new opportunities for discov-
ering clinically relevant tissue biology beyond predefined cell types and expert-selected features. In NSCLC,
VirTues-M2-based annotation-free discovery identifies signatures that provide stronger patient stratification
than current clinical schemes and established morphological and immune-based predictors. Joint analysis further
improves stratification over either modality alone, suggesting that tissue morphology and protein expression
reveal complementary aspects of prognostic biology. Their integration offers a way to investigate how molecular
phenotypes and tissue organization together relate to disease outcome. Importantly, VirTues-M2 also enables
recovering related prognostic signatures from H&E alone, extending the reach of multimodal biomarker discovery:
molecularly characterized cohorts can reveal tissue patterns that can then be studied at the scale of routine
histology, including in patients for whom multiplex measurements are unavailable.
Combining modalities can also change how tissue is characterized and which measurements are required. A
single one-shot model integrates morphological boundaries and molecular specificity for joint cell segmentation
and typing, while virtual staining and panel completion extend analysis to partially measured samples. Coupled
with an RL-based marker-selection strategy74, VirTues-M2 guides subsequent measurements and approaches
full-panel performance with fewer markers. These capabilities suggest a workflow in which models help extract
information from existing measurements and select the additional measurements needed, potentially reducing
cost, acquisition time, and tissue consumption.
To understand which investments would most advance multimodal tissue modeling, we systematically varied
model capacity, pretraining data volume, and cross-modal pairing. These results highlight the joint importance
of data scale, biological diversity, and cross-modal pairing. VirTues-M2 benefits from precisely registered
measurements, while its transformer design also enables learning from unpaired observations and paired but
unregistered data, allowing a broader range of clinical cohorts to contribute to pretraining. Such analyses can
guide investment in future data generation: where should we expand data diversity, and where will additional
multimodal measurements most advance biological discovery and model capabilities? Coordinated community
efforts to collect and openly share these data could establish a common foundation for tissue modeling, allowing
each cohort to advance biological discovery and clinical applications beyond its original purpose.
Several steps remain before these capabilities can inform clinical practice. Prospective multi-site evaluation is
needed to establish robustness and assess institutional and demographic confounding. Biomarker transfer requires
larger independent cohorts: While this study expands the available multimodal data with a newly assembled cohort
of >1,000 NSCLC patients with paired H&E and spatial proteomics for biomarker discovery, the independent
survival validation was limited in patient numbers and requires confirmation in larger external cohorts. Molecular
predictions remain constrained by the measured input and require calibrated uncertainty and explicit assessment of
failure modes. Adaptive marker acquisition likewise needs experimental validation to establish its practical benefits.
VirTues-M2 establishes native multimodal learning as a foundation for connecting tissue morphology with molec-
ular phenotype across heterogeneous clinical datasets. Its significance extends beyond combining measurements:
learning their relationships enables complementary tissue biology to inform biomarker discovery and allows related
prognostic patterns to be investigated through routine H&E. This provides a concrete direction for digital
pathology 2.0, where molecularly characterized cohorts contribute knowledge that can be applied beyond the
patients directly profiled. With larger paired datasets and prospective validation, the opportunity is to bring the
depth of molecular tissue analysis to the scale at which pathology is practiced.

Methods
Data acquisition.
Multiplex data collection. For pretraining of VirTues-M2, a total of 37 spatial proteomics cohorts were
collected either from open-source data portals or were requested from the authors of the respective studies;
no private or in-house data was used. The collection covers data from four technologies, namely IMC, Orion,
PhenoCycler (previously CODEX) and MIBI, encompasses 314 distinct protein markers and includes around 270
million cells from 17,275 different multiplex tissues from 5,849 patients originating from more than 13 organs
as shown in Tab. S29. While IMC is an ablative imaging method making subsequent same-slide H&E tissue
staining impossible, the immunofluorescence-based technologies PhenoCycler and Orion both allow imaging
of morphological and proteomic information on the same tissue section. As the typical resolution of IMC is
the coarsest among the covered technologies, all images were resized to match the IMC datasets’ 1µm/px
resolution. Afterwards, for registered images, a tissue segmentation model26 is used to segment the tissue
from the background based on the H&E image. For segmenting unpaired or unaligned multiplex images, we
use the log transformed maximum signal across the channels and apply an adaptive threshold76 to segment
the background from the tissue. We preprocess the spatial proteomic images by creating crops of 256 × 256
pixels using the tissue mask to restrict them to tissue regions of the image. As our dataset corpus comprises
a large amount of tissue cores consisting of pre-determined regions of interests, we argue that classical grid
tiling as commonly used for whole slide histopathology images is not suitable. To gain the highest coverage
of these regions of interest and discard as little tissue as possible, we formulate the generation of crops as a
greedy optimization problem in which the coverage of the tissue should be maximized with the least amount of
crops using a target coverage of 95% of the tissue space. Post crop generation, the 99th percentile is calculated
channel-wise for each mx cohort, only considering parts of the image containing tissue. For normalization of
the images, after loading a crop, we clip the marker intensities between zero and the 99th percentile of the
corresponding channels, followed by rescaling the marker expressions to the range of 0 to 1 and applying gaussian
blurring with a kernel size of 3 × 3 and a unit variance to reduce noise in the image77. The total of 314 unique
proteins of all antibody panels used in the various cohorts were manually matched with UniProt identifier, based
on which ESM-233 embeddings of the canonical UniProt amino acid sequence are generated and saved to disk.
H&E data collection.
For the histopathology data collection, we additionally curate a unimodal dataset using
14,558 whole slide images from 5,538 patients from the TCGA78 and GTEx79 databases as shown in Tab. S30,
selecting WSIs of the same tissue types as included in our mx database. Matching the resolution of the spatial
proteomics data collection, all WSIs are resized to a resolution of 1µm/px and non-overlapping crops from
tissue regions are generated resulting in 29M unique crops. Following Vo et al. 80 and Chen et al. 32, we embed
all crops using the histopathology FM UNI8 and perform a hierarchical clustering and sampling to reduce visual
redundancy and promote morphological diversity. Hierarchical clustering is applied jointly to all H&E crops from
both multiplex and H&E-only cohorts, comprising a total of 29.5 million crops. The clustering tree depth is
set to four, with the number of clusters at each level (from bottom to top) fixed to {29, 500; 7, 690; 480; 60},
consistent with established data curation guidelines. From the resulting hierarchy, we select a subset of 2.95M
crops from the H&E-only data to complement the mx data. Sampling is performed at the bottom-cluster level
by selecting crops whose distances to the cluster centroid are approximately uniformly distributed, ensuring
balanced coverage of common and rare morphologies. Normalization of the H&E images is done using the same
standardization values as the VirTues-M2 initialization model, Hibou-L.
All multiplex cohorts are split at a 80-20 train-test ratio at the patient level for later model evaluation, preventing
data leakage from patient-level batch effects. Data from TCGA or GTEx is not used for model evaluation
purposes. Thus, all curated crops extracted from these databases are used during pretraining.
VirTues-M2 architecture.
The VirTues-M2 architecture is designed to accommodate the heterogeneous nature of spatial proteomics images
while also processing conventional histopathology images. In contrast to the fixed RGB channels of brightfield
imaging, the channels of a spatial proteomics image each correspond to a distinct protein marker, and both the
number and identity of these markers vary substantially across acquisitions depending on the imaging technology,
disease of interest and organ. Traditional vision architectures are neither able to handle such channel diversity nor
20


designed to process different imaging modalities or to bridge across modalities in a latent space81. Furthermore,
commonly employed (pre-)training strategies for vision FMs, such as DINOv234, are not designed to account for
multiple modalities in their training objectives or data sampling. Tackling these unique challenges, both from
an architectural and training point of view, while efficiently leveraging available data, is a core contribution of
the proposed VirTues-M2 model. To integrate cross-modal interactions of the pixel-wise aligned multimodal
images and to enhance model scalability, VirTues-M2 is built using an early-fusion approach with modality-wise
tokenizers and a vision transformer backbone, as shown in Fig. 1.
Multiplex tokenization.
The multiplex tokenization of VirTues-M2 builds upon the tokenization and marker
attention layers introduced by VirTues10: Given a normalized multiplex image of size W × H × C pixels, each
channel c ∈C is tiled into a grid of N = W
dp × H
dp patches of size dp × dp = 14 × 14 pixels. Each patch is
flattened and passed through the same linear layer with output dimensionality dmodel. In parallel, for each
channel the corresponding protein is embedded using the Protein Language Model ESM-233 and projected
to the dimension dmodel via a learnable linear projection. These projected protein embeddings are then fused
via addition with the corresponding tokens of the mx image. Next, we group the projected image patches by
their respective grid position (i, j), resulting in N sets {xc|c ∈C}ij and append an additional learnable patch
multiplex summary token to each of these sets. Lastly, we apply two layers of marker attention10 to each of
these sets and retain from the outputs only the now encoded N patch multiplex summary tokens. This yields
the final multiplex tokens xmx ∈RNxdmodel. Note that this procedure transforms the channel-dependent input size,
into a channel-independent number of tokens, alleviating the computation overhead of the subsequent vision
transformer (see Fig. S1). The detailed hyperparameters of the multiplex tokenizer can be found in Tab. S31.
H&E tokenization.
For tokenization of the H&E images, we use the traditional RGB image tokenization82:
Each H&E image is divided into patches of size dp ×dp ×3. These patches are then flattened and passed through
a linear layer of output dimensionality dmodel, resulting in xHE ∈RN×dmodel. This ensures an evenly balanced number
of tokens for both modalities.
Unimodal processing.
In settings where only single modalities are available, we only tokenize the present
modality and impute the missing modality tokens using the learnable modality-specific masking tokens □mx ∈Rdmodel
or □HE ∈Rdmodel, respectively. This allows us not only to rely on paired multimodal images but also to utilize
unimodal cohorts during both training and inference, extending the model’s utility.
VirTues-M2 encoder.
The encoder of VirTues-M2 is based on the vision transformer architecture, namely a
ViT-Large82 with dmodel = 1024, SwiGLU as the activation function for the feed-forward layers and the attention
heads, and 4 learnable register tokens xREG ∈R4×dmodel 83. More detailed model hyperparameters are shown in
Tab. S31. Shared learnable positional embeddings are added to both modalities’ tokens xmx and xHE to encode the
spatial relationships between tokens. The input of the VirTues-M2 encoder is the concatenation of a learnable
classification token xCLS ∈R1×dmodel, the register tokens and modality-specific tokens
xVirTues-M2 = (xCLS, xREG, xmx, xHE)
(1)
resulting in (2N + 5) input tokens independent of the present combination of modalities and spatial proteomics
channels. During the forward pass through the vision transformer all tokens attend to each other, yielding
contextualized output embeddings ˜xVirTues-M2 = (˜xCLS, ˜xREG, ˜xmx, ˜xHE). The classification token embedding ˜xCLS is
meant to capture global information across modalities, whereas ˜xmx and ˜xHE capture modality-specific information
while being informed by the other modality.
During pretraining, the encoder is additionally equipped with a shared multi-layer perceptron head, which is
applied to the classification token embedding and modality-specific tokens, mapping these to learnable prototypes
for loss computations.
Multimodal pretraining.
To enable the model to learn both unimodal and cross-modal representations, we build on the self-supervised
student-teacher pretraining framework of DINOv234 and extend it to: a) allow uni- and multimodal training; b)
capture global cross-modality information in a single token xCLS; c) embed the diversity of channels captured
21


in the spatial proteomics images; d) facilitate later in-painting of a full modality. To this end, we modify the
iBOT-based patch-level objective35 as well the DINO-based crop-level objective36, replace the default KoLeo
regularizer84, adapt the data augmentation for multimodal and multiplexed inputs and adjust the data sampling
to varying cohort sizes.

Data augmentation.
For both modalities, we first generate two global views and multiple local views for
each field of view using randomly resized cropping and geometric transformations. Global crops are obtained by
loading a 256x256 image crop and cropping & resizing them to a fixed resolution (e.g., 224 × 224), whereas
local crops focus on smaller scales and are resized to a lower resolution (e.g., 98 × 98). Random rotations
and random symmetries composed of flips and 90° rotations are applied to the crops. The same geometric
transformations are applied jointly to all channels and modalities to preserve spatial correspondence between
stains or markers. Multiplex images are capped at 30 channels, images exceeding this are randomly subsampled
to 30, which keeps GPU memory usage more uniform, prevents sudden memory spikes and hinders the model to
overfit on certain antibody panels per cohort.
The following channel augmentations are applied for both global and local views. For multiplex imaging, the
subsequent photometric augmentations are designed to mimic realistic variability in staining intensity, channel
balance and image quality. A randomly selected subset comprising 75–90% of the original marker set is used
for each crop, ensuring that different crops within the same image use different marker subsets. A random
brightness scaling and a random channel shift are applied with high probability, such that the overall signal
intensity and the relative contributions of individual channels vary between views87. In addition, Gaussian blurring
is randomly applied to both global and local crops, with higher blur probabilities for local crops, to simulate
defocus, motion blur and other optical degradations that frequently occur in microscopy.
For H&E brightfield images, a color jitter transform perturbs the brightness, contrast, saturation and hue of the
RGB channels, thereby modeling differences in scanner calibration, illumination and staining protocol between
slides and laboratories. Furthermore, a stain-space transformation is applied in the HED (hematoxylin-eosin-DAB)
color space, where small random biases and scaling factors perturb the concentrations of each of the three
biomarkers, emulating realistic fluctuations of H&E staining88. To avoid a too unrealistic color perturbation,
only one of the two color augmentations is applied uniformly at random with a probability of 80%. A random
conversion to near-grayscale is introduced with a probability of 20%, removing most chromatic information
in some views. As in the multiplex case, Gaussian blur is also applied to H&E crops with view-dependent
probabilities. Finally, all crops are normalized using modality-specific normalization.

Optimization.
To support the large batch sizes required by recent histopathology FMs86,8, VirTues-M2 is
trained on 16 compute nodes, each equipped with four GH200 GPUs of 96 GB each. On a single GPU, the
maximum feasible batch size is 26 samples, thus scaling to 64 GH200 GPUs enabled training with an effective
global batch size of 1,664. As proposed by the DINOv234 authors, Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP) is used
to split the model replicas required by the optimizer across GPUs to more evenly shard the memory footprint
used for the student, teacher, AdamW’s90 first and second moments. We initialize both the student and teacher
models with the weights of the histopathology foundation model, Hibou-L23. Furthermore, xformers91 is used to
more efficiently utilize the GPU hardware and speed up the attention layers. All other pretraining hyperparameters
used for the final model training are displayed in Tab. S31.

Baselines.

Virtual staining.
VirTues-M2 decoder.
We developed a virtual staining decoder that combines foundation model representations
with ESM-2 protein embeddings33 to condition the decoder explicitly on marker identity (Fig. S9). This design
enables prediction of arbitrary marker combinations rather than restricting the model to a fixed panel.
25


The architecture comprises two main components. A frozen VirTues-M2 encoder tokenizes the H&E or multiplex
input (224 × 224) into 16 × 16 patch tokens. These are passed to a decoder consisting of two parts: a semantic
decoder, inspired by the VirTues decoder, which translates the tokens into marker-specific semantic features,
and an upsampling head, which restores the spatial detail required for subcellular-resolution prediction. To retain
information at multiple levels of abstraction, linear skip projection layers extract hidden representations after
encoder blocks 6, 12, and 18 and route them, together with the final patch tokens, to both the semantic decoder
and the upsampling head.
In the decoder, the ESM-2 embedding of each target marker is added to the encoder tokens, after which two
full-attention blocks and a layer normalization produce decoded multiplex tokens of shape p × 16 × 16 × d, where
p is the number of requested protein marker. The upsampling head applies a 1×1 convolution (d = 1024 →128)
followed by four PixelShuffle convolution blocks97 that progressively increase spatial resolution from 16 × 16 to
256 × 256, and a resize operation to 224 × 224. Before the final 1 × 1 convolution (d = 128 →1), we add
the output of an input guide layer that operates directly on the H&E image, sharpening the spatial detail of
the predicted multiplex channels. As the training objective, we use the mean squared error (MSE) between the

We use HEX13, ROSIE72 and HistoPlexer73 as fully supervised baselines. For HEX, we use the official provided
implementation, which combines a MUSK backbone98 with a regression head that predicts a single mean
expression score per marker for each crop. ROSIE employs a ConvNext architecture that takes 128 × 128-pixel
H&E patches as input and predicts mean mx marker intensities for the central 8 × 8 pixels. Contiguous virtual
stainings are generated by sliding-window inference, iteratively stitching patch-level intensity predictions across
whole tissues or crop sections. Thus, to stain a 256 × 256 crop, ROSIE requires 1,024 forward passes, which
can be batched.
HistoPlexer employs a conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN) architecture with four complementary
loss components: an adversarial loss for realistic image generation, an L1 loss for pixel-level accuracy, a Gaussian
pyramid loss to handle alignment imperfections across serial sections, and a patch-wise contrastive loss for
embedding-space consistency between corresponding patches. The complexity of the combined loss functions led
to training instability across our heterogeneous cohorts, necessitating methodological additions to the original
implementation, including label smoothing, spectral normalization for the discriminator, and distinct gradient
scaling for the generator and discriminator.
All virtual staining hyperparameters and model selection are determined on a held-out 15% validation set.

Minimal diagnostic tests with VirTues-M2 and RL.
Multimodal Active VIew Selection (MAVIS)74 is a RL framework that dynamically selects diagnostic tests at
inference time by unifying modality selection (choosing between diagnostic platforms) and feature selection
(selecting specific measurements within a platform) into a single view selection task. The problem is formulated
as a Markov decision process where the state comprises FM embeddings, classifier logits, and a mask of acquired
views, while actions include available views, a [stop] token to terminate acquisition, and a [jump] token
to transition between modalities. The reward function combines instantaneous rewards reflecting diagnostic
improvement at each selection step with episodic rewards for correct final predictions, encouraging greedy
selection of maximally informative views while penalizing unnecessary acquisitions. The policy is trained using
Proximal Policy Optimization with invalid action masking to prevent redundant acquisitions.
The original MAVIS leverages two pretrained FMs as feature extractors without fine-tuning: UNI2-H for H&E
images and VirTues for multiplex. However, we adapted MAVIS to work only with one FM, i.e., VirTues-M2,
also improving computational efficiency. In our multimodal setting, MAVIS learns a hierarchical decision process
that always starts with the H&E tissue and all multiplex marker have the same acquisition cost.
Spatial biomarker discovery framework.
We filter the Cords et al. 22 cohort based on patients with survival data and at least 200 segmented cells within
each modality, resulting in a total of 1, 053 patients. For all ROIs, single-cell embeddings are generated from
both modalities using the frozen VirTues-M2 encoder based on the provided cell segmentation masks. Rather
than using only local cell information, the transformer architecture encodes both neighborhood context and local
patch information into the embedding space of the patch to which the segmented cell belongs, enabling the
discovery of cell states defined by their spatial microenvironment. We validate this property in Fig. S14.
To identify fine-grained cell states analogous to specific cell subtypes or neighborhoods, we perform unsupervised
Leiden clustering iteratively in high resolutions (r ∈{4.0, 4.2, 4.4}) on the discovery set (n = 737 with 505
events). These resolutions substantially exceed the default value of r = 1 to capture subtle cell subpopulations
that may be obscured at coarser clustering granularities. We retain only clusters containing at least five cells per
patient to ensure robust proportion estimates, and remove clusters exhibiting high multicollinearity to ensure
independent signatures. For each Leiden resolution, we compute for every patient a vector representing the
relative proportion of cells belonging to each cluster, yielding a matrix Xprop ∈R(n×c) where n represents the
number of patients and c the number of clusters identified at that resolution.
The predictive strength of each cluster proportion was screened on the discovery set by Cox regression on overall
survival, adjusted for demographic and diagnostic covariates to ensure independence from these confounders,
with hyperparameters selected by five-fold cross-validation split by patient. Across all resolutions, p-values were
corrected within each modality using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate, and we selected per modality

Pretraining Scaling Laws
Given the methodological pretraining advancements developed for multimodal training of VirTues-M2, we run
different ablation experiments to understand the trainings’ scaling behavior, robustness under different data
settings, and performance attributions during varying modality and dataset compositions. For all ablations, we
use the same effective batch size, 20,000 pretraining steps, dataset composition, training hyperparameters, and
model backbone, unless explicitly stated otherwise. For the unimodal H&E performance, we average over the
macro average F1 scores of the nine H&E benchmarks shown in Fig. S3, for multiplex performance, we report
the average macro F1 score for Pre-, On- and Post-Treatment on the response prediction on the TNBC patient
cohort Wang et al. 44, the ERBB2 and ER Status prediction on the METABRIC cohort Danenberg et al. 45 and
on the cancer grading and typing prediction task on the lung cancer cohort from22. In the multimodal task,
we use cancer grading and typing from Cords et al. 22 as evaluation tasks. Due to the variation in clinical data
collected with each cohort, we could not use the same downstream tasks across all modalities. Thus, we present
in Fig. S4 the relative performance gain within each modality, making gains comparable between modalities.
Dataset size. The growing availability of histopathological data was one of the main success factors for H&E
foundation models32,8. However, not only the amount of crops but also their diversity play an important role
when training foundation models32,100. Therefore, to understand the crop diversity in our pretraining corpus, in
a first experiment we randomly subsample the number of crops from the overall pretraining corpus for both the
multiplex as well as the H&E data corpus, using 1%, 5%, 10%, 25% and 50% of all available crops. For H&E,
we observe a clear scaling trend, with a relative Macro F1 score gain of 3.25% when scaling from 1% to 10% of
the data and further scaling to 5.25% when further increasing the data by a factor of ten (Fig. S4a.). Similarly,
for multiplex and multimodal performance, VirTues-M2 improves its performance by 2.48% and 1.25% for 10%
of the data and by 3.29% and 4.64% for 100% of the data, respectively.
H&E-to-MX ratio. In a second experiment we vary the ratio of H&E-to-multiplex pretraining data. This
experiment is meant to understand the effect of balanced modality sampling and to study catastrophic forgetting
of the histopathology weight initialization. While the H&E performance barely changes, multiplex performance
is best using a 10:90 H&E-to-multiplex ratio. Most notably, multimodal performance benefits most from a
balanced sampling underlining the decision of a balanced 50:50 modality sampling for VirTues-M2.
Registration setting. As reflected in our dataset collection (Tab. S29) the modality pairings vary across datasets,
ranging from strictly unimodal over paired but unaligned to pixel-wise alignment, emphasizing the importance of
the multimodal pretraining scheme proposed which accounts for them. A third ablation experiment is meant
to ablate the effect of the registration settings. As shown in Fig. S4, starting from only sampling from the
five registered datasets, we see an immediate performance increase, most expressed for the unimodal settings,
when using crops from all datasets but sampling them in an unpaired manner. Further multimodal performance
improvements are shown when adding registered data. To simulate consecutive cut data alignments, the two
modalities we randomly zoom into different parts of all sampled paired 256 × 256 crops with a probability of 20%
and only apply the contrastive DINO loss capturing global image features. In contrast to the strong multimodal
improvement, only a slight increase for the H&E and a slight decrease for the multiplex setting is observed.
Without sampling artificially unregistered samples and without using unaligned crops, VirTues-M2 shows the
best performance across all three modality settings.
Model size. Following conventional vision and histopathology foundation models23,8,34,100 we ablate the scaling
laws of the model size in a fourth experiment. Using a vision transformer base (ViT-B) comprising 96 million
parameters and initialized with Hibou-B weights Nechaev et al. 23 performance increase across all modalities
are shown when scaling to a model spanning more than triple the size (VirTues-M2 with a vision transformer
large). However, when scaling to a vision transformer huge, containing 715 million parameters and initialized
with UNI2-h Chen et al. 8), only the H&E performance increases whereas multiplex performance drops under the
one of the ViT-B. While we controlled for the effective batch size in all three experiments, we hypothesize that
either a larger data corpus is needed to benefit from scaling further than a ViT-L or more hyperparameter tuning
is needed.
Pretraining steps. In a fifth an last ablation experiment, we analyze the scaling laws over the time of pretraining.
For this, in Fig. S4 e,f we show the models’ performance every 2,000 pretraining steps alongside PCA visualizations
of the patch-level token representations. After a steep performance increase of the unimodal settings, the H&E
perform is plateauing whereas multiplex performance is increasing until 20,000 pretraining steps. The multimodal
performance is more steadily growing until 20,000 steps. Across all settings, the best performance is exhibited at
20,000 steps emphasizing the selection of this checkpoint. With regards to input tokens, each pretraining step

Figure S14: Cell tokens are shaped by their local tissue context. Leave-one-patch-out ablation for two representative cells per
modality, in a H&E and b multiplex imaging. Left: 224 µm crop with the 16 × 16 patch grid and segmentation masks, target patch
outlined in blue. Center: change in the cell token, 1 −cos(ablated, full), when each patch is replaced with background or with tissue
from an unrelated core. Gray patches lie outside the tissue mask. Right: influence versus Manhattan distance from the target patch,
points showing individual patches and lines the median.
Cell tokens encode local microenvironment.
VirTues-M2 is pretrained with a patch-level iBOT objective,
which predicts masked patches in latent space, and a crop-level DINO objective over all patch tokens. Because
the model is a vision transformer, every patch representation is computed with attention over all patches in
the crop and is therefore contextual by construction. The masked-prediction objective further rewards patch
representations that are predictive of their surroundings.
We exploit this to define cell tokens. Images are resampled such that a patch spans approximately the scale of a
single cell (14µm at 1.0µm/px), and for each segmented cell we extract a 224px crop positioned so that the cell
centroid falls at the center of patch (8, 8) of the 16 × 16 grid. The representation of that patch, computed
with attention over the full crop, is taken as the cell token. Since a patch is larger than a typical nucleus and is
contextualized by the surrounding tissue, the resulting token encodes both the properties of the cell itself and
those of its local microenvironment.
We verify this by leave-one-patch-out ablation in Fig. S14.
For a given cell token (blue patch), each of
the remaining 255 patches is replaced in turn, either with background (glass for H&E, off-tissue signal for
multiplex) or with tissue drawn from an unrelated core, and the resulting change in the token is measured as
1 −cos(ablated, full). In both modalities the influence is concentrated on the patches immediately adjacent to
the cell and decays to baseline within approximately five patches, corresponding to a radius of roughly 55µm.
22


The two substitution types produce near-identical decay profiles, indicating that the effect reflects the removal
of tissue context rather than the particular fill value.

Related literature
Foundation models for histopathology.
Self-supervised learning on large-scale histopathology datasets has
produced foundation models that capture generalizable morphological features from H&E-stained tissue. UNI8
26


introduced the first large-scale general-purpose foundation model for computational pathology, training a Vision
Transformer on 100 million patches from over 100,000 diagnostic WSIs using DINOv234, with UNI2 subsequently
expanding to 350,000 slides including immunohistochemistry. Virchow9 achieved clinical-grade performance by
training on 1.5 million H&E slides, demonstrating pan-cancer detection capabilities. Prov-GigaPath114 was the
first WSI foundation model, processing 1.3 billion tiles using a two-stage curriculum combining DINOv2 for
tile-level pretraining with a LongNet-based115 masked autoencoder for slide-level context. Additional models
including CTransPath116, Phikon117, and Hibou23 have contributed open-source alternatives with competitive
performance. These models have become standard feature extractors for computational pathology, yet they remain
fundamentally unimodal-learning from morphology alone without direct integration of molecular measurements.
Foundation models for spatial proteomics.
Foundation models for multiplex imaging must address heteroge-
neous marker panels across technologies and smaller training datasets compared to histopathology. VirTues10
introduced marker-aware tokenization using protein language model embeddings, enabling pretraining across
diverse multiplex panels without assuming fixed channel orderings. The model generates hierarchical representa-
tions spanning patch, cell, niche, and tissue scales. CA-MAE25 introduced channel-agnostic masked autoencoders
for microscopy, trained on 93 million images, demonstrating scaling laws for microscopy foundation models.
KRONOS12 adopted a DINOv2-based approach on 47 million patches from 175 protein markers.
Multimodal learning in computational pathology.
Recent work has begun exploring multimodal integration,
though predominantly through late fusion or vision-language alignment. MUSK98 introduced unified masked
modeling for multimodal pathology, training on 50 million images and 1 billion text tokens using a mixture-
of-modality-experts architecture that leverages unpaired data. CONCH118 trained on 1.17 million pathology
image-caption pairs using contrastive captioning, achieving strong zero-shot performance including rare disease
subtyping. TANGLE21 was the first method for slide representation learning using transcriptomics, aligning H&E
slides with bulk RNA-seq profiles. BiomedCLIP119 provides broader biomedical coverage trained on 15 million
PubMed image-text pairs. These approaches demonstrate the value of multimodal information but do not enable
the cross-modal structure learning that emerges from joint pretraining of histology and spatial proteomics within
a unified architecture. In addition, a separate line of work combines H&E with predicted molecular readouts
(e.g., virtual stains) using light cross-attention or late fusion, typically as task-tuned pipelines rather than as
general-purpose multimodal foundation models13,14.
Virtual staining and cross-modal prediction.
Virtual staining, i.e., predicting molecular marker expression
from H&E, offers a route to expand information content of routine slides. ROSIE72 achieved prediction of 50
protein biomarkers from H&E using a ConvNeXt architecture trained on over 16 million cells, demonstrating
cross-platform generalization. HEX13 targets virtual staining for a fixed 40-plex panel by extracting features
from H&E tiles with a pretrained pathology foundation model backbone (e.g., MUSK) and using a lightweight
MLP regression head to jointly predict the expression of all 40 markers. As a second step, Li et al. 13 introduce
a multimodal integration module with separate unimodal encoders for H&E and (virtual) spatial proteomics,
coupled via co-attention layers and fine-tuned for downstream tasks. Similarly, GigaTIME14 learns a cross-modal
translator from paired H&E and mIF to generate virtual mIF images for a fixed 21-marker panel, and uses
the resulting virtual protein maps to analyze associations with clinical biomarkers, cancer stage, and survival.
HistoPlexer73 predicts 11 IMC markers simultaneously using conditional GANs, improving survival prediction and
immune subtype classification. DeepLIIF120 provides simultaneous stain deconvolution, segmentation, and scoring.
VirtualMultiplexer121 introduced unpaired translation eliminating pixel-wise registration requirements. These
approaches require fixed marker sets and separate models, whereas foundation model embeddings offer more
flexible prediction as a downstream capability. Because these methods impute a fixed, predefined marker panel
and are typically optimized for specific downstream endpoints, they are best viewed as cross-modal prediction or
task-specialized pipelines rather than multimodal foundation models that learn a shared representation through
joint pretraining on both modalities.
Cell segmentation and phenotyping.
Instance segmentation and phenotyping are typically addressed through
staged pipelines. Cellpose122 introduced gradient flow representations and became the standard for general
cell segmentation with human-in-the-loop fine-tuning. HoVer-Net123 introduced horizontal-vertical distance
maps enabling simultaneous segmentation and classification. StarDist55 models cells as star-convex polygons for
computational efficiency. Mesmer124 achieved human-level whole-cell segmentation using TissueNet with over 1
27


million labeled cells. CellViT52 applied Vision Transformers to nuclear segmentation, demonstrating foundation
model transferability. These staged approaches can propagate errors and do not exploit complementarity between
morphological and molecular signals for joint inference.
Spatial proteomics technologies.
Multiplexed spatial proteomics technologies enable in situ quantification
of dozens of proteins while preserving tissue architecture. Iterative multiplexed immunofluorescence (mIF)
methods achieve high-plex imaging through repeated staining and imaging cycles: CODEX (now PhenoCycler)15
uses DNA-barcoded antibodies with fluorescent oligonucleotide hybridization; Orion16 employs staining with
fluorophore inactivation; and cyclic immunofluorescence provides an accessible public-domain alternative using
standard fluorescence microscopy; all achieving 50–60+ markers at subcellular resolution on FFPE tissues. Mass
spectrometry-based approaches offer complementary capabilities: imaging mass cytometry (IMC)17 couples
immunohistochemistry with laser ablation and CyTOF detection of 40+ metal-conjugated antibodies at ∼1µm
resolution, while MIBI125 and MIBI-TOF18 use secondary ion mass spectrometry for up to 100 markers with
exceptional sensitivity and dynamic range.
Protein language models.
Protein language models provide semantic embeddings enabling marker-aware
tokenization across heterogeneous antibody panels. ESM-233 demonstrated emergence of structural information
at scale, with the 650M parameter model providing embeddings that capture biochemical properties and enable
downstream applications. ProtTrans126 systematically compared transformer architectures for proteins. These
embeddings enable VirTues-M2 to generalize across different marker configurations by encoding marker identity
through biological meaning rather than channel position.
