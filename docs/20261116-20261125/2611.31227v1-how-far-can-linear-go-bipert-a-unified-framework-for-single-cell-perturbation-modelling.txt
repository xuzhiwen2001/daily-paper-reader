How Far Can Linear Go? BiPert, a Unified Framework for
Single-Cell Perturbation Modelling
Abhishek Sharma1 and Aleksej Zelezniak1,2,3
1Randall Centre for Cell & Molecular Biophysics, King’s College London, London, UK
2Department of Life Sciences, Chalmers University of Technology, Gothenburg, Sweden
3Institute of Biotechnology, Life Sciences Centre, Vilnius University, Vilnius, Lithuania

Abstract
Understanding how genetic perturbations affect cellular behaviour is essential for studying gene func-
tion and regulatory mechanisms. Large-scale CRISPR screens measure these effects across thousands of
genes, but cover relatively few cellular contexts. Predicting perturbation responses in unseen cell types
and identifying perturbed genes from their transcriptional effects therefore remain challenging. Here,
we show that both tasks can be addressed through a common linear decomposition of transcriptional
responses into shared and perturbation-specific components. Building on this principle, we introduce
BiPert, a simple linear framework for both forward perturbation prediction and inverse identification.
For forward prediction, BiPert outperforms existing methods that use no external biological knowledge
when predicting gene expression changes in unseen cell lines. Its few-shot extension, BiPert-FS, adapts
to new cell lines using as few as ten measured perturbations. For inverse identification, BiPert-ID identi-
fies perturbed genes from single-cell expression profiles, outperforming deep-learning methods in unseen
cell lines and donors. We further show that perturbations whose effects transfer more consistently across
cellular contexts are also easier to identify, even after controlling for perturbation effect size. Together,
these results establish a connection between perturbation transfer and identification, and demonstrate
that simple linear models provide strong, interpretable baselines for both tasks.
Keywords: Perturb-seq; genetic perturbation prediction; perturbation identification; linear models; low-rank
decomposition; transfer across cell lines; interpretable models

Introduction
Large-scale perturbation screens now measure how thousands of genetic perturbations change the state of
a cell1–3. Modelling these changes has two complementary directions. In the forward direction, given the
effects of a perturbation across previously studied cell lines, we seek to predict its effect in a new cellular
context4,5. In the inverse direction, given an observed cellular state, we seek to identify the perturbation that
most likely produced it6,7. The first problem enables the transfer of experimental knowledge to unprofiled
cell lines, potentially reducing the need for additional perturbation screens. The second enables the interpre-
tation of previously unexplained cellular states by matching them to known perturbation responses. A key
observation is that although these tasks have different prediction targets, both require understanding which
aspects of a perturbation response are shared across cellular contexts and which are context-dependent.
Many perturbations induce a common cellular response, while retaining perturbation-specific compo-
nents5,8. Existing approaches exploit aspects of this structure. For example, COMPASS5 decomposes per-
turbation effects into a cell-line-specific direction and a perturbation-specific residual, enabling transfer to

decomposition assumes that cellular context modifies these effects through a single shared direction, with
the remaining perturbation-specific component invariant across lines. This is a restrictive assumption: dif-
ferent cellular contexts may modulate distinct components of a perturbation response to different degrees.
We therefore generalise this decomposition to a low-rank operator, allowing each cell line to apply its own
linear transformation to a shared perturbation representation.
Models designed for the forward task do not solve the inverse problem7. For inverse identification6,7,
we exploit the same decomposition to construct perturbation-specific prototypes from training cell lines. By
removing shared cellular responses and matching individual cells to these prototypes, we obtain a simple,
training-free linear method for identifying perturbations in unseen cell lines.
Importantly, we find that cross-cell-line transferability is not only relevant to predicting perturbation
effects, but also provides a link between the forward and inverse tasks. A transfer score estimated entirely
from training cell lines strongly predicts whether a perturbation can be identified in a previously unseen line.
Conversely, in an independent screen, we show that identifiability within a cell type limits transfer across
cell types. Together, these results suggest that a common linear structure can support both the transfer of
perturbation effects and their identification from cellular states.
Our contributions are threefold:
1. A two-way link between perturbation transfer and identification. Transferability measured on
training cell lines alone predicts identification in held-out lines, for both BiPert-ID and the deep net-
work; conversely, in an independent screen, identifiability within a cell type limits transfer across cell
types.
2. Low-rank models for perturbation transfer. We formulate cross-cell-line transfer as structured ma-
trix completion and introduce a low-rank operator, BiPert, generalising COMPASS, together with a
few-shot extension, BiPert-FS, for adapting to new cell lines with limited measurements.
3. Training-free perturbation identification. We extend the shared decomposition to inverse identifi-
cation and propose a training-free linear prototype model, BiPert-ID, that outperforms the state of the
art in unseen cell lines.

Related work
Forward: deep models for perturbation effect prediction.
Most methods for predicting the effect of a
genetic perturbation are deep networks. CPA encodes cells into a latent space and adds a learned embedding
per perturbation9; GEARS propagates perturbations over a graph of known gene relations to reach unseen
genes and combinations10; scGPT, a transformer pre-trained on millions of cells, is fine-tuned to the same
task11; GenePert regresses effects on gene embeddings derived from language models12; TabICL, a tabular
foundation model, predicts effects in context from external gene knowledge, six sets of prior gene features
covering protein sequence, text, morphology, protein interactions and gene dependency13,14; TxPert com-
bines several biological knowledge graphs to predict effects outside the training distribution15; and State,
a transformer trained across many cell contexts, targets transfer between them4. Of these, only State and
TxPert evaluate transfer to cell lines held out from training.
Linear baselines.
A growing body of work has questioned the effectiveness of single-cell foundation
models for predicting perturbation responses. Ahlmann-Eltze, Huber and Anders showed that a linear model
fitted to the training perturbations can match or outperform deep and foundation models on held-out pertur-
bation effects16, a finding echoed by broader benchmarks of single-cell foundation models17,18. COMPASS
extends this linear perspective to cross-cell-line transfer by decomposing the effect of perturbation p in cell
line ℓinto a cell-line-specific direction and a perturbation representation shared across cell lines (Sec. 4).
This shared representation enables prediction in unseen cell lines, outperforming GEARS, scGPT and State
in this setting5. A concurrent evaluation study separates each response into a part shared across contexts
and a context-specific part, and finds that current predictors recover mainly the former19; it does not pro-
pose a model or study the inverse task. PerturbMap20 fits linear maps between treatment conditions of a
single screen to predict held-out perturbations, without holding out cell lines. We build on the COMPASS
decomposition in two directions: first, by generalising it to a low-rank operator formulation21 that recovers
COMPASS as a special case and adapts to a new cell line from as few as 10 measurements; and second, by
extending the framework to the inverse problem of identifying the perturbation from the resulting cellular
state. Our few-shot model builds on subspace alignment22 and on regularising towards a source model23,24.
It is similar in spirit to geometric matrix completion, which uses side information25,26.
Factor models.
Factor models decompose multiple datasets into shared and dataset-specific latent factors.
JIVE21 separates joint and individual variation, while MOFA and MOFA+27,28 extend factor analysis to
multiple omics layers and sample groups. Guided sparse factor analysis similarly models perturbation ef-
fects through latent factors within a single CRISPR screen29. These methods primarily explain variation in
observed data, typically requiring additional factors or loadings to be estimated for a new dataset. BiPert-
FS addresses a different task: predicting unmeasured perturbation responses in a new cell line from only
a few observations. Rather than decomposing the new line, it keeps the source response programs and av-
erage effects fixed, and learns only a regularised affine correction (Eq. (7)). The correction is evaluated on
knockdowns not used for fitting.
Inverse: inferring the perturbation from cells. In contrast to predicting cellular responses given a
perturbation, the inverse problem of identifying the perturbation responsible for an observed transcriptional
state has received comparatively little attention. RNA fingerprinting6 addresses this task by matching indi-
vidual cells to transcriptional response signatures associated with known perturbations. Footprint methods
such as PROGENy derive consensus response signatures from many perturbation experiments and score
new expression profiles against them to infer pathway activity30; BiPert-ID applies the same principle to in-
dividual gene knockdowns in single cells of an unseen cell line. ExPert7, currently the state of the art, takes a
learned approach: it trains a neural encoder across multiple perturbation screens and classifies cells by com-
3


paring their representations with learned per-gene prototypes. In ExPert’s held-out-cell-line benchmark7,
models designed for forward prediction (GEARS, scGPT and GenePert) perform close to chance, showing
that predicting perturbation effects does not necessarily translate into identifying their causes. However,
none exploits the line-invariant perturbation representation introduced above.

Background
Why perturbation effects decompose.
Three properties of cellular organisation motivate the models in-
troduced in Section 4.
Genes act in functional modules. Proteins operate in complexes and pathways, meaning that perturbing
different members of the same module can disrupt shared cellular functions. Consistent with this, genome-
scale perturbation screens show that knockdowns of genes belonging to the same protein complex produce
strongly correlated transcriptional effects1. This suggests that perturbation effects occupy a low-dimensional
space of shared cellular programs.
Distinct perturbations converge on shared response directions. Different knockdowns can engage over-
lapping cellular programs and consequently produce similar transcriptional responses. Perturbations of core
growth machinery, for example, induce broad and stereotyped responses, whereas perturbations of more
specific regulators produce additional gene-dependent effects5. Crucially, the strength with which a pertur-
bation engages this shared response direction is relatively well conserved across cell lines, more so than
the gene-specific remainder5. This motivates separating a conserved perturbation component from cell-line-
dependent effects.
Cellular context determines how shared programs are expressed. Different cell lines express different
subsets of genes and have distinct active regulatory networks. Consequently, the same underlying cellu-
lar program can affect different downstream genes, and with different strengths, depending on the cellular
context. Perturbation responses are therefore more similar between related cell lines than between unre-
lated ones2. This motivates modelling cell-line-specific loadings that determine how shared programs are
translated into gene-expression changes.
Together, these observations motivate the decomposition used in our models: a shared response whose
strength depends on the perturbation and the cell line, a conserved perturbation-specific component, and a
cell-line-specific weighting of shared programs.
4
BiPert: Linear Decomposition Model
We consider two complementary prediction tasks corresponding to the forward and inverse directions of a
common decomposition. For each task, we provide a background formulation and introduce a linear alterna-
tive. Experiments, results and ablations follow in Section 5. Both tasks follow a cross-cell-line generalisation
setting: models are trained on a set of cell lines and evaluated on a previously unseen line. The forward task
predicts the effect of a perturbation not measured in the target line, whereas the inverse task identifies the
perturbation from the resulting cellular expression profile (Fig. 1). For each task, we first state the problem
and the existing approach, then introduce our linear model.
4.1
Perturbation Transfer as Structured Matrix Completion
Motivation.
Perturbation screens are available for only a limited number of cell lines, while performing
genome-scale screens across every cell type of interest is impractical. This motivates transferring knowledge
across cellular contexts: given the effects of a perturbation measured in existing cell lines, predict its effect
in a new line5.

4.2
BiPert-ID: Perturbation Identification from Single Cells
Motivation.
The inverse task is to identify the perturbation responsible for an observed cellular state7.
In applications such as disease analysis, the cell is observed in an altered state, but the perturbation that
produced it is unknown. Differential expression captures the downstream response but does not directly
identify its cause. A perturbation atlas provides a reference of cellular responses with known perturbations,
allowing an unknown state to be matched to its likely cause. Existing approaches construct this reference
6


within individual screens and report limited transferability of perturbation effects across cellular contexts6,7.
Our decomposition addresses this limitation by explicitly modelling cross-line transfer.
Task.
Given the expression vector x ∈RG of a perturbed cell, the goal is to identify the knocked-down
gene. We use the ExPert benchmark7, which holds out entire cell lines. Models are trained on perturbation
screens from the remaining lines and evaluated on a held-out line, which makes this a zero-shot cross-domain
identification task.
Our training-free linear prototype model (Fig. 2).
Given a cell from an unseen cell line, the task is to
identify the gene that was knocked down. Our method requires no training: it constructs perturbation-specific
reference profiles from the training lines and matches the query cell against them.
1. Build a prototype for each perturbation. For each knockdown p, measure its effect yp,ℓin each train-
ing cell line. Subtract the response shared by all knockdowns in that line—the general response to
perturbation—and average the residuals across lines. The result, gp ∈RG, is a prototype of the gene-
specific response that transfers across cell lines. It is the same residual used in the forward-transfer
model.
2. Represent the query cell. Compare the query cell x with unperturbed control cells from its own line to
obtain its expression change d ∈RG. As in ExPert, subtract the response shared across all perturbed
query cells, leaving the change specific to the query.
3. Score each candidate gene using two signals. First, does the query’s expression change resemble the
candidate’s prototype? Second, is the candidate gene itself downregulated? We combine these signals
with equal weight: prototype similarity captures the overall response pattern, while the gene’s own
expression provides direct evidence of its knockdown. Candidates are ranked by

Experiments and Results
Every comparison is run on the published method’s own benchmark (COMPASS5 or ExPert7), unless
marked otherwise. We first show perturbation transfer results, and then perturbation identification.
Perturbation transfer. We follow COMPASS’s benchmark5, which comprises CRISPR interference
(CRISPRi)31 screens in four cell lines1,2 and in the Orion pair of cell lines3 (Appendix A). We use the
2,000 most variable genes and log(1 + x) of counts normalised to 104 per cell. Each cell line is held out
in turn; 20% of its knockdowns form the test set, over five random splits. De-biased Pearson correlation
compares predicted and true effects using disjoint halves of control cells, avoiding inflation from shared
controls.
Implementation.
BiPert uses rank 50 (rank ablation in Appendix C). BiPert-FS uses k = 30, with ridge
regularisation selected using source lines only, treating each in turn as a pseudo-target. BiPert-ID has no free
parameters. All linear models fit in seconds to minutes on a CPU.

On COMPASS’s benchmark (Fig. 3), BiPert predicts held-out perturbation effects more accu-

A Two-Way Link Between Transfer and Identification
Transfer predicts identification (Table 6).
Our decomposition suggests that both tasks depend on the
same quantity: how well a perturbation’s specific effect gp transfers across cell lines. We test this using
training cell lines alone. For each perturbation measured in at least two training cell lines, we calculate its
transfer score as the mean cosine similarity between its specific effect in each line and the average across its
other lines, leaving each line out in turn. We then assess whether the perturbation is identified in a held-out
line, defined as ranking among the top 10 of 1,157 candidates using pooled query cells.
Across 3,340 perturbation–line pairs, perturbations that transfer better are identified more often: for both
methods, the share identified rises steadily from the lowest to the highest quarter of transfer scores, with
quarters cut within each held-out line. Stronger effects also transfer more readily (rank correlation 0.54), but
the link is not explained by effect size alone: at a fixed effect size, perturbations that transfer better are still
identified more often by both methods (Appendix E). The link holds for ExPert as well, although it does not
model transfer, suggesting that transferability is a useful inductive bias for identification models in general.
This association is predictive, not causal.
Identifiability within a cell type limits transfer (Table 7).
We test cross-cell-type transfer in an inde-
pendent screen of CRISPR activation targeting 1,833 transcription factors (TFs) in fibroblasts and RPE-1
cells32. For each TF, we split its guide RNAs into two disjoint sets and test whether the effect estimated
from one set identifies the TF (top-1 among 1,833) from the other set. We perform this test within each cell
type and across cell types, considering transfer successful if either direction (fibroblast to RPE-1 or vice
versa) succeeds. Cross-cell-type identification closely tracks within-cell-type reproducibility: 25 of 33 TFs
identified independently in both cell types also transfer across them, compared with only 8 of 1,720 TFs
identified in neither. This association is not simply driven by effect strength. Even among the 10% of TFs
with the strongest effects, cross-cell-type identification is much more frequent for TFs identified within both
cell types (25 of 33) than for those identified in neither (5 of 91). The eight TFs reproducible within both cell
types but not across them are candidates for cell-type-specific effects. The comparison must use different
guides on each side; with the same guides, it would overstate cross-cell-type identification about fourfold
(Appendix F).

Limitations and Conclusion
Limitations. BiPert-ID is outperformed by the deep network ExPert when the training data contain cells of
the same type as the test, as on views where the same donor was seen in training under another condition
(Table 3). Methods that draw on external gene knowledge can also edge out BiPert, which uses the gene
expression only: TabICL14, given six sets of prior gene features, reaches a de-biased Pearson correlation of
0.48 against 0.47 for BiPert, although BiPert leads on the Orion pair (0.25 vs. 0.24)5.
BiPert, a closed-form linear decomposition, matches or outperforms state-of-the-art methods on two
single-cell perturbation tasks when evaluated on held-out cell lines and donors. The learned parameters
have direct biological interpretations: perturbation codes Up reveal shared response patterns, while cell-line
loadings Vℓcapture how each context reweights them. Inverse-identification scores further decompose into
prototype similarity and the knocked-down gene’s own expression drop. Deep models perform better when
the test context resembles their training data, as ExPert does on same-donor views. Benchmarks should
therefore report performance on genuinely new contexts separately from performance on closely related
ones. For generalisation to new contexts, a simple linear model is the baseline to beat.
Finally, the relationship between the two tasks persists after controlling for perturbation effect size. This
suggests that the ability to transfer perturbation-specific responses across cellular contexts is closely linked
to their identifiability from observed cells. Because the transfer score is computed using training cell lines
alone, it can also provide an advance estimate of which perturbations are likely to be reliably identified in a
new cell line, independently of the identification method used.

raises pseudobulk top-10 recall by 0.014 on the four held-out cell lines and by 0.025 over all 34 views. The

Query.
A query cell’s change d is computed relative to the mean of the held-out line’s control cells and
divided by the per-gene spread of those control cells. Following ExPert, the shared response is removed by
projecting out the direction of the mean change across all query cells of that line.
Scores.
The candidate’s own gene is removed from both vectors before the cosine. Both terms of Eq. (8) in
the main text are standardised across the 1,157 candidates and added with equal weight; there are no tuned
parameters. For a group of cells from the same knockdown, per-cell log-softmax scores are averaged before
ranking (ExPert’s pseudobulk evaluation).

Ablations
BiPert: rank.
Accuracy increases from 0.43 at rank 10 to a plateau of 0.47 at ranks 50–100, before declin-
ing slightly to 0.44 at rank 300. The perturbation discrimination score (PDS) increases steadily with rank,
from 0.15 at rank 10 to 0.25 at rank 300, matching COMPASS-X only at ranks 200–300. Thus, rank controls
a trade-off between prediction accuracy and perturbation discrimination: at rank 50, the PDS of BiPert is
lower than that of COMPASS-X (0.21 vs. 0.25). We also tested whether BiPert benefits from first separating
components shared across all cell lines from cell-line-specific ones, as JIVE does21. It does not: replacing
the plain SVD with JIVE changes accuracy by at most 0.004 and PDS by at most 0.010 across ranks. BiPert
therefore needs no such split; the improvement comes from the low-rank structure itself.
BiPert-ID: score.
We ablate the scoring rule (Eq. (8)) to assess the individual importance of the cosine
term and the own-gene drop term. Both are needed: either one alone identifies far fewer perturbations than
the two combined (Fig. S2). Reading the own-gene drop from every gene measured in the query line, rather
than only from genes shared by all training cell lines, adds a further gain.

Why Forward Models Identify Poorly
Having described how BiPert-ID identifies knockdowns, we now ask why models built for the forward task
perform so poorly on the same task (Table 2). A forward model can be used for identification by predicting
one expression profile per candidate knockdown and matching each test cell to these profiles; the forward-
model scores in that table were released by ExPert’s authors7. We reran GenePert12 on the four held-out
cell lines to see why it scores low, and added State4, a forward model built to predict effects in an unseen
cell line. For each model we ask two questions: does the predicted profile of a knockdown agree with its
measured effect once the mean effect of all knockdowns is removed, and do measured effects, put through
the same scoring, identify more knockdowns than the predictions? Figure S3 gives the numbers.
GenePert
fits a ridge regression from a gene’s text embedding to the effect of knocking it down. For
knockdowns included in the fit, its profile is a smoothed copy of the measured effect (agreement 0.82), and

measured effects themselves. For knockdowns left out of the fit (five-fold), the agreement falls to 0.10 and
identification to 8%. ExPert’s authors report 6% for GenePert; their adapter, as we read it, uses the fitted
profiles, so we could not reproduce that value with our re-implementation (one model per training context).

Transfer Predicts Identification After Controlling for Effect Size
Motivation.
Section 6 and Table 6 show that perturbations with higher transfer scores are more likely to
be identified in a held-out cell line. However, perturbations with larger effects may be easier to identify and
may also transfer more consistently. Indeed, transfer score and effect size have a rank correlation of 0.54.
We therefore test whether transfer predicts identification independently of effect size.
Setup.
We analyse 3,340 perturbation–line pairs. For each pair, the outcome is y = 1 if the perturbation
is identified among the top 10 of 1,157 candidates in the held-out line (pooled across cells), and y = 0
otherwise. We use two predictors, both computed exclusively from training cell lines: the transfer score t
(defined in the main text) and effect size e, measured as the logarithm of the norm of the perturbation’s mean
effect. Both predictors are standardised to zero mean and unit variance.

Full ExPert benchmark: uncertainty of the results.
Across all 34 held-out views of ExPert’s full bench-
mark (Table 3 and Section 5), ExPert achieves a mean pseudobulk top-10 recall of 0.482, compared with
0.449 for BiPert-ID, a difference of −0.033 (BiPert-ID minus ExPert).
To quantify uncertainty, we use a paired bootstrap over knockdowns. Within each view, we resample
knockdowns with replacement and compute both methods’ top-10 recall on the same resampled set. We
then average the differences across the 34 views, giving each view equal weight, as in ExPert’s headline
metric.
Over 2,000 bootstrap resamples, the 95% confidence interval for the mean difference is [−0.040,−0.026].
The interval excludes zero, indicating a small but real overall advantage for ExPert on the full benchmark.
However, this advantage comes entirely from views in which the same donor appears in training under
another condition. BiPert-ID performs better on every new-donor view and on 9 of the 10 new-cell-line
views, as discussed in the main text. The corresponding intervals lie above zero for new cell lines and new
donors, and below zero for same-donor views.

Identifiability and transfer: why different guides are needed.
In the CRISPR activation screen of
Southard et al.32, each transcription factor (TF) is targeted by multiple guide RNAs. Individual guides
can also produce effects unrelated to their intended TF. Consequently, comparing the same guide across cell
types may overestimate how well a TF’s response transfers.
Across 1,833 TFs, the mean correlation between responses to the same guide in fibroblasts and RPE-1
cells is 0.060–0.065. This exceeds the correlation between different guides targeting the same TF within one
cell type (0.035–0.039).
The difference also affects identification. Using the same guide across cell types identifies 153–160 TFs
correctly (top-1 among 1,833 candidates), compared with only 36–46 when different guides are used. Thus,
reusing guides inflates apparent cross-cell-type identification by approximately fourfold.
To avoid this confound, the analysis in Section 6 and Table 7 divides each TF’s guides into two disjoint
sets and always compares responses obtained using different sets across cell types.
