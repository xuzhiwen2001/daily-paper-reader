## Abstract 

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) prediction from histology aims to in-fer spatially resolved gene expression profiles directly from tissue images. This is a challenging problem that is currently often ad-dressed with complicated model architectures, additional inputs, and complicated training procedures, while remaining limited to fixed, small gene panels. This raises a natural question: Is there an efficient method for large-panel ST prediction? Here, we present GeneFlow, a simpler yet effective framework for large-scale spatial gene expression prediction that unifies flow-matching generative modeling with structured multimodal fusion. Specifically, Gene-Flow first encodes both histological morphology and cross-gene interactions in spatial spots through histology and gene tokenizers. The model directly predicts spatial gene expression, rather than an intermediate velocity field, while being trained with a velocity-based flow-matching objective. We show that, even without relying on heavy generative pipelines, GeneFlow achieves state-of-the-art performance across multiple ST benchmarks, with up to 56.8% gains in 1,000-gene prediction. Extensive experiments further show that GeneFlow produces biologically meaningful spot profiles while re-ducing computational cost by orders of magnitude, thereby improv-ing efficiency and scalability, particularly in clinical settings. ## 1 Introduction 

Histopathology with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining has been the gold standard for clinical diagnosis of cancer and other diseases for more than a century. However, microscopic examina-tion of H&E images is often time-consuming and cannot reliably distinguish many molecularly distinct cell types by visual inspec-tion alone. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies have enabled the joint measurement of tissue morphology and gene expression over the past decade [7, 30]. For instance, spot-based platforms such as 10x Visium [15] can quantify over 20,000 genes across entire sections without tissue dissociation. By providing spatially resolved gene expression profiles, ST platforms enable new insights into the prediction of key clinical outcomes of complex cellular behavior and disease states [3, 17]. However, despite recent advances in ST technologies, signifi-cant challenges remain for clinical translation, including high cost and experimental complexity. In contrast, histopathology images arXiv:2611.17899v1 [cs.CV] 13 Nov 2026 Conference acronym ’XX, June 03–05, 2018, Woodstock, NY Zhang et al. 

are routinely acquired and widely available in clinical practice. H&E-stained tissue sections provide rich structural information that reflects underlying biological processes at both cellular and tissue levels. Motivated by this complementarity between ST and histopathology, an appealing direction is to infer spatial spot expres-sion from histology images, enabling the reconstruction of omics profiles from easily accessible pathology data. As shown in Fig. 2, each histology image is partitioned into small spots and treated as a unit for spatial gene expression prediction. The goal is to infer the corresponding gene expression of each spot conditioned on its local image content and spatial coordinates. Several prior works have explored the spatial gene expression prediction problem using different modeling approaches. Some of them have used pretrained image encoders to directly regress gene expression from local histology patches [12, 25, 35, 37]; some exploited multi-scale feature fusion to capture cross-modal align-ment and spatial context [4, 6, 8, 32, 34]; and some went further to leverage generative modeling to better model gene expression distributions and improve prediction [10, 13, 22, 28, 41]. However, these advancements have several inherent limitations that restrict their clinical applicability: 

• Complex pipelines. These existing approaches rely on complex architectures and multi-stage pipelines, which increase computa-tional cost and introduce additional design and optimization chal-lenges, making them difficult to deploy in resource-constrained clinical environments. 

• Limited gene-panel scalability. All the previous methods de-pend on predefined gene panels often limited to a subset of 200 genes, whereas practical clinical applications typically require a coverage of 1000 or more genes; this gap discounts their analysis reliability for downstream tasks. 

• Insufficient interaction modeling. Existing methods often fail to capture inter-gene interactions, instead treating genes in a largely independent manner or weakly coupled manner. This lack of structured modeling leads to off-manifold generation, where synthesized gene expression profiles may drift away from the true biological data distribution, especially for near-zero expression values or long-tailed gene distributions. Therefore, in the sequel, we try to ask and better address the fol-lowing question: 

Can an efficient generative model support large-panel spatial gene expression prediction solely from histology images? 

In response, GeneFlow is designed around three key ideas that make flow-based spatial gene expression prediction simple, sta-ble, and scalable. (1) Histology-conditioned gene-expression flow. GeneFlow uses pretrained pathology representations to con-dition a lightweight fusion network that directly predicts target gene expression profiles, bypassing iterative denoising and complex spatial interaction modeling while maintaining efficient histology-to-expression translation [39]. (2) ZINB-guided gene token space. 

GeneFlow further maps high-dimensional gene vectors into struc-tured latent gene tokens and initializes the generative process from a ZINB prior, allowing the model to capture cross-gene dependen-cies under sparsity and overdispersion while remaining scalable to larger gene panels. Together, these designs enable GeneFlow to model spatial gene expression distributions with fewer architectural

assumptions and lower computational cost. (3) Manifold-stable velocity loss. To better match the sparse and noisy geometry of ST measurements, GeneFlow predicts the terminal expression end-point and constructs the velocity from this endpoint, avoiding the unstable normalization that can arise when velocity is estimated from intermediate noisy states [21, 24]. We evaluate GeneFlow on four publicly available datasets span-ning different tissue types and sequencing platforms, including kidney, HER2ST, PRAD, and mouse brain. GeneFlow achieves state-of-the-art performance on gene expression prediction, consistently outperforming existing methods across PCC metrics, with a 17.1% improvement on PCC-1000 on the PRAD dataset, highlighting its effectiveness in modeling large-scale gene sets. GeneFlow also demonstrates improved accuracy in biologically relevant biomarker prediction, suggesting better preservation of meaningful cellular variation. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 1, our GeneFlow also offers orders-of-magnitude reductions in runtime and FLOPs compared to Diffusion-based approaches. We further analyze the evaluation protocol and show that distance-based metrics such as MSE and MAE are not sufficient to assess prediction quality, as they fail to capture biologically meaningful variation. We also conduct de-tailed ablation studies on key design components to validate the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed framework. To sum up, we have the following summarized contributions: 

• Effective flow-based prediction. We propose GeneFlow, a novel flow-based framework for predicting spatial gene expres-sion from H&E histology images. It directly models gene expres-sion from image patches without extra multi-stage alignment. 

• Gene-aware multimodal fusion. GeneFlow introduces a light-weight multi-modal fusion architecture to combine image and gene features, enabling efficient prediction over a broader gene set. This design improves flexibility for downstream tasks and supports potential clinical use. 

• Accurate and efficient inference. GeneFlow achieves high-fidelity predictions that are spatially consistent and biologically meaningful over multiple datasets, while also enabling faster inference speed. ## 2 Related works 

Spatial gene expression prediction from histology images. 

Early deep learning methods for spatial gene expression predic-tion aim to infer gene expression profiles from histological con-text [12, 25, 35, 37]. StNet fine-tuned a DenseNet-121 pretrained on ImageNet to directly regress Visium spot gene expression from H&E image patches [12]. Xie et al. [34] applies a contrastive learn-ing paradigm: BLEEP maps histology patches and gene-expression profiles into a joint embedding space and enforces biological align-ment. Other approaches emphasize multi-scale and multi-branch architectures to capture tissue context [10, 11, 22, 27, 28]. TRIPLEX uses three parallel encoder branches and fuses features across scales to capture morphology at different resolutions [8]. Recent work also explores generative modeling of spatial transcriptomics. Stem formulates spot-level gene expression prediction as a conditional diffusion process [41]. Huang et al. [13] proposes STFlow, a flow-matching generative model that employs a slide-level encoder with local spatial attention to capture cell–cell interactions efficiently. In contrast, we argue that explicitly modeling spot–spot spatial re-lations is not always necessary for accurate prediction. Instead, we focus on a simpler and more scalable design that avoids hard-coded priors, enabling efficient extension to larger gene sets and faster inference without sacrificing performance. 

Flow matching for generative modeling. Flow matching (FM) has recently emerged as an effective paradigm for generative mod-eling, demonstrating strong performance across diverse domains, including vision, biomolecular modeling, and robotics [18, 19, 29, 33, 39]. Rather than simulating a reverse stochastic process during training, FM learns a time-dependent vector field that transports samples from a simple prior distribution to the target data distribu-tion along a prescribed probability path. This formulation yields a conceptually simple objective and supports efficient, often deter-ministic, sampling, making it attractive for large-scale generation tasks. Recent studies have further revisited the parameterization of continuous generative models, suggesting that directly predict-ing clean signals can simplify the learning objective and improve empirical performance, compared with modeling noise or velocity fields [21, 24, 29, 36]. Motivated by these advances, we formulate gene expression prediction as a generative process that maps a flexible prior to target expression profiles, which is well suited to large gene panel prediction settings.

Overall fusion architecture . Our GeneFlow network is built on a DiT-based transformer [26] that parameterizes a flow matching model. Gene expression inputs are encoded using a gene tokenizer, resulting in a sequence of hidden vectors with 𝐷 dimensions. Time information is encoded using a standard 256-dimensional sinusoidal embedding followed by a 2-layer MLP. For histology, each patch is represented by a single embedding extracted from pretrained pathology foundation models, either via special tokens or pooling; when multiple models are used, their embeddings are concatenated and projected through a 2-layer MLP to obtain a unified condi-tioning vector. This conditioning vector, together with the time embedding, is injected into the transformer blocks via adaLN-Zero modulation. The model consists of 12 DiT blocks with 6-head at-tention, followed by a final adaLN and linear layer to predict the flow field. An overview of the architecture is presented in Fig. 3. 

Some useful tricks. 1. Parameter-free RMSNorm. We apply RMS normalization [38] without learnable parameters throughout the DiT network. Given the high sparsity and large dynamic range of gene expression data, this design provides stable feature scaling across spots and genes without introducing additional parameters. It is inserted after the input embedding, before each attention and MLP block, and before the final prediction head, ensuring stable fea-ture scaling with zero parameter overhead. 2. ReLU 2 activation. We replace GELU with a squared ReLU activation [40] in MLP blocks, i.e., F.relu(x).square() . This activation better matches the non-negative and sparse nature of gene expression signals, promoting sparse responses without sacrificing representational capacity. 3. QK normalization. Although RoPE does not provide noticeable gains in our setting, we retain query–key normalization and ap-ply it directly as 𝑞, 𝑘 = norm (𝑞 ), norm (𝑘 ). This stabilizes attention computation and removes the need for additional techniques. 

## 3.3 The generalized manifold perspective of our formulation 

Boundary cases. When 𝑡 → 0, the interpolated state approaches the prior sample, i.e., x𝑡 → x0, and the predicted endpoint ˆ x1 =

net 𝜃 (x𝑡 , 𝑡, c𝐼 ) provides an explicit target on the gene-expression data manifold. The resulting velocity v𝜃 = ( ˆx1 −x𝑡 )/( 1−𝑡 ) therefore points from the prior toward a predicted clean expression profile. When 𝑡 → 1, both x𝑡 and the ideally predicted ˆ x1 approach the clean data x1, so the displacement ˆ x1 − x𝑡 connects two states close to the data manifold. Meanwhile, the direction of the predicted ve-locity is still anchored by an explicit terminal expression estimate rather than an unconstrained velocity output. 

General case. For 𝑡 ∈ ( 0, 1), the interpolated state x𝑡 may lie outside the low-dimensional and highly non-uniform manifold of observed gene-expression profiles. GeneFlow first maps this intermediate state and its histology condition to a clean endpoint estimate ˆ x1, which is directly supervised by real expression profiles, and then constructs the velocity from the displacement between x𝑡 

and ˆ x1. This endpoint-oriented parameterization encourages the predicted flow to point toward biologically plausible regions of the expression space, whereas direct velocity prediction does not explicitly associate its output with a point on the data manifold. Our empirical results suggest that it provides a useful manifold-anchored inductive bias for sparse, heavy-tailed, and technically noisy ST data. 

## 4 Experiment 

In this section, we evaluate GeneFlow on spatial gene expression prediction and conduct ablation studies across four datasets, com-paring it against six representative baselines. To make the analysis explicit, we gradually answer the following research questions: 

• RQ1: Is GeneFlow truly effective in ST benchmarks? 

• RQ2: Are GeneFlow predictions biologically meaningful? 

• RQ3: Is GeneFlow sufficiently efficient and scalable? 

• RQ4: What drives the performance of GeneFlow? 

## 4.1 Training Details 

Datasets . We evaluated our method on four ST datasets from the HEST-1k database [16], covering multiple organs, species, and disease conditions [1, 9, 20, 31]. All datasets were generated using spot-based ST platforms and provide paired histology images and spatially resolved gene expression profiles, as summarized in Tab. 6. Following the slide-level holdout protocol [41], we reserve one pre-defined slide from each dataset for evaluation and use all remaining slides for training. 

Experiment setup. We compare our method with several state-of-the-art approaches for spatial gene expression prediction, in-cluding StNet [12], EGN [35], BLEEP [34], TRIPLEX [8], Stem [41], and STFlow [13]. All baselines are implemented using their official codebases and original training configurations. To maintain train-ing stability, we clip the gradient norm to 1.0 at each iteration. The learning rate is selected from the range {1e-3 , 5e-4 , 1e-4 }, and we set the default value to 1e-4. All training and inference are performed in the original gene-count space, ensuring that the ground-truth expression and the ZINB prior lie in the same numerical space. ## 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we propose GeneFlow, a novel framework for spatially resolved gene expression inference from H&E-stained histology images. By combining flow-based generative modeling with struc-tured multi-modal fusion, GeneFlow provides a simple yet effective way to capture both morphological cues and cross-gene depen-dencies in a unified representation space. As a result, GeneFlow generates highly accurate and biologically meaningful predictions for unseen histology images at test time and achieves state-of-the-art performance across multiple datasets and evaluation metrics. Its strong accuracy, efficiency, and scalability suggest that simple generative designs can serve as practical and competitive solutions for large-scale spatial gene expression prediction. 

## 6 Limitations and Ethical Considerations 

Our results are bounded by several limitations. First, its prediction accuracy is constrained by the quality and representation capacity of the pathology foundation model. Second, GeneFlow relies pri-marily on paired H&E and ST data for supervision and does not ex-plicitly incorporate external biological knowledge, which may limit its generalizability in settings where paired training data are scarce. ## C Baselines 

We compare our method with several state-of-the-art approaches for spatial gene expression prediction, including StNet [12], EGN [35], BLEEP [34], TRIPLEX [8], Stem [41], and STFlow [13]. 

• StNet adopts a convolutional neural network as the image en-coder to extract visual features from spot images. It maps the learned visual features to gene expression profiles through a simple linear projection head. 

• EGN introduces an exemplar-guided network structure for ac-curate ST analysis. It achieves effective prediction by learning from representative and informative samples. 

• BLEEP learns joint representations of histology images and gene expressions via contrastive learning. It predicts gene expressions by retrieving and averaging expressions of similar reference spots. 

• TRIPLEX designs a multi-branch encoder architecture to capture target, local, and global information. It fuses multi-scale spatial features to achieve comprehensive transcriptomics prediction from histology images. 

• Stem integrates histology image features and gene embeddings as conditions within a DiT-based generative architecture. It gener-ates accurate spatial gene expressions by conditioning on image patches through a transformer-based diffusion model. 

• STFlow constructs a flow-based generative framework for ST prediction. It leverages conditional flow matching to model the distribution of gene expressions from histology images. ## D.1 Loss Space Analysis 

As shown in Fig. 11, increasing the hidden dimension monotonically reduces the converged training loss, indicating that larger models can better fit the FM objective in the velocity space. However, the best predictive accuracy is achieved at a moderate dimension (24), while further increasing capacity degrades performance, suggesting that minimizing LFM is not well aligned with the downstream metric and cannot reliably reflect generalization. It indicates the need for more effective training-time evaluation protocols that are better correlated with downstream prediction quality, rather than relying solely on the FM loss as a proxy.
