# Abstract 22 

Perturb-seq enables systematic interrogation of massively parallel genetic perturbations at single-cell 23 

resolution, yet exhaustive characterization of the full perturbation space remains impractical. Here, we 24 

present Graph-guided Flow Matching (GFM), a generative framework that integrates biological 25 

knowledge graphs with latent flow matching to predict post-perturbation single-cell transcriptomes. 26 

Across multiple large-scale Perturb-seq datasets, GFM outperforms a strong linear baseline and 27 

current deep-learning models in predicting unseen single-gene perturbations, while training up to 30x 28 

faster and remaining robust with limited training data. GFM also demonstrates competitive 29 

performance in the two-gene perturbation prediction task when neither perturbed gene is observed 30 

during training. Ablation experiments show that combining flow matching with knowledge graphs 31 

enables accurate prediction of perturbation responses. Finally, GFM accurately calculates 32 

transcriptional consequences of HSP90AA1 inactivation and identifies target candidates unseen by 33 

the model in chronic myeloid leukemia. Our study establishes GFM as a biologically informed 34 

generative framework that predicts single-cell perturbation responses for prioritization of therapeutic 35 

targets. 36 

# Introduction 37 

Perturbation sequencing (Perturb-seq) is a recently developed high-throughput screening technology 38 

that combines CRISPR-based gene activation or repression with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-39 

seq). This assay enables systematic examination of hundreds of thousands of genetic perturbations 40 

and their effects on the transcriptome of individual cells, facilitating the study of gene-gene 41 

interactions, cell differentiation processes, and target identification in drug development 1–5 . Although 42 

Perturb-seq captures causal relationships between target genes and their downstream effectors, 43 

experimentally testing all possible genetic perturbations and their combinations remains impractical 44 

because the complexity of the perturbation space rapidly outpaces experimental scalability. Therefore, 45 

computational tools capable of learning from existing Perturb-seq data and generalizing to unseen 46 

perturbations are instrumental for scalable target discovery and a deeper understanding of complex 47 

gene regulatory networks 6.48

Recent perturbation prediction methods leverage pretrained foundation models to improve 49 

generalization to unseen perturbations. For example, single-cell generative pre-trained transformer 50 

(scGPT) 7 adapts a transformer pretrained on observational scRNA-seq data, whereas CellFlow 851 

incorporates representations from a protein language model (ESM2 9). However, these approaches do 52 

not explicitly model perturbation-specific gene-gene interactions and therefore have limited capacity to 53 

capture the complexity of gene regulatory cascades induced by genetic perturbations. Recently, 54 

biological priors have been integrated into perturbation prediction frameworks using graph neural 55 

networks (GNNs). GEARS 10 incorporates Gene Ontology (GO) and gene co-expression network 56 

using GNNs to predict responses to unseen genetic perturbations, whereas TxPert 11 further 57 

incorporates protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, a data-derived perturbation graph, and image-58 

based features. Despite these advances, the contributions of biological priors to predictive 59 

performance remain poorly understood, and their incorporation into an effective generative framework 60 

is still lacking. 61 

Notwithstanding, advances in generative modeling have provided new opportunities for predicting 62 

cellular responses to unseen perturbations. Flow matching is a recently emerged generative modeling 63 

framework that learns continuous transformations, parameterized by neural ordinary differential 64 

equations (ODEs), from noise or arbitrary source distributions to target data distributions 12,13 , making 65 

it well-suited for single-cell data generation. Therefore, flow matching has been increasingly applied to 66 

model cellular dynamics and to interpolate or extrapolate to unobserved cellular states in tasks such 67 

as trajectory inference 14,15 and perturbation prediction 8. However, a central challenge for flow 68 

matching is that the trajectories it seeks to learn are never directly observed: single-cell perturbation 69 

experiments provide only separate populations of control and perturbed cells and not paired 70 

observations of the same cell before and after perturbation. In addition, existing flow matching 71 

approaches remain limited in their ability to incorporate biological knowledge and generalize to 72 

unseen perturbations. To address this challenge, Meta Flow Matching 16 employs a GNN to condition 73 

the source distribution, whereas MixFlow 17 learns a condition-dependent Gaussian-mixture source 74 

distribution. Despite these advances, these methods primarily improve how the flow is initialized, 75 

rather than how perturbation-induced changes are modeled throughout the transformation. 76 

Consequently, they do not explicitly model how perturbations propagate through biological systems, 77 

limiting their ability to generalize to unseen perturbations. 78

Here, we present Graph-guided Flow Matching (GFM), a generative framework that combines multiple 79 

biological knowledge graphs with latent flow matching to predict single-cell transcriptomic responses 80 

to unseen perturbations. GFM employs latent flow matching with a Gaussian source distribution, 81 

classifier-free guidance, and condition-specific optimal transport (OT) couplings to efficiently learn 82 

perturbation-induced transcriptomic changes. Through comprehensive benchmarking, we 83 

demonstrate that GFM achieves superior performance in single-gene perturbation prediction and 84 

competitive performance in two-gene perturbation settings. We functionally validate GFM predictions 85 

and show that GFM prioritizes therapeutic targets for chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). 86 

# Results 87 

## GFM model overview 88 

GFM is an end-to-end framework that takes scRNA-seq data with perturbation labels during training 89 

and generates post-perturbation gene expression profiles at single-cell resolution. Inspired by prompt-90 

guided generation in modern image synthesis models 18–20 , GFM aims to transform Gaussian noise 91 

into perturbed cellular states in a learned latent space conditioned on a knowledge-graph-derived 92 

perturbation representation. 93 

Specifically, GFM is built on three major components: a variational autoencoder (VAE), a GNN 94 

module, and a neural ODE module. The VAE learns a latent representation from log-normalized 95 

single-cell gene expression data while preserving the ability to reconstruct gene expression profiles 96 

from the latent space (Fig. 1A). We adapted the GO graph from GEARS 10 but additionally constructed 97 

a PPI graph from the STRING database 21 and a perturbation similarity graph derived from a K562 98 

genome-wide CRISPRi Perturb-seq dataset 3. These three graphs are combined and parameterized 99 

by a graph attention network (GAT), allowing for biologically informed inductive bias. Each gene in the 100 

graph is associated with a trainable perturbation embedding that propagates to neighboring genes, 101 

essentially enabling the model to generalize to unseen genetic perturbations. Importantly, the 102 

perturbation embedding guides the velocity field generated by the neural ODE module that transports 103 

Gaussian noise toward the corresponding perturbed cell state, allowing the model to learn 104 

perturbation-induced shifts in small steps rather than directly generating data points (Fig. 1A). 105

The VAE module is pretrained independently of the perturbation labels, and the GNN and neural ODE 106 

modules are trained jointly using a flow matching objective. Before the flow-matching training, we 107 

defined perturbation-specific probability paths between Gaussian noise and perturbed cells as straight 108 

interpolants coupled by OT. The neural ODE that is conditioned on the GNN-derived perturbation 109 

embeddings is then trained to match the velocity field along these OT paths. Once trained, GFM 110 

predicts cellular states under unseen perturbations in the latent space and decodes them back into 111 

gene expression space (Fig. 1A). Taken together, GFM is a generative framework for predicting 112 

single-cell transcriptomic responses to unseen perturbations by integrating biologically informed 113 

conditioning with perturbation-specific OT-based flow matching. 114 

## GFM accurately predicts unseen single-gene perturbation responses 115 

In order to test its generalization to unseen single-gene perturbations, we benchmarked GFM on four 116 

CRISPRi Perturb-seq datasets targeting thousands of genes in the chronic myeloid leukemia cell line 117 

K562, the retinal pigment epithelial cell line RPE1 4, the hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2, and 118 

the T-cell leukemia cell line Jurkat 5. For each dataset, perturbations were split into training (75%), 119 

validation (10%), and test (15%) sets across three independent data splits (Fig. 2A). GFM was 120 

compared with current models, including CellFlow 8, GEARS 10 , and scGPT 7. Additionally, a linear 121 

baseline model that learns to linearly map a data-driven PCA-derived gene embedding to the 122 

perturbation-induced gene expression shift was included 22 (see Methods). To provide empirical upper-123 

and lower-bound references, we evaluated a technical duplicate (Dup), in which half of the ground-124 

truth perturbed cells served as the prediction for the other half 23 , and a Perturb train baseline that 125 

randomly samples perturbed cells from the training set 24 . Model performance was assessed using 126 

complementary metrics that capture both mean expression prediction accuracy (Pearson correlation, 127 

Pearson delta, and mean squared error (MSE)) and distributional similarity between predicted and 128 

observed perturbation responses (2-Wasserstein distance (2-WD) and energy distance (E-distance)). 129 

To facilitate comparison of perturbation-specific prediction performance across conditions, all metrics 130 

except Pearson delta were normalized by subtracting the corresponding perturb-train baseline values. 131 

Pearson delta was exempt from this normalization as it is inherently defined relative to the perturb-132 

train baseline. Unless otherwise stated, evaluation was restricted to the top 20 differentially expressed 133 

genes (DEGs) for each perturbation to focus on perturbation-specific transcriptional responses. 134

Next, we examined the robustness of GFM under reduced training data. To this end, we evaluated its 154 

ability to predict the held-out test set as the training size was gradually reduced from 75% to 2.5%, 155 

across three independent data splits (Table S1). As expected, performance declined with a 156 

decreasing amount of training data for all models. Notably, GFM consistently outperformed CellFlow, 157

Furthermore, GFM required substantially less training time than the other deep-learning models (1.5-159 

30× faster), while achieving inference times comparable to GEARS and ~100× shorter than those of 160

for predicting unseen perturbation-induced transcriptional responses. By integrating a latent flow-162

## GFM predicts two-gene perturbations 166 

Next, we applied GFM to a CRISPRa Perturb-seq dataset comprising 131 two-gene and 152 one-167 

gene perturbations in K562 cells 3. Here, we only implemented the GO and PPI graphs in GFM since 168 

the perturbation similarity graph was constructed based on a CRISPRi dataset 4, which intrinsically 169 

carries different perturbation-specific information from the CRISPRa dataset. The two-gene 170 

perturbations in the held-out test set were further divided into three subgroups based on the number 171 

of perturbed genes seen during training: both genes seen (seen-2), one gene seen (seen-1), or 172 

neither gene seen (seen-0), reflecting increasing prediction difficulty. For the seen-2 subgroup, we 173 

additionally implemented an additive baseline, in which the predicted expression change is defined as 174 

the sum of the two corresponding single-gene perturbation changes observed in the training data. 175 

GFM achieved similar performance to the other models in the seen-2 and seen-1 subgroups but 176 

showed superior performance in the seen-0 subgroup, where neither perturbed gene was present 177 

during training (Fig. 3A, Fig. S6). For example, for the dual activation of CBL and PTPN9, GFM-178 

predicted cells overlapped more closely with the ground truth in 2D UMAP space (Fig. 3B), and the 179 

top 10 DEGs predicted by GFM and GEARS were better aligned with the observed data compared to 180 

CellFlow and scGPT (Fig. 3C). Consistent with previous reports 22 , the additive model achieved the 181 

highest performance for predicting the top 20 DEGs in the seen-2 subgroup, suggesting that the 182 

transcriptional effects of two-gene perturbations in this dataset are largely additive. In summary, GFM 183 

achieves comparable performance when one or both genes are seen and predicts two-gene 184 

perturbations better than current deep-learning models when neither perturbed gene is seen during 185 

training. 186

# Discussion 256 

In this study, we presented GFM, a biologically informed generative model for simulating post-257 

perturbation single-cell transcriptomes. Building on previous work 10,11 , we incorporated GO, PPI, and 258 

perturbation similarity graphs into the model, enabling perturbation information to propagate and 259 

aggregate through biologically meaningful relationships. Unlike transformer-based perturbation 260 

embeddings, which rely on large-scale pretraining data to learn gene and perturbation 261 

relationships 7,8,28 , GFM directly incorporates biological priors through graph structures, providing 262 

inductive biases that facilitate learning in settings with limited perturbation data while allowing dataset-263 

and context-specific optimization of perturbation representations. Beyond improving predictive 264 

performance, these biological graphs provide a structured framework for modeling how perturbations 265 

propagate through cellular systems. The perturbation similarity graph enables information sharing 266 

between perturbations with related transcriptional consequences, whereas GO and PPI graphs 267 

constrain gene-level information aggregation based on functional and physical relationships. It will be 268 

interesting to test additional biological priors as they become available, such as transcription factor 269 

regulatory networks, chromatin accessibility profiles, or ligand-receptor interaction networks. However, 270 

since GFM performance depends on perturbation-relevant graph connectivity (Fig. 4C, 271 

Supplementary Fig. 9), the utility of each prior will need to be evaluated for specific use cases rather 272 

than assuming that any additional prior knowledge improves prediction. 273 

Our analyses show that the improved performance of GFM arises from the combination of 274 

complementary architectural components. First, GNNs introduce biologically informed inductive 275 

biases that facilitate information transfer between related genes and perturbations, reducing the 276 

dependence on large-scale perturbation datasets. Second, GFM leverages the latent flow matching 277 

framework 20 , which models cellular state generation by transforming a simple Gaussian source 278 

distribution into the target perturbed transcriptomic distribution through a deterministic ODE. Unlike 279

An effective latent flow matching model relies on a smooth and well-structured latent space, defined 289 

by a VAE in GFM. Designing an autoencoder that simultaneously captures generalizable biological 290 

variation and accurately reconstructs gene expression profiles remains challenging. Recent efforts 291 

have focused on developing universal single-cell foundation models trained with both observational 292 

and perturbational data 7,28–32 . However, their efficacy in predicting single-cell transcriptomes of out-of-293 

distribution perturbations still requires further systematic evaluation. Here, we implemented a 294 

standard VAE to demonstrate the effectiveness of the GFM framework and provide a foundation for 295 

future integration of more advanced encoder architectures. We speculate that as improved 296 

representations of cellular states become increasingly more available, they will further enhance the 297 

ability of flow-based generative models to capture complex perturbation responses more accurately. 298

outperforms all other current deep-learning models in predicting unseen single-gene perturbations, 300

# Materials and Methods 323 

## GFM 324 

GFM is a deep learning framework designed to model and generate perturbation effects on single-cell 325 

transcriptomic profiles. GFM consists of three core modules: (i) a VAE to encode gene expression 326 

profiles into the latent space and decode them back to the gene expression space, (ii) a GNN for 327 

learning perturbation representations informed by biological knowledge graphs, and (iii) a neural ODE 328 

module to generate perturbed cells in the latent space conditioned by the GNN-derived perturbation 329 

representation. 330

## Data preprocessing 483 

For the Perturb-seq data from Replogle et al. 4 (K562, RPE1, K562 genome-wide perturbation 484 

screening), we followed GEARS’ approach to filter for strong perturbations. Specifically, perturbations 485 

were retained if they exhibited more than 50 differentially expressed genes, a percent knockdown of 486 

at least 30%, and at least 25 cells. We further removed individual cells that did not show evidence of 487 

knockdown at the single-cell level. For each perturbed gene, only cells in which the expression of the 488 

targeted gene was at or below the 10th percentile of its expression in non-targeting control cells were 489 

retained. HepG2 and Jurkat datasets from Nadig et al. 5 were pre-filtered in STATE 31 for strong 490 

knockdown efficiency. For the two-gene perturbation dataset from Norman et al. 3, we followed the 491 

steps in GEARS ( GEARS Norman preprocessing ) to reformat the metadata without further filtering. 492 

Following perturbation-level filtering, all perturb-seq data were normalized by library size and log1p 493 

transformed. The top 5000 highly variable genes and all perturbed genes were retained for 494 

downstream analysis. Per-condition differentially expressed genes relative to the non-targeting control 495 

were ranked by absolute effect size using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test 40 .496

## Ablation experiments 530 

For the ablation of GFM modules, we examined the role of the flow-matching objective and graph 531 

topology. To disable flow matching, we replaced the OT velocity-matching objective with direct 532 

regression from the source latent state  𝑧 0 to the target latent state  𝑧 1. Specifically, the neural ODE 533 

module took uncoupled Gaussian noise and a sinusoidal time embedding 36 at  𝑡 = 0  as the inputs and 534 

was optimized with an MSE loss between the predicted latent endpoint and the target latent state. 535 

This preserves the same conditional architecture but removes the OT coupling and the continuous 536 

transport formulation. To remove biologically informed graph topology, we kept the same number of 537 

nodes and edges but randomly permuted graph source nodes, thereby disrupting biologically 538 

meaningful connectivity while preserving graph density and degree scale. We performed both 539 

operations if both modules were ablated. 540 

For the ablation of different types of knowledge graphs, we evaluated the contribution of each 541 

biological graph while keeping the rest of the model fixed. As mentioned above, we assessed whether 542 

predictive performance is driven primarily by GO, PPI, perturbation similarity, or their combination by 543 

randomizing the selected biological graph(s). 544 

For all ablation experiments, model performance was assessed on held-out test conditions. 545 

## Graph connectivity analysis 546 

We quantified whether local graph connectivity was associated with predictive performance in GFM. 547 

For each held-out test condition, we extracted upstream source nodes by traversing incoming edges 548 

in the directed graph for two hops, corresponding to the local neighborhood used in this analysis. We 549 

then identified which source nodes had been observed in the training split and recorded two 550 

connectivity statistics: the number of trained source nodes and the fraction of all source nodes that 551 

were present in training. To quantify transcriptional similarity between a test perturbation and its 552 

trained graph neighbors, we computed the mean expression profile for each condition, subtracted the 553 

mean control profile to obtain a perturbation effect vector, and calculated cosine similarity between the 554 

held-out condition and each trained source-node perturbation reference. For each test condition, we 555 

summarized these similarities by their maximum and median values. This analysis was performed 556 

using three predefined data splits of the indicated single-gene perturbation datasets. 557

## CML target identification 558 

GFM was trained on the Replogle K562 genome-wide perturbation screen dataset 4 using a 90% 559 

training and 10% validation split and no held-out test set. The GO graph, PPI graph, and the 560 

perturbation similarity graph as mentioned above were used here. The predictions were generated for 561 

every perturbation node in the graph. For each perturbation, 20 synthetic single-cell profiles were 562 

sampled for downstream analysis. Predicted cells were then aggregated to perturbation-level mean 563 

expression profiles. The resulting perturbation means were embedded by PCA followed by Leiden 564 

clustering and t-SNE visualization. Cluster identities were annotated by ranking differentially 565 

expressed genes across Leiden clusters with a Wilcoxon test and performing Gene Ontology 566 

Biological Process 41 enrichment on the 100 most downregulated genes per cluster. 567 

For target identification, predicted perturbation profiles were compared against sampled control cells 568 

by differential expression analysis. Using HSP90AA1 as the query perturbation, we quantified 569 

similarity between the query signature and every other perturbation by the Jaccard index computed 570 

on the two sets of 100 downregulated genes. Functional interpretation of the query signature was 571 

obtained by Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the same 100 downregulated genes using 572 

`gseapy` 42 .573
