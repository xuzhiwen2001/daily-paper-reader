## Abstract 

Predicting the RNA and protein content of cells from rou-tine H&E images could extend spatial molecular profiling to existing slide archives. However, both H&E–spatial pro-teomics and H&E–spatial transcriptomics paired datasets are limited by the number of available markers and the in-sufficient availability of high-quality, cell-resolved data. We present SCOPE ( Single-Cell Omics Prediction via RNA– protein prior Enhancement), a unified framework that lever-ages biologically complementary information between RNA and proteins to address the limited availability of single-omics supervision. Our key insight is that, although RNA and proteins represent distinct molecular modalities, their biological relationships provide valuable shared knowledge that can benefit single-omics prediction tasks. To this end, SCOPE introduces biologically informed biomarker priors to enhance shared gene-level representations, en-abling knowledge transfer across RNA and protein modal-ities through gene–protein correspondence and molecular associations. By exploiting these complementary relation-ships, SCOPE enables both prediction tasks to benefit from a shared backbone enriched with cross-omics biological knowledge. Furthermore, its biomarker correlation de-sign facilitates the prediction of unseen markers, extend-ing spatially resolved molecular profiling beyond directly supervised targets. Across test datasets spanning 16 or-gans, SCOPE improves the average RNA Pearson correla-tion over the strongest baseline by 8.8%. On two external protein cohorts, it improves the average Pearson correla-tion over the strongest baseline by 9.7% for seen markers and 11.9% for unseen markers. 

## 1. Introduction 

Spatially resolved transcriptomic and proteomic measure-ments provide complementary insights into cellular states and tissue organization in health and disease [5, 13, 23]. However, obtaining these measurements at single-cell res-olution requires costly and technically demanding as-says, including in situ spatial transcriptomics [14] and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) techniques such as CODEX [10]. In contrast, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining is routinely performed in pathology. Predicting single-cell RNA and protein expression directly from H&E images could therefore extend spatial molecular profiling to routinely collected tissue sections and existing slide archives, supporting biological investigation and potentially informing clinical decisions. Recent models have demonstrated the feasibility of pre-dicting spatial molecular profiles from histology. How-ever, many approaches target a single molecular modality or a predefined set of markers [7, 12, 31], limiting their applicability across assays with different molecular panels (Fig. 1(b)). Joint learning from transcriptomic and pro-teomic data offers a promising opportunity to leverage their complementary biological information. However, these modalities are rarely profiled on the same tissue sections, while variations in marker panels leave many molecular tar-gets without direct H&E supervision. These limitations mo-tivate a unified framework that can exploit complementary knowledge across unpaired omics modalities and generalize to molecular markers beyond those directly observed during training. Addressing these challenges requires a unified repre-sentation that bridges complementary supervision across molecular modalities and biomarker targets. The corre-spondence between proteins and their coding genes pro-vides a natural biological link between RNA and protein measurements, enabling supervision from either modality to contribute to shared gene-level representations. In this paper, we introduce SCOPE, a unified frame-work for single-cell RNA and protein prediction from H&E images. At its core, SCOPE leverages the biological cor-respondence between genes and their encoded proteins to establish a shared molecular representation, enabling complementary supervision across transcriptomic and pro-teomic modalities. To extend prediction beyond directly supervised targets, we further introduce biologically in-formed biomarker co-encoding, which incorporates gene co-expression patterns, molecular interactions, and pathway relationships to transfer knowledge from observed to un-seen markers. SCOPE improves the average Pearson correlation over the strongest RNA baseline by 8.8%, and over the strongest protein baseline by 9.7% and 11.9% for seen and unseen markers, respectively. 

## 2. Related work 

Molecular profiles from H&E. Most methods predict ei-ther spatial RNA or protein expression from H&E, at the cell, spot, or pixel level. For RNA, DeepSpot-M uses gene-conditioned decoding from image tokens [22], and CELLO predicts per-cell expression using features from a pathol-ogy foundation model [7]. Pixel-level approaches recon-struct expression maps from spot-level or binned-transcript supervision [38, 39]. For protein, virtual staining methods such as ROSIE, GigaTIME, and HistoPlexer generate mul-tiplexed images [1, 31, 35]. Fewer methods address both assays. H2O uses parallel decoders trained on consecutive sections profiled by both assays [11]; CUBE fuses protein-derived and predicted transcriptomic representations [9]. SCOPE trains on sections with either RNA or protein mea-surements from unpaired datasets, but couples the assays through shared gene embeddings and predicts all requested targets in one forward pass. It combines cell-level and dense expression predictions. 

Marker embeddings. Models predicting many molecu-lar targets use learned identifiers or embeddings derived from sequence or text. CAPTAIN and CARE maintain 2separate gene and protein embeddings [15, 40], whereas UCE and VirTues use protein language-model representa-tions [24, 33]. To generalize beyond directly supervised genes, SGN uses gene descriptions [37], DeepSpot-M uses frozen gene embeddings [22], GSAT-RAG retrieves expres-sion information from seen genes [34], and HINGE con-ditions a pretrained single-cell model on histology [6]. In contrast, SCOPE learns a shared embedding for each gene and its encoded protein from both assays. For genes with-out H&E-linked labels, it complements this embedding with a sparse, signed combination of related genes, chosen by single-cell co-expression and by interaction and pathway re-sources and weighted by single-cell expression covariation. 

## 3. Method 

Method overview. SCOPE predicts the RNA and protein of every H&E nucleus from the image alone (Fig. 2). Ev-ery marker is read through a gene embedding that RNA and protein share and that adds a combination of related genes (Sec. 3.1). Two branches read these embeddings at two res-olutions, a cell model that scores one image vector per nu-cleus (Sec. 3.2) and a dense expression field that scores ev-ery pixel and is trained on transcript positions and protein images (Sec. 3.3). Late fusion averages their predictions (Sec. 3.3). 

3.1. Biomarker co-encoding 

We compose each embedding from the gene’s own learn-able vector and the vectors of related genes, allowing infor-mation learned from supervised partner genes to improve the representations of genes with limited or no direct su-pervision. We take candidate partners of each gene from single-cell co-expression and from STRING [29], which scores functional associations between proteins, and Re-actome [20], which organizes curated molecular reactions into pathways. The signed weight of each partner is esti-mated from gene covariation in single-cell transcriptomic data, and the database links set the prior mean of this esti-mate. 3.3. Dense branch 

The dense branch predicts expression on a pixel grid, trained on transcript positions and CODEX pixels, and a cell takes the sum (RNA) or mean (protein) of the field over its pixels. It reads the H&E at two scales. Foundation-model tokens carry tissue context but are coarse, one 7 μm token being about the width of a nucleus, while the pixels resolve nuclear outlines. The field is predicted on 0.875 μm pixels, in tiles of 56 μm ( 64 ×64 pixels). Frozen H-optimus-1 reads the 112 μm box around each tile at 0.5 μm/px ( 16 ×

16 tokens of 7 μm). We keep the 8 × 8 tokens over the tile, one per 8 × 8 pixels, so that every kept token is encoded with at least 28 μm of surrounding tissue, and reduce them to 256 dimensions by a PCA fitted on training tiles. On windows of 4 × 4 tiles (224 μm, 32 × 32 tokens, 256 × 256 

pixels), a 1 × 1 convolution and two ConvNeXt blocks [18] process the tokens, whose 7 × 7 depthwise convolutions let each token use its neighbors within 42 μm, across tiles that the encoder saw separately. The output is upsampled ×8

bilinearly and concatenated with a two-layer CNN ( 3 × 3

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Setup 

Data. We train on 43 Xenium sections from 10 organs (11 in-house and 32 public), with panels of 280–5,098 genes (median 300), and 76 CODEX in-house sections (517 in-testinal tissue pieces) from three donors, with 58 or 59 protein markers. Sections are registered to H&E using VALIS [8], and measured cells are matched one-to-one with H&E nuclei, yielding 7.87M Xenium and 26.49M CODEX training cells. The Xenium sections cover 5,828 genes, each measured on a median of three sections. To estimate the relation weights S, we additionally use RNA profiles of 2.44M cells from 150 training CITE-seq datasets across 21 studies [28]. These datasets provide gene co-expression in-formation but no H&E-linked supervision. Four Xenium and three CODEX sections are reserved for validation. We evaluate on (i) 16 Xenium sections from 16 organs, six of them from organs without any training section (3.38M cells, 5,649 genes); (ii) 22 CODEX sections from a fourth donor, in-house colon (4.11M cells, 57 markers); and (iii) two external colorectal cancer cohorts: CRC [26, 27] (35 patients, 139 cores, 192,779 cells) and ORION [16] (40 patients, 41 slides, 22.32M cells). No evaluation patient appears in SCOPE’s training data. Seen RNA genes are training targets, while unseen genes are withheld from di-rect training supervision. Protein markers are considered unseen if they are absent from the CODEX training panels. 

Evaluation. We compute Pearson correlation between predicted and measured expression across cells for each marker, then average over markers and evaluation units. Missing predictions contribute zero correlation. For the baseline comparison in Tab. 2, CRC correlations are com-puted using cells pooled by patient across 135 eligible cores (190,666 cells), and ORION is evaluated on the four pa-tients excluded from MIPHEI-ViT training. Component ab-lations use all ORION patients. Statistical significance is assessed using one-sided paired 

t-tests, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction within each table ( q < 0.05 ). The testing units are section–gene pairs for Tab. 1, markers for Tab. 2, and sections or patients for the component ablations. Results are averaged over three seeds.

4.3. Results 

RNA. Table 1 compares SCOPE with four published mod-els on the 16 test sections. SCOPE achieves the high-est correlation in all ten columns. Averaged over the four organ groups, it reaches 0.484 on HVG10 and 0.167 on all genes, against 0.437 and 0.154 for the strongest base-line, DeepSpot-M fine-tuned on our training sections, and it is significantly above every baseline in both average columns. The margin over the models run with their re-leased weights is much larger (CELLO 0.307 and 0.114, DeepSpot-M 0.189 and 0.049), and the two models that learn from spots, sCellST and DeepSpot2Cell, average be-low 0.27 on HVG10 after retraining on our sections, since a spot label sums the expression of many cells and does not tell which cell expresses a gene. The largest gains are on HVG10, the genes that vary most between the cells of a section and therefore separate its cell types and states. The lead holds where training data are scarce. In post hoc views, SCOPE is significantly above the fine-tuned DeepSpot-M on the six sections from organs without any training sec-tion (+0.0132), as on the ten sections from organs with one (+0.0138), and on genes labeled on only one to three train-ing sections (+0.0193). 4.4. Ablation study on joint training 

Table 3 tests whether RNA supervision improves protein prediction when the two assays are measured on separate sections. Each part of SCOPE is trained on both assays and compared with a single-modality counterpart using the same number of protein steps, controlling for additional protein training. Joint training improves prediction of seen protein markers in both external cohorts. The cell model gains +0.0271 on CRC and +0.0246 on ORION, the dense branch +0.0209 and +0.0373, and the dual branch +0.0244 and +0.0288, all significant. The benefit extends to un-seen markers, where the dual branch gains +0.0413 on CRC and +0.0306 on ORION, both significant, while the dense branch achieves the largest gain on unseen CRC markers (+0.0698). Two mechanisms may contribute to these gains. First, RNA labels come from 43 sections across 10 organs, whereas protein labels come from intestinal tissue of three donors, exposing the shared image representations to a broader range of tissues. Second, shared gene embeddings provide a route for cross-assay transfer. All unseen pro-tein markers but one have coding genes supervised by RNA labels, so their embeddings can benefit from RNA supervi-sion. These results demonstrate that RNA supervision bene-fits protein prediction, including markers without direct pro-tein supervision. 

4.5. Ablation study on dual-branch fusion 

The cell and dense branches predict molecular expres-sion at different spatial resolutions, and the dual branch combines their predictions with fixed equal weights. In Tab. 3, the dual branch significantly outperforms the cell branch in every column and the dense branch in every seen-marker column, by +0.0120 on RNA, +0.0099 on CRC, and +0.0144 on ORION. These improvements suggest that the two branches provide complementary predictive informa-tion. For unseen markers, the relative contributions of the branches differ. On unseen CRC markers, the dense branch achieves the highest correlation (0.225), followed by the dual branch (0.185) and the cell branch (0.066). On unseen ORION markers, the dual and dense branches achieve com-parable performance (0.386), while on unseen RNA genes, the dual branch outperforms both individual branches. Overall, these results show that the cell and dense branches provide complementary predictions, with their equal-weight combination improving performance over both branches on seen markers. ## 5. Conclusion 

We presented SCOPE, which learns to predict the RNA and protein of every cell from H&E alone using unpaired sec-tions, each measuring one assay with its own panel. One learnable embedding per gene, read by both the RNA and the protein heads and composed from the embeddings of related genes, connects the two assays and reaches markers that no section labels. Three findings follow. First, RNA supervision improves protein prediction on both external cohorts, although no sec-tion measures both assays. Second, gene embeddings com-posed from related genes predict RNA genes that no H&E section labels, and the gain depends on the right partners. Third, an equal-weight fusion of the cell-level branch and the dense branch beats the cell-level branch on every test set and the dense branch on the seen markers of every test set. 8Across RNA and protein, SCOPE outperforms the strongest RNA baseline on average and, averaged over the two exter-nal cohorts, the strongest protein baseline on seen and un-seen markers.
