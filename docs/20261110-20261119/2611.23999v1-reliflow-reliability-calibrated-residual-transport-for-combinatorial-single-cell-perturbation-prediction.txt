# Abstract 

Predicting the transcriptional response to combined perturbations requires distinguishing interactions between interventions from errors in their individual effect estimates. The latter become more pronounced when single-perturbation evidence is sparse or withheld. We introduce R ELI FLOW , a conditional flow-matching model that estimates the reliability of single-gene effects from training-only cross-fitting and uses those estimates to calibrate the additive source distribution. The velocity is decomposed into a known correction of the additive reference and a learned residual transport field. On Norman genetic perturbations, R ELI FLOW 

reduces mean absolute error (MAE) from 0.142 to 0.131 for unseen combinations of observed singles and from 0.198 to 0.178 when constituent singles are withheld. On held-out ComboSciPlex drug combinations, MAE decreases from 0.191 to 0.176. Non-additive residual correlation improves from 0.574 to 0.638, and component ablations identify the contribution of reliability weighting. The results indicate that accounting for uncertain constituent effects improves generalization to unseen genetic and chemical combinations, with a moderate increase in training cost. 

# 1 Introduction 

A transcriptional response to a genetic or chemical perturba-tion is a distribution over heterogeneous cellular states rather than a single deterministic vector. Predicting that distribution for interventions not yet measured could help prioritize ex-periments and explore regulatory interactions, but the space of potential combinations quickly exceeds the available ob-servations [1, 2, 3]. A typical prediction system can learn common responses from training perturbations while miss-ing rare response states or interaction-specific changes. The central difficulty is therefore not only to reconstruct the domi-nant expression pattern, but also to determine which evidence supports the predicted deviation from a reference state. A natural reference is additive composition. When the ef-fects of interventions a and b are measured separately, their summed expression shifts offer a useful prediction of the com-bined treatment. Indeed, simple linear strategies remain com-petitive against considerably larger perturbation models on some benchmarks [4, 5]. The value of additive composition, however, depends on how accurately its constituent effects were estimated. This is particularly consequential when evalu-ating generalization beyond familiar intervention combinations. For a pair of well-characterized perturbations, the additive com-ponent may be stable and the residual largely reflects interac-tions. For a pair involving an unseen perturbation, the residual also contains error inherited from extrapolating a single-effect predictor. Treating both cases as equivalent may obscure what the model is learning. Conditional flow matching provides a flexible way to learn distributions without assuming a pairing between individual control and treated cells [6, 7]. An additive estimate can in-stead be used as the flow source, leaving a conditional velocity field to learn the residual transport. This is an informative starting point: it reduces the burden of generating a complete expression vector from an unrelated noise distribution. Yet an additive anchor estimated from sparse evidence is not equally trustworthy across genes and perturbations. If its errors are ignored, a velocity model may use its capacity to repair the anchor rather than model higher-order biological effects. The issue is most acute precisely where predictive models are ex-pected to extrapolate. 

We ask whether the reliability of observed single-perturbation evidence should determine where residual transport begins and which part of the required correction is learned. We develop R ELI FLOW to make this choice ex-plicit. It estimates a gene-wise support scale using cross-fitted training perturbations, shrinks uncertain additive shifts toward a learned prior, and adjusts source variability accordingly. We then decompose the conditional velocity into a known correc-tion that restores the raw additive reference and a learned field that targets the remaining interaction-dependent transport. In this way, anchor uncertainty is represented rather than silently mixed into the nonlinear residual. The model makes three distinctions operational. First, re-liability is estimated from training data and transferred to excluded interventions through their representations; it cannot use the held-out outcomes that it seeks to predict. Second, full-response reconstruction and recovery of non-additive effects receive separate diagnostic analyses. Third, improvements must be assessed on matched partitions and compute budgets rather than inferred from attractive samples or one aggregate score. These choices reflect a broader principle in predictive modeling: model confidence and downstream utility must be tied to the evidence actually available, not simply to the plau-sibility of generated outputs [8]. We evaluate the resulting model using condition-disjoint genetic and chemical perturbation splits, interaction-sensitive 1arXiv:2611.23999v1 [stat.ML] 18 Nov 2026 metrics, and component ablations. 

# 2 Related work 

2.1 Learning perturbation response distribu-tions 

Single-cell response prediction has progressed from latent perturbation arithmetic in scGen [9] to neural optimal trans-port [7], compositional perturbation autoencoders [3], graph-based gene perturbation models such as GEARS [2], and predictive systems for both chemical and genetic interven-tions [10, 11]. Drug combinations can be assayed through combinatorial transcriptomic screens [12], while statistical models such as GPerturb offer an alternative account of gene-level variation [13]. Recent models target the response dis-tribution itself, including direct flow models and conditional distribution shift prediction [14, 15, 16]. Heterogeneous out-comes and multi-response populations make it important to evaluate more than mean error [17, 18]. Large-scale pretrained representations offer reusable gene features. These include scBERT [19], transformer-based cell representations [20], scGPT [21], Geneformer [22], and uni-versal cell embeddings [23]. The deep generative scVI frame-work models technical variation and uncertainty in single-cell expression [24]; foundation-model approaches and virtual-cell benchmarks extend the scope of perturbation predic-tion [25, 26]. Benchmarking studies report competitive perfor-mance from strong linear baselines and sensitivity to evaluation metrics and data composition [4, 5, 27]. These observations motivate separating an uncertain single-effect estimate from non-additive response generation. 

2.2 Transport, generative dynamics and evi-dence quality 

Diffusion modeling [28], score-based stochastic dynam-ics [29], and flow matching [6] provide related approaches to learning complex distributions. Stochastic interpolants formalize the relationship between diffusion and determin-istic transport [30]. Kernel two-sample comparisons furnish distribution-level losses without observed cell-to-cell corre-spondence [31, 32]. This is important because destructive expression profiling does not provide paired before-after ob-servations for the same cell. Structure priors can be sensitive to dropout and nuisance variation [33, 34]. Differential atten-tion and other selective representation mechanisms provide architectural options but do not themselves establish calibrated perturbation effects [35]. Reliability and grounding also arise in other forms of con-ditional prediction. Structured multimodal diffusion has been studied for multi-agent trajectory forecasting [36], whereas ra-diology grounding and radiographic world models connect gen-erated representations to evidence in clinical imaging [37, 38]. Medical visual reasoning and uncertainty-aware imaging mod-els provide further perspectives on distinguishing plausible outputs from supported predictions [39, 8]. These studies ad-dress different tasks rather than direct single-cell baselines. Here we take the narrower position that training-supported single-effect reliability should explicitly modulate the source distribution used for conditional transport.

5.1 Data, preprocessing, and held-out parti-tions 

We evaluated genetic perturbation prediction on the Norman dataset [1] and chemical combination prediction on ComboSci-Plex [12, 40]. We split perturbation conditions, rather than individual cells, before fitting predictors or selecting hyperpa-rameters. Expression was normalized by counts per 10,000 followed by log(1 + x). The 5,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected using training cells only. We evalu-ated three random seeds in each dataset. Figure 3 shows the allocation of observed double-perturbation conditions. The Norman seen-single partition contains 105 training sin-gles and 91/20/20 training/validation/test double conditions, with 68,000/10,000/13,000 cells, respectively. In the excluded-single partition, training uses 93 singles and 79 double condi-tions, validation uses 20 doubles, and testing uses 12 withheld singles and 32 doubles; the corresponding cell counts are 62,000/9,000/20,000. There are no validation singles in either partition. The excluded genes occur in no training singles, training doubles, or validation conditions. All these counts are exact. ComboSciPlex comprises A549 responses to seven single-drug conditions and 25 drug pairs. All seven measured singles and 15 double-drug conditions are in training; five double con-ditions are used for validation and five for testing (Table 2). The split contains 43,000 training cells, 9,000 validation cells, and 11,378 test cells. The complete identities of the five valida-tion and five test pairs appear in Appendix E. Both evaluation splits are condition-disjoint and use the same 5,000-gene nor-malization procedure as Norman. 

5.2 Baselines, metrics, and experimental con-trols 

We compared R ELI FLOW with the additive predictor, CPA [3], GEARS [2], scDFM [14], and CellFlow [15] where applicable. Each tabulated comparison uses the corresponding partition defined above. Mean absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE) measure expression reconstruction; differential-expression Spearman correlation (DE-Spearman or DE-ρ) mea-sures gene-rank agreement; effect Pearson correlation mea-sures perturbation-effect recovery. We also report the discrimi-nation score (DS) and, for chemical perturbations, maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). We use the same metric implemen-tation for models within each table; higher values are preferred for correlations and DS, and lower values for MAE, MSE, and MMD. To evaluate non-additive interaction, we use held-out dou-ble conditions for which both constituent single-intervention effects are separately observed. The mean interaction target follows Eq. (16). Metrics include Pearson correlation of mean residual effects (Resid. r), residual MAE, and F1 for recov-ery of the top-k interaction-associated genes. The additive predictor has no separately learned interaction ranking; corre-sponding correlation and top-k F1 entries are undefined. The four ablations remove reliability weights, the analytic correc-tion field, the pairwise moment penalty, or adaptive source covariance, respectively. The leakage audit was performed on the excluded-single genetic split. No excluded gene was present in training singles, training doubles, or validation conditions. No test response was used to fit preprocessing, estimate single effects, train the reliability head, or select the model. The HVG selector and the cross-fitting procedure used training data only; hyperparameter selection used the validation conditions only (Appendix F). 

# 6 Results 

6.1 Genetic perturbation prediction 

On Norman, R ELI FLOW reduced MAE from 0.142 for scDFM to 0.131 when constituent singles were observed during train-ing, and from 0.198 to 0.178 when the single-gene perturba-tions were excluded (Table 3). These are absolute reductions of 0.011 and 0.020, corresponding to 7.7% and 10.1% relative reductions. MSE fell from 0.056 to 0.049 and from 0.093 to 0.078, respectively. DE-Spearman increased from 0.724 to 0.752 in the seen-single partition, and from 0.548 to 0.601 in the excluded-single partition. Effect Pearson and DS also improved in both settings. Figure 4 compares MAE with the strongest listed baseline, scDFM. 

6.2 Chemical combination prediction 

On the A549 ComboSciPlex split, R ELI FLOW achieved an MAE of 0.176 versus 0.191 for scDFM (Table 4), a reduction of 0.015 (7.9%). MMD decreased from 0.074 to 0.063, while DE-ρ increased from 0.605 to 0.641 and DS from 0.630 to 0.670. The gain therefore extended beyond genetic pertur-bations to a separate chemical response setting, although the datasets differ in interventions and evaluation design. 

6.3 Recovering non-additive effects 

A model may reconstruct full expression accurately while fail-ing to recover the deviation from additive single-perturbation effects. We therefore compared interaction-specific predictions on held-out pairs with observed constituent singles (Table 5). RELI FLOW increased residual Pearson correlation from 0.574 to 0.638 relative to scDFM and reduced residual MAE from 0.078 to 0.068. Top-k F1 increased from 0.493 to 0.551. These comparisons support improved population-level recovery of non-additive effects; they do not identify a unique causal cell-to-cell trajectory.

# 7 Discussion 

Reliability-aware source calibration addresses an error source that is distinct from biological interaction: the uncertainty of the separately predicted single effects used to assemble a combined perturbation. The improvement on Norman is larger in absolute MAE when constituent singles are excluded from training (0.020) than when they have been observed (0.011). This pattern is consistent with the motivation for evidence-dependent calibration, although it does not isolate the calibration mechanism by itself. The ablation results provide a more direct comparison, with the largest loss of accuracy following removal of reliability weights. Interaction recovery is another useful test of the proposed decomposition. Residual correlation, residual MAE, and top-k

F1 all improve over scDFM on pairs whose constituent single effects can be independently measured. These are population-level estimates of non-additivity, not identified cell-level causal effects. Since single-cell sequencing profiles distinct cells before and after perturbation, multiple transport paths may agree with the same observed endpoint distributions. The observed scale–error correlations suggest that relia-bility estimates are associated with predictive difficulty, but correlation is not a demonstration of calibrated uncertainty. Probability coverage and error-versus-scale calibration would require additional testing. The excluded-intervention stratum has the highest error, indicating that extrapolation to compo-nents without measured training responses remains difficult. The cost comparison also shows a trade-off: the improved accuracy required 2.4 additional GPU-hours and slightly lower sampling throughput at equal NFE. Three-seed results quantify repeatability but do not establish statistical significance across perturbation conditions. 

# 8 Conclusion 

RELI FLOW combines a training-only estimate of single-effect reliability with a calibrated additive source and an explicit correction-plus-residual velocity decomposition. On condition-disjoint genetic and chemical perturbation tests, it improves reconstruction and interaction-sensitive metrics compared with the reported baselines. Reliability weighting makes the largest contribution in the ablation study; improving calibration for genuinely unseen perturbations remains an important next step.
