# Abstract 

A cell observed after a gene knockdown has an uncertain fu-ture, both in how its appearance will evolve and when it will divide. Existing approaches predict endpoints or population-level changes without jointly modeling individual trajecto-ries and division timing. In this work, we present MitoWorld, a stochastic world model that generates these future ap-pearances as continuous evolution in a Gaussian reference latent, terminated by a division hazard and converted into images by a conditional diffusion renderer. The ordinary transition provably preserves its Gaussian reference, tying its noise to its dynamics; paired observations identify its discrete transition where stationary marginals cannot. The hazard turns division timing into a survival likelihood that can be tested against real cycle lengths. On 325 held-out MitoCheck tracks spanning 17 knockdowns and a control, MitoWorld lowers FID by 34.6% and the video metric JEDi by 45.6% relative to the strongest baselines. Its latent-space prediction intervals achieve near-nominal 90% coverage, are 35% narrower than those of a model with freely fitted noise. The hazard also yields cell-cycle length distributions with KS distance close to the model-based finite-sample ref-erence. Testable single-cell stochastic dynamics thus offer a generative tool for time-lapse perturbation screens. 

# 1. Introduction 

The phenotype of a gene knockdown unfolds over time, and cells that start alike can follow different growth trajectories and divide at different times [3, 23, 48, 58, 63]. We therefore aim to model an observed cell’s future appearance distribu-tion under a given gene knockdown until division, that is, a world model of the single-cell cycle [14, 73]. Unlike snap-shot images, time-lapse screens follow individual cells over time under controlled interventions, so each generated future appearance can be scored against the real one. Existing approaches to predicting cellular responses largely model population-level changes, through either end-point prediction or time-lapse video generation (Fig. 1(a)). First, one-step endpoint predictors learn to map control pop-ulations to perturbed populations [13, 41]. Because destruc-tive measurements prevent repeated observations of the same cells, these methods learn from unpaired snapshots of differ-ent cell populations [72]. Coupling populations across time can infer trajectories, but lacks directly observed same-cell correspondence [60, 66]. Methods limited to endpoint pre-diction additionally leave cellular growth and division timing unspecified. Second, time-lapse video predictors extend gen-erative video models [8, 18, 31] to microscopy, synthesizing population-level cellular dynamics [32]. Although these videos can depict mitosis, they target population-level visual dynamics rather than explicitly modeling each individual cell’s growth trajectory and division time. Our key insight is to make three aspects of a single-cell fu-1arXiv:2611.23953v1 [cs.CV] 18 Nov 2026 ture explicit: cross-time dependence, predictive uncertainty, and the division event. A paired transition learns which future belongs to the same cell; a reference-constrained stochastic kernel couples the amount of noise to the learned dynamics; and a state-, age-, and gene-conditioned hazard turns division into a random stopping time. These compo-nents retain the state, transition, termination, and renderer structure of latent world models [25, 27, 61, 79], while mak-ing their predictions separately testable (Fig. 1(b)). We instantiate this design as MitoWorld . A nonlin-ear encoder maps frames to a Gaussian-regularized latent space, where a gene-conditioned Ornstein–Uhlenbeck transi-tion [69] generates future states and a conditional diffusion renderer converts these states into images. The ordinary transition preserves its Gaussian reference and composes consistently across step sizes (Prop. 1). Under this reference model, paired observations identify the discrete transition kernel that population marginals cannot identify (Prop. 2). A hazard and two terminal transitions complete each sampled cycle with a two-daughter composite image. The method is designed to preserve individual correspondence, quantify possible futures, and predict when a trajectory terminates, rather than optimize appearance alone. We evaluate on MitoCheck [48], using 1,183 training and 325 test tracks spanning 17 knockdowns and a scrambled control. Our experiments evaluate image and sequence fi-delity together with same-cell retrieval, then test the paired dynamics, latent uncertainty and stability, and division tim-ing in the same order as these claims. In summary, our contributions are threefold: • We cast the single-cell cycle under genetic perturbation as a world-model problem , where a cell’s state evolves stochastically and stops at a state-dependent division event, so each cell’s future is a distribution over paths (§3.1). • We propose MitoWorld and prove that its transition pre-serves a Gaussian reference distribution over any number of steps (Prop. 1) and show that its discrete transition kernel is identifiable from per-cell pairs but not from stationary marginals (Prop. 2). • On 325 held-out MitoCheck tracks, we demonstrate Mi-toWorld’s forecast fidelity, uncertainty quantification, and cell-cycle timing : FID decreases by 34.6% relative to the strongest baseline, latent prediction intervals are 34.8% narrower than those with freely fitted noise while maintaining near-nominal 90% coverage, and generated cell-cycle length distributions achieve mean per-condition KS close to the model-based finite-sample reference (§4). 

# 2. Related Work 

Perturbation response prediction. Perturbation screens compare profiles across conditions, with transcriptional readouts of pooled screens [21, 55, 75] or morphological features from microscopy [10, 16, 17]. Predictors then map a control population to a perturbed one, in expres-sion space [13, 41, 42, 57] or, with conditional genera-tive models, in image space [9, 46, 51, 78], and what they pair is one population with another, because destructive profiling never observes the same cell twice [72]. Snap-shots at successive times let optimal transport, Schr¨ odinger bridges, and flow matching pair populations across time as well [12, 33, 36, 60, 66, 67], but a coupling fitted to marginals cannot say which future belongs to which cell [39]. Live-cell tracking pairs each cell with its own future through the whole cycle [29, 45, 47, 48, 80], yet such tracks have served classification and mitotic staging rather than gener-ation, and no model above predicts when a given cell will divide [15, 58]. MitoWorld takes these same-cell pairs as su-pervision, which identify the transition that marginals leave ambiguous (Prop. 2), and models the response as a process ending at a predicted division rather than an endpoint (§3.1). 

World models for visual dynamics. World models learn how a system evolves from observations and simulate fu-tures from a given state [25, 26], and three ingredients recur. 

Reconstruction grounds the latent in images, from masked image and video modeling to recurrent world models [27, 28, 37, 68]. Prediction learns temporal structure through feature prediction or latent transitions [6, 7, 53, 56, 77]. 

Conditioning makes external inputs explicit, as in action-conditioned simulators and conditional diffusion world mod-els [1, 2, 11, 19, 70, 74]. Closest to us, video diffusion has been adapted to time-lapse microscopy at the field-of-view level rather than per cell [32]. In all of them the transition is a network, so the spread of sampled futures is a design choice rather than a tested quantity, and a rollout ends by a learned continue flag, if at all, rather than by an event with checkable timing. MitoWorld keeps the state, transition, ter-mination, and renderer of latent world models, but makes the transition an explicit reference-preserving operator (§3.3) and the termination a division hazard (§3.4). 

Structured stochastic dynamics and division timing. Acell-cycle forecast must describe how the cell evolves when it enters division. For the first problem, neural latent transitions offer flexible predictors [26, 56, 76], but repeated prediction can compound errors [4]. Learning nonlinear coordinates in which the dynamics are approximately linear makes this propagation easier to analyze [5, 43, 50, 64, 71]. In Gaussian-regularized coordinates [38, 44], Ornstein–Uhlenbeck transi-tion attenuates existing deviations, and preserving the Gaus-sian reference ties its noise covariance to the mean opera-tor [34, 49, 59, 69]. MitoWorld learns a gene-conditioned transition of this form from same-cell trajectories and evalu-ates per-cell prediction error over long rollouts (§4.4). Divi-sion ends the trajectory, making its timing a time-to-event problem. In discrete-time survival analysis, the hazard is the probability of an event at a given step conditional on its not having occurred earlier [20, 62]. # 3. Method

3.6. Learning the space, then the motion 

We first learn the latent representation through reconstruction and Gaussian regularization. We then freeze the encoder and learn the motion and rendering models in fixed latent coordinates. The objectives for these two stages are: 

Lspace = Lreconst + λshape Lshape ,

Lmotion = Lcoupling + λdiff Ldiff . (13) Here λshape and λdiff represent the weight of Lshape and Ldiff ,respectively. We fit the coupling independently of the hazard using latent pairs encoded from observed frames of the same cell. Once the coupling is trained, we keep the encoder and coupling fixed and fit the division hazard with Lh using both observed and rollout states (§3.4). Delaying hazard fitting avoids exposing it to unreliable predictions from early coupling training, while rollout states expose it to the model-generated inputs it will encounter at inference. At inference, we initialize the latent state as zt = Eψ (xt).At each step, we sample a trigger from the hazard. If no event is sampled, the ordinary transition (Eq. (4)) advances the state. Upon a trigger, the two terminal transitions (Eq. (11)) generate the final states in sequence. The diffusion renderer converts the sampled states into images, and the rollout ends after the composite frame. Repeating this procedure from the same observation yields joint samples of future image trajectories and division times for the same cell. 

# 4. Experiments 

We first describe the experimental setup(§4.1) and then com-pare MitoWorld with strong baselines on single-cell forecast-ing performance and further complement this evaluation with qualitative analyses in image and latent space (§4.2). After that, we ablate paired supervision and operator structure, as-sess calibration and rollout stability, and test the contribution of hazard conditioning to division timing in §4.3–4.5 and close with failure case analysis (§4.6).

Our subset comprises 17 gene knockdown conditions and one negative control. After excluding censored tracks, we retain 1,508 complete single-cell cycles with recorded birth and division events, partitioned at the track level into 1,183 training and 325 test trajectories. For each tracked cell, we rotate the image to align the segmentation mask’s major axis vertically and set pixels outside the mask to zero. We center the mask’s bounding-box crop on a ( 96 × 96 ) grayscale canvas, discard-ing the oversized crops.

4.2. Predicting individual cellular futures 

Appearance and individual correspondence. We first assess whether the model predicts individual cellular futures and improves image fidelity and sequence-level temporal realism while preserving correspondence to the same cell. In Tab. 1, MitoWorld achieves an overall FID of 35.54 and JEDi of 2.04, improving on the strongest respective base-lines (54.36 and 3.75) by 34.6% and 45.6%. Image-feature retrieval reaches 41.87%, compared with 37.33% for the strongest baseline. This is consistent with explicit stochas-tic dynamics constraining evolution across time and same-cell paired supervision learning the cross-time dependence needed for individual forecasting (§3.3 and Prop. 2). In addition, for interpretable cellular-feature evaluation, we follow the 17-feature taxonomy of LIVECell [22]: size 

(area, perimeter, Feret’s diameter), intensity (mean, mini-mum, maximum, fifth and 95th percentiles), intensity distri-bution (skewness, kurtosis, normalized weighted centroid), 

texture (intensity standard deviation), and shape (eccentric-ity, roundness, circularity, solidity, aspect ratio). MitoWorld achieves the lowest category-averaged marginal W1 distance in all five biological categories. Together, these results show that MitoWorld not only generates temporally evolving cel-lular trajectories but also captures the morphological feature distributions of future cells. 

Image- and latent-level trajectories. In Fig. 3a, we com-pare observed snapshots with predictions against two strong baselines from Tab. 1, IMPA and HI, at corresponding stages of the cell cycle. IMPA captures size changes but rounds the cell excessively, whereas HI yields smoother shape transi-tions with little of the observed growth. MitoWorld captures changes in both size and shape, consistent with its lowest distances on both metrics in Tab. 1, and continues through chromatin condensation to two-daughter formation. Panel (b) visualizes state evolution on a fixed UMAP. Before division, the observed and MitoWorld trajectories progress through similar regions, while HI follows a broader, less ordered path through the central region. During the hazard-triggered terminal transitions, MitoWorld reproduces the observed late return toward regions of smaller nuclear area, consistent with chromatin condensation before division. These examples complement Fig. 3a by illustrating how the model organizes individual futures across both gradual progression and abrupt division events. Figure 3c shows that MitoWorld captures gene-dependent differences in cell-cycle length distributions. Together, these results further support MitoWorld’s ability to forecast both how a cell evolves and when it divides.

4.5. Predicting division events 

Having evaluated cellular state evolution, we now examine division timing. Building on the cycle-length distributions in Fig. 3c, we assess how state, age, and gene conditioning con-tribute to the hazard model (Eq. (8)). The full hazard yields an average KS distance of 0.2229 across the 18 conditions. Even samples drawn from the same distribution can differ when only a few cells are available per condition. To esti-8mate the size of this difference, we drew 200 simulated test sets from the model’s predicted cycle lengths, matching the actual number of test cells in each condition. The resulting average KS distance was 0.2234, close to the 0.2229 mea-sured on the real test set. We then ablate each conditioning factor using Brier score, per-condition KS, and terminal FID (Tab. 6). Removing age increases KS from 0.2229 to 0.4743, identifying age as the main timing signal. Removing state or gene conditioning also worsens all three metrics, support-ing their complementary roles in adjusting age-dependent division propensity. The full hazard also yields the low-est terminal FID, indicating closer agreement in terminal appearance distributions.

# 5. Conclusion 

MitoWorld encodes an observed cell in a Gaussian-regularized latent space and learns an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck transition from same-cell pairs. This linear-Gaussian transi-tion supports stable long-horizon rollouts, a division hazard determines when the cell enters division, and a diffusion renderer maps predicted states to microscopy images. On held-out MitoCheck tracks, MitoWorld reduces overall FID by 34.6% and JEDi by 45.6% relative to the strongest respec-tive baselines. We learn cell dynamics from paired obser-vations and evaluate on known knockdowns in one dataset and cell line. Future work includes improving generalization both in rare single-cell case and unseen gene conditions, and adapting the dynamics to unpaired snapshots.
