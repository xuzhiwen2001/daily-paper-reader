Abstract 

Predicting the effects of individual and combined perturbations is central to identifying context-specific vulnerabilities and designing targeted interventions. Yet learning gene representations that in-tegrate functional relationships with cellular state and transfer across perturbation tasks remains chal-lenging. Here, we present E MBER , a gene-centric foundation model that learns context-dependent functional representations of genes. E MBER combines interaction-guided contrastive learning with masked expression reconstruction during pretraining on a single-cell corpus curated for diversity and balanced sampling across cell types and experimental batches. E MBER decouples gene representa-tion learning from task-specific prediction through a composable embedding framework: the pre-trained encoder remains frozen, while lightweight probes combine contextualized gene embeddings to predict individual and combined perturbation effects. The same pretrained representations support single-gene perturbation-response prediction, digenic interaction prediction, and multi-target drug-response prediction. E MBER improves cross-context perturbation-response prediction and genetic-interaction ranking relative to evaluated baselines. EMBER also prioritized SCFD1 , a mediator of autophagosome–lysosome fusion, as a candidate dependency during mTOR inhibition. Together, these results establish contextualized gene embeddings as reusable building blocks for modeling genetic and pharmacological perturbations, supporting the prioritization of genetic interactions and context-specific interventions for experimental testing.

## Introduction 

Gene functions are multifaceted, pleiotropic, and shaped by cellular state [1, 2]. Despite sharing essen-tially the same genome, cells within an individual exhibit distinct gene expression patterns, regulatory programs, and pathway activities across cellular states [3, 4]. As a result, gene identity alone is insuffi-cient to determine the consequences of a perturbation. This context dependence is especially important in combinatorial genetic settings, where accurate prediction requires accounting for both the individual effects of perturbed genes and their context-dependent interactions [5, 6]. Predicting these effects at scale calls for gene representations that encode context-specific functional relationships and serve as reusable building blocks for modeling individual and combined perturbations. Existing approaches only partially address this challenge. Representations derived from protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks [7] encode molecular relationships but are often static across contexts and limited in coverage, restricting their ability to represent context-specific gene function. Contextu-alized PPI methods such as PINNACLE [8] introduce cell-type-specific graph structure, but their repre-sentations are defined over the proteins and annotated cell types included in the constructed networks. Gene representations derived from text-embedding large language models [9, 10] have performed well on prior benchmarks [11, 12], but are typically static and limited by the incompleteness of the underlying knowledge sources. Single-cell foundation models (scFMs) [13–19] offer a complementary, data-driven route to learning representations from diverse cellular states. Although models such as Geneformer [13] and scGPT [14] learn gene-level features, many prominent applications emphasize cell-level tasks, in-cluding cell-type annotation, batch integration, and cell-state representation [20, 21]. However, strong performance on cell-level tasks does not necessarily translate into gene representations that general-ize across gene-level prediction tasks. Evaluations have reported limitations in the downstream per-formance of representations extracted from scFMs [12, 20–24], and many applications rely on encoder fine-tuning [14, 17, 19, 25] or substantial task-specific adaptation [14, 15, 17]. These limitations motivate reusable gene representations that jointly capture functional relationships and cellular state. Dedicated perturbation prediction models have made progress, but limited experimental coverage makes generalization to unseen perturbations and cellular contexts challenging, particularly for multi-gene effects. STATE [26] and STACK [27] address cross-context prediction using perturbation responses observed in other contexts, while GEARS [28] predicts unseen perturbations and their combinations within a given cellular context. More recently, PIE [29] has demonstrated joint generalization to unseen perturbations and cellular contexts for transcriptional-response prediction. Prior work has shown that simple predictors, including linear models using gene embeddings, can outperform dedicated perturba-tion models [12, 22]. These findings motivate a new focus on context-dependent gene representations that can be reused across tasks and composed to predict individual and combined perturbation effects across distinct experimental readouts without encoder retraining. Here we present E MBER , a gene-centric foundation model that combines interaction-guided con-trastive learning with masked expression reconstruction to learn context-dependent functional represen-tations of genes. E MBER makes the gene, conditioned on cellular state, the primary unit of represen-tation and downstream reuse. Its composable embedding framework separates representation learning from task-specific prediction: the pretrained encoder remains frozen, while lightweight downstream pre-dictors combine gene embeddings to model perturbation responses and interactions involving individual genes and gene sets. ## Results 

Overview of E MBER 

EMBER is a gene-centric foundation model that provides reusable gene representations from single-cell transcriptomic data ( Fig. 1a ). Here, cellular context refers to the cellular state captured by single-cell transcriptomic measurements. By conditioning each gene representation on this state, E MBER is designed to capture context-specific functional relationships for reuse across downstream tasks. To capture diverse cellular contexts, we curated a pretraining corpus from CellxGene [30], priori-tizing diversity across cell types and experimental batches while maintaining approximately balanced sampling across cell types. This design was motivated by evidence that pretraining data composition, rather than cell count alone, influences downstream performance [31–33]. The resulting CellxGene corpus contains 250,283 cells balanced across 345 cell types, with each primary cell type contributing between 255 and 1,048 cells ( Fig. 1c ). An additional 2,000 control cells from each of K562, Jurkat, HepG2, and RPE1 were also included in pretraining of the main model ( Methods ). EMBER is pretrained on this corpus using two complementary objectives. Cell subgraph contrastive learning (CSCL) uses an annotated protein-protein interaction (PPI) network [7] to define contrastive training pairs within each cell. Positive pairs are annotated PPI edges between expressed genes in the cell’s tokenized context, contrasted against sampled pairs without annotated interactions ( Fig. 1c ). Because annotated PPIs are incomplete [34], this information is used as a contrastive training objec-tive [35] rather than a fixed architectural constraint. In parallel, masked gene-expression reconstruction encourages the model to capture contextual expression relationships by reconstructing masked expres-sion values from the remaining genes in the same cellular state. Joint training integrates an expression-conditioned interaction prior with transcriptional information in a shared representation space. Details of model pretraining are described in Methods .After pretraining, the encoder is frozen. For downstream prediction, each gene’s embeddings are averaged across control cells within the relevant context. Each downstream task trains only a lightweight probe that composes frozen gene embeddings: a perturbed gene with a readout gene (E MBER -PERT), two co-perturbed genes (E MBER -GI), or a drug’s target-gene set with a readout gene (E MBER -DRUG) (Fig. 1b ; Methods). In each task, we compare matched-context embeddings against static gene-identity embeddings and mismatched-context variants to assess the predictive utility of the encoder outputs and their alignment with the target context. We next asked whether masked reconstruction alone captures information associated with per-turbation responsiveness . We defined a gene’s perturbation responsiveness as the fraction of as-sayed genetic perturbations that induced its differential expression in a given context using Perturb-seq data [37] ( Fig. S1d and Supplementary Information ). For this analysis, we used a reconstruction-only checkpoint pretrained on 100 cell types excluding lineages related to the four Perturb-seq cell lines (Supplementary Information ). We found that the L2 distance between a gene’s embedding and the centroid of gene embeddings in a given cell line correlated positively with perturbation responsiveness ,even after adjusting for expression magnitude, expression variance, and tokenization sampling frequency (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Information ). Correlations were higher for matched than mismatched cell-line contexts (Spearman ρ = 0.522 vs. 0.298; p = 1.7×10 −4, two-sided paired t-test across four cell lines). We next examined whether this association depended on the diversity of the pretraining data. Holding the number of training cells fixed at 176,000, we pretrained reconstruction-only models on four nested subsets comprising 1, 10, 50, and 100 cell types ( Supplementary Information ). Across these four tiers, the mean correlation between embedding centroid distance and experimentally measured pertur-bation responsiveness , adjusted for the same covariates, increased monotonically with cell-type diversity (Spearman ρ = 1.00 across tiers) ( Fig. 2g ). Together, these analyses distinguish complementary contri-butions of the pretraining objectives: CSCL promotes functional neighborhood organization, whereas reconstruction captures transcriptional information associated with perturbation responsiveness . Joint training integrates these complementary signals into context-dependent gene representations that we next evaluate directly in perturbation prediction. 6

EMBER predicts context-specific perturbation responses 

Predicting transcriptional responses to genetic perturbations is central to understanding cell-state-specific gene regulation [38, 39]. We used the frozen E MBER model to embed the perturbed and readout genes in the relevant control-cell context, composed the ordered pair with a lightweight probe, and predicted whether the readout gene was differentially expressed ( Methods ). To reduce predictive shortcuts based on average transcriptional response [40, 41], we used a response-aware negative-sampling strategy that matches positives and negatives by readout gene across perturba-tions ( Methods ). We evaluated three generalization regimes involving transfer to held-out perturbations (Fig. 3c and Fig. S2c ), transfer to held-out cellular contexts ( Fig. 3b and Fig. S2b ), and transfer to held-out perturbations and contexts ( Fig. 3a and Fig. S2a ). Here, a held-out cellular context refers to a cell line excluded from probe training; its control cells were available for pretraining and embedding extraction, and a separate subset of its perturbation data was used for validation ( Methods ). We com-pared matched-context E MBER embeddings with static gene-identity embeddings and embeddings from a mismatched cell-type context. Additional baselines included PPI- and text-derived representations, scFM embeddings including scGPT [14] and Geneformer [13], sequence-derived embeddings including ESM2 [42], and perturbation models GEARS [28] and STATE [26]. All embedding-based methods were evaluated using the same prediction framework as E MBER (Methods ). EMBER achieved the highest mean AUPRC and AUROC across both cross-context regimes, reach-ing an average AUPRC of 0.425 for the joint cell-line and perturbation holdout and 0.486 for held-out cell lines with seen perturbations across all four cell lines ( Fig. 3a,b and Fig. S2 ). In the joint holdout, matched-context embeddings outperformed both the static (AUPRC 0.387) and mismatched-context (AUPRC 0.385) variants ( Fig. 3a ), supporting the predictive value of target-context information. STRING (GNN) embeddings were among the strongest external baselines in these regimes (AUPRC 0.383 for the joint holdout and 0.424 for cell-line-only holdout), consistent with earlier benchmark-ing [12]. By contrast, STRING (Direct) and STRING (Hop) performed near random ( Fig. S2 ). When only the cellular context was held out, scGPT-Pan-Cancer [14] was the strongest scFM baseline (AUPRC 0.441), and the Context Mean predictor [26] was competitive with several scFM embeddings (AUPRC 0.413) ( Fig. 3b ). STATE [26] performed worse than E MBER (AUPRC 0.389), particularly in the Ju-rkat cell line ( Fig. S2 ). These comparisons assess recovery of differentially expressed genes under the specified splits rather than overall transcriptomic prediction accuracy. For held-out perturbations within the same cell line, E MBER ’s advantage was smaller (AUPRC 0.494 versus 0.481 for STRING (GNN)). EMBER remained competitive with STRING (GNN) across all four cell lines ( Fig. S2 ). The strength of network-derived embeddings in this setting agrees with previous evaluations [12, 43]. We next evaluated performance under experimental prioritization budgets by using the full set of el-igible negative readout genes rather than the 1:2 positive-to-negative ratio used above. Across budgets of 10 to 100 predicted genes per perturbation, E MBER maintained precision above the random baseline in all three regions. Recall increased with larger budgets ( Fig. 3d ). Holding out both context and per-turbation reduced precision and recall relative to either holdout alone. We also assessed dependence on prior knowledge by stratifying performance by STRING connectivity. Performance generally increased with the number of annotated PPI edges. For held-out perturbations within the same context, E MBER 

and STRING (GNN) performed comparably for genes with many PPI edges, whereas E MBER showed larger gains for genes with fewer PPI edges ( Fig. 3e ). These results indicate that E MBER ’s advantage extends to sparsely annotated genes. 7

EMBER improves genetic interaction prediction

In digenic interactions, two-gene perturbations produce effects that differ from expectations based on the corresponding single-gene perturbations [6, 44]. Although the HAP1 comparison did not reach statistical significance, the direction of the effect was consistent with that observed in EPP2 cells. These observations support prioritizing SCFD1

E MBER ranks SCFD1 and mTOR much higher (top 0.81%) compared to STRING (GNN) (top 1.90%) and its static counterpart (top 12.2%). We then asked whether E MBER -GI could help prioritize candidate trigenic interactions in human cells ( Fig. 4d ). Trigenic interactions capture higher-order effects beyond the corresponding single-gene and pairwise perturbations [55, 56]. Although E MBER -GI scores pairwise interactions, trigenic effects frequently modify existing digenic interactions [55], and summed digenic scores correlated with trigenic scores in yeast (Pearson r = 0 .507 ; Spearman ρ = 0 .459 ; Fig. S3c ). These observations motivated using pairwise interaction structure to prioritize trigenic candidates rather than directly predict higher-order effects. EMBER enhances drug-specific transcriptional response prediction 

Many drugs engage multiple protein targets, whose effects vary across cellular contexts [66–68]. We therefore asked whether E MBER embeddings could represent drugs as sets of contextualized target genes to predict drug-induced transcriptional responses. For each drug, we obtained known target genes from DrugBank [69] and computed matched E MBER embeddings in the relevant cellular context. E MBER -DRUG pools these embeddings and target-specific mechanism-of-action (MoA) annotations using an order-invariant probe and combines the resulting drug representation with a readout-gene embedding to predict its response ( Fig. S5 and Methods ). We evaluated generalization to unseen drugs on LINCS L1000 [70] and Tahoe-100M using drug-level holdouts within each cell line, keeping drugs with identical annotated target sets in the same split and comparing against alternative gene and drug-structure representations and the drug-response trans-former model XPert [71]. A baseline assigning every held-out drug the mean response across training drugs outperformed all learned models under control-reference Pearson correlation ( Supplementary In-formation & Table S7 ), consistent with reports that this metric can reward shared responses [40, 41]. To assess drug-specific variation, we used perturbed-reference Pearson correlation as the primary metric, removing each gene’s mean response across training drugs from both predicted and observed profiles before computing correlation [40] ( Methods ). EMBER -DRUG achieved the best average rank across the evaluated cell lines in both LINCS L1000 and Tahoe-100M ( Fig. 5a,b and Fig. S5c,d ). Average perturbed-reference Pearson correlations were approximately 0.15 and 0.05, respectively ( Table S7 ). Contextualized E MBER embeddings also outper-formed the context-free static gene-identity variant, supporting the predictive utility of the contextualized encoder outputs for this task. Matched-context embeddings also ranked above the mismatched-context variant on average in both datasets ( Fig. S5c,d ). Replicate agreement under the same metric was lower in Tahoe-100M (r=0.15) than in LINCS L1000 (r=0.51), providing a reference for measurement repro-ducibility rather than an established prediction ceiling. We next asked whether the transcriptional responses predicted by E MBER -DRUG could support can-didate prioritization by disease-signature reversal ( Fig. 5c ). We used E MBER -DRUG to predict tran-scriptional responses for 1,277 drugs and rank compounds by predicted reversal of the lung adenocar-cinoma expression signature in A549 cells ( Fig. 5c ). ## Discussion 

In this work, we developed E MBER , a contextualized gene foundation model that addresses the challenge of learning gene representations that integrate functional relationships with cellular context. Frozen EMBER embeddings composed with downstream probes improve cross-context perturbation-response prediction and genetic-interaction ranking, while also supporting drug-response modeling without task-specific encoder retraining. E MBER also nominated SCFD1 as a candidate dependency during mTOR 

inhibition, consistent with retrospective evidence from CRISPR screens. These results support a gene-centric pretraining strategy in which functional knowledge and cellular state are integrated into represen-tations that can be reused and composed across biological tasks. The ablations suggest complementary contributions from the interaction prior and masked reconstruction, while the fixed-cell-count compar-isons highlight the importance of cellular diversity during pretraining. Together, these findings motivate attention to pretraining objectives and data composition alongside increases in corpus size. EMBER has several limitations. First, it currently contextualizes representations from transcrip-tomic measurements alone. Transcription-factor activity, chromatin accessibility, protein abundance, post-translational regulation and spatial microenvironment can all shape gene function without being fully reflected in gene-expression measurements. Integrating genotype information with epigenomic, proteomic, and spatial measurements could broaden the definition of cellular context and distinguish regulatory states that are not resolved by transcriptomics alone. E MBER also depends on an incomplete, unevenly annotated interaction prior. Although its gains extend to sparsely annotated genes, this does not establish independence from prior-knowledge biases. Evaluating alternative interaction priors and transfer to underrepresented genes and cellular states will be important for assessing the robustness of the learned representations. Second, performance gains remain task- and metric-dependent, and substantial room remains for improving predictive performance. For perturbation prediction, the main benchmark concerns essential-gene CRISPR interference in cultured cell lines and evaluates differential-expression responses rather than complete transcriptomic profiles. Target-context perturbations also inform validation, so cross-context evaluation is not entirely target-label-free. Testing primary cells, broader perturbation classes, and contexts excluded from both pretraining and model selection would better establish generalizability. For drug perturbations, the benchmarks assess held-out drugs within each cell line, rather than transfer to unseen cell types, and depend on available target and mechanism-of-action annotations. Measure-ment variability and limited perturbation coverage further constrain prediction. Additional replicated measurements and calibrated uncertainty estimates could help identify predictions reliable enough for experimental prioritization. Third, compositional prediction does not itself establish higher-order genetic mechanisms. The tri-13

Predicted disease-signature reversal and its retrospective association with PRISM viability support compound prioritization rather than estab-lishing therapeutic efficacy. Prospective validation using data independent of candidate selection, with appropriate single- and pairwise-perturbation controls, is needed to test the proposed interactions and their context specificity. Such experiments could also provide training data for models that directly learn higher-order effects rather than relying on pairwise surrogates. Our findings also highlight an important direction for single-cell foundation models. Many current evaluations emphasize cell-level tasks, such as cell-type classification, clustering, batch integration, and cell-state representation. Although these tasks are important, they do not directly test whether a model has learned useful representations of genes. By centering context-dependent gene embeddings as the primary learned object, E MBER provides a practical framework for gene-focused prediction without re-quiring encoder fine-tuning for each downstream task. Evaluating frozen representations across distinct tasks offers a complementary test of whether pretraining captures transferable functional information. More broadly, E MBER provides a gene-centric interface between observational single-cell data and per-turbation experiments. Coupling reusable representations with prospective validation could help priori-tize experiments that resolve context-dependent gene function and uncover genetic and pharmacological vulnerabilities. 14

Contextual embeddings and probes 

The pretrained encoder was frozen for every downstream task. To embed gene g in context T , we ran unperturbed control cells of T through the encoder and averaged the output zg over cells to give zTg . The static variant used the learned gene-identity embedding Egene (g) taken before the transformer, which carries no cellular context. The mismatched-context variant replaced zTg with the embedding of the same gene in a CellxGene cell type whose expression profile had a Pearson correlation of approximately 0.5 with the target cell line ( Supplementary Information and Table S4). Probes composed ordered gene pairs by concatenation followed by an MLP, and unordered gene sets by a sum-pooled set encoder [80]. Only probe weights were trained ( Table S2). 

Embedding space analyses 

All analyses used frozen embeddings and are detailed in the Supplementary Information . Recovery of Gene Ontology (GO) structure was scored by the adjusted Rand index (ARI) and normalized mutual information (NMI) after clustering gene embeddings within 33 balanced GO parent terms in each of HepG2, Jurkat, K562 and RPE1 ( Table S3). Lineage recovery was scored by ARI and NMI on per-cell embeddings of 54 genes (18 lineage markers, 18 transcription factors and 18 housekeeping genes) across 2,816 cells from 11 lineages.

Evaluation Predictions were adjusted for gene-wise mean effects to obtain pair-specific residual scores, 

Rij = Sij − ri − rj + ¯ r, ¯r = mean i ri, (4) where Sij is the predicted score and ri is the mean predicted score across pairs containing gene i. Genome-wide imputation For imputation, we trained one E MBER -GI model on signed qGI with an 85/15 split of query genes, with library genes shared, and selected it by validation Spearman correlation. We scored all ordered pairs of the 16,408 library genes to obtain a matrix M and symmetrized it as 

S = 12 (M + M ⊤), leaving the diagonal undefined. Residuals Rij were computed from S with ri defined as the row mean of gene i.

Synthetic lethality benchmark We compared the residuals with synthetic-lethal pairs from Syn-LethDB [84], collapsed to unique unordered pairs with both genes in the imputed map and restricted to pairs with experimental evidence. Pairs were scored by −Rij . AUROC was computed against an equal number of degree-matched negative pairs, overall and stratified by evidence type and cell line. 

Trigenic candidate prioritization We mapped the top 5% of DANGO-predicted yeast triplets [56] to human genes using many-to-many InParanoid orthology [85], with a shared-domain fallback for genes lacking direct orthologs. Retaining the top 15% by summed pair scores left 5,627,852 triplets. We ranked these by the sum of the three E MBER -GI residuals Rij and retained the top 10%, which gave 446,861 candidate human trigenic interactions. Triplets containing genes that could not be transferred were dropped, so the retained sets are smaller than the nominal fractions. Pathway enrichment We tested the 446,861 prioritized triplets against the background of all 46,035,705 mapped triplets. We used Reactome [58] and KEGG [59] pathways of 3 to 500 genes and CORUM complexes [57], and counted a triplet as a hit only when all three genes belonged to the gene set. Enrichment was the observed hit count divided by its expectation under the background rate, with one-sided hypergeometric tests and Benjamini-Hochberg correction (FDR < 0.05 ). An empirical null came from 100 random foreground sets of equal size drawn from the same background. 

DepMap co-loss analysis We tested prioritized triplets using DepMap 24Q4 Public copy-number and CRISPR gene-effect data [62] from 1,178 cell lines. Each triplet yielded up to three frames, with one gene as the CRISPR readout and the other two as the co-lost pair. Eligible frames required genes on distinct chromosome arms, sufficient representation of all four loss groups and co-loss across at least two tissue lineages. We centered gene-effect values within tissue lineages and residualized them against readout copy number and genome-wide loss burden, then fitted the two loss indicators and their interac-tion to these residualized outcomes. A frame was considered significant when the interaction coefficient was negative at Benjamini-Hochberg FDR < 0.20 across all tested frames, and the number of significant frames was compared with 128 permutations of the loss-matrix rows ( Supplementary Information ).

Drug identifiers were matched by BRD core, and non-missing viability AUC values were aggregated by their median per drug. Hits were defined as drugs with median viability AUC strictly below the 20th percentile across all PRISM-measured A549 drugs, calculated before intersecting with the model predictions. The intersec-tion contained 399 drugs, including 74 hits; drugs without viability measurements were excluded. ROC curves and AUROC were calculated using the negated predicted CMap connectivity score, so stronger predicted disease reversal corresponded to higher scores. 21
