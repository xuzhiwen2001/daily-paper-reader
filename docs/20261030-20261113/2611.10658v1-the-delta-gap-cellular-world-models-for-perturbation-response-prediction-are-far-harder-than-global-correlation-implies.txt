# The Delta Gap: Cellular World Models for Perturbation Response Prediction Are Far Harder Than Global Correlation Implies 

Junhui Gao Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, China 

jhgao68@163.com

Abstract 

Cellular world models aim to predict how cells respond to genetic perturbations, analogous to world models in reinforcement learning. Perturbation response models are evaluated almost exclusively by global Pearson correlation between predicted and observed transcriptomes. We show this metric is dominated by thousands of non-responsive genes and severely inflates apparent performance. On the Replogle K562 screen (509 unseen perturbations, 5000 genes), GEARS achieves global Pearson 0.977, but its delta Pearson is only 0.076. A trivial baseline predicting mean training delta achieves 0.267 — 3.5x better than GEARS. Our primary contribution is methodological: we identify and quantify this delta gap as a systematic evaluation artifact, and propose delta-space evaluation as the primary benchmark. We provide two post-hoc corrections (RWR on co-expression graphs and kNN matching) and show that 2.8x of the remaining gap lies in perturbation representation geometry, not decoder capacity. We further show the gap is partially addressable end-to-end: delta-aligned training (lambda = 3.0) raises delta Pearson to 0.179 on Replogle (2.35x over GEARS) and surpasses the trivial baseline on direction accuracy (0.680 vs 0.567) and gene recovery (1.52 vs 1.19); combined with kNN it reaches 0.217. An amplitude-calibration variant (eta = 0.3) further raises gene recovery to 2.16 while reaching 0.218. Critically, these representations transfer zero-shot across cell lines: from Replogle K562 to unseen Norman A549, the delta-aligned model reaches 0.328 on 741 shared genes, 4.7x a trivial baseline and 2.1x standard GEARS. This is the first systematic quantification of the delta gap, and the first diagnostic framework localizing the residual gap to perturbation representation geometry via an oracle upper bound. Keywords: cellular world model; virtual cell; perturbation response prediction; single-cell transcriptomics; gene perturbation; delta gap; evaluation metrics; zero-shot transfer

# 1 Introduction 

Perturbation-seq technologies measure the transcriptional response to gene perturbations at single-cell resolution [1,2]. A central computational goal is to build a cellular world model: given a perturbation (an ’action’), predict the resulting cell state (’next observation’). This mirrors world models in 2reinforcement learning, where an agent learns environment dynamics to plan without real interaction [3,4]. The key insight from Ha & Schmidhuber [3] is that a world model must capture the dynamics of the environment, not just memorize observations. LeCun [5] further argues that predictive world models are essential for autonomous machine intelligence. Recent models including GEARS [6], flow matching methods such as PRiMeFlow [7] and scDFM [8], and optimal transport approaches [9] report strong unseen-perturbation Pearson correlations of 0.85-0.98 on standard benchmarks. However, world models are only useful if they capture the causal dynamics of the environment - in this case, how perturbations actually change gene expression. A world model that merely memorizes the default cell state adds no predictive value for planning. We show that current cellular world models appear accurate only because their evaluation metric is dominated by unchanged genes, not by perturbation-induced dynamics. These numbers are computed over the entire genome. However, a typical CRISPR perturbation alters only tens to hundreds of genes out of thousands measured. The remaining genes are unchanged, and both predicted and observed vectors match the control baseline on these dimensions. Global correlation is therefore dominated by the control state, not by the perturbation-specific signal. We demonstrate that this inflation is substantial and consistent across datasets. On Replogle K562 essential data [1], GEARS achieves global Pearson 0.977 on unseen single perturbations, but when evaluated on the perturbation-induced delta (predicted minus control vs observed minus control), Pearson drops to 0.076. On Norman A549 [2], global Pearson 0.877 corresponds to delta Pearson 0.548. Most strikingly, a trivial baseline that predicts the same mean training delta for every test perturbation achieves delta Pearson 0.267 on Replogle-3.5x higher than GEARS-indicating that GEARS’s deep network learns almost no perturbation-specific information beyond reconstructing the control transcriptome. We introduce two post-hoc corrections that require no retraining: (a) random walk with restart (RWR) on a control-derived co-expression graph, which propagates the model’s one-step delta along co-expressed gene neighborhoods (+0.027 delta Pearson); and (b) k-nearest neighbor matching on predicted perturbation profiles, which retrieves the true delta of the most similar training perturbations based on the model’s own predictions (+0.135 delta Pearson, reaching 0.211). We show that kNN matching substantially outperforms RWR, but both remain below the trivial LinearPerturb baseline. Finally, we use oracle kNN (using true test deltas to find neighbors) as an upper bound (0.592 delta Pearson). The 2.8x gap between oracle and predicted-delta kNN quantifies how much GEARS’s perturbation representations need to improve. This suggests that improving the latent space geometry-which perturbations are close to which-is more important than improving the output decoder. 3Figure 3: Left: Global vs delta Pearson on Replogle and Norman on a log scale, showing the 13x (Replogle) and 1.6x (Norman) gaps between global and delta performance. Right: RWR alpha sweep on Replogle; the best restart probability is alpha = 0.7. 

# 2 Related Work 

GEARS [6] uses a graph neural network over a gene co-expression graph to predict perturbation responses, and is the standard baseline. Earlier GRN-based methods include CellOracle [10], which simulates TF perturbation effects along an inferred gene regulatory network, and GraphVCI [11], which uses graph-based variational inference to capture regulatory variation. Recent 2026 works including PRiMeFlow [7] and scDFM [8] use conditional flow matching to model control-to-perturbation transitions, while OCOO-T [9] uses optimal transport transitions. D-squared-R-squared [12] performs gene-sequential generation guided by a regulatory network. Chreode [13] models temporal dynamics on developmental trajectories. Foundation models for single-cell transcriptomics include scGPT [14], scFoundation [15], GeneCom-pass [16], and CellFM [17]. These models are pretrained on millions of cells and fine-tuned for perturbation prediction, with scGPT [18] showing zero-shot transfer capability across perturbation types. The original Perturb-seq protocol was introduced by Dixit et al. [19] and Adamson et al. [20], establishing the CRISPRi + scRNA-seq paradigm that modern perturbation prediction models build upon. Several recent works have raised concerns about evaluation metrics for perturbation prediction. AUPRC-based evaluation [21,22] argues that R2 and global Pearson fail to capture differentially expressed (DE) genes. Diversity-by-design [23] proposes weighted delta R2 and shows that shared reference shifts inflate global performance. D2R2 [12] already reports Pearson delta as a supplementary metric. A family of recent perturbation models has gone further and adopted delta-space correlation as a primary training or evaluation objective: PerturbDiff proposes Pearson Delta Correlation [24], HarmonyCell reports DeltaPCC as its headline metric [25], Lingshu-Cell evaluates Pearson delta alongside distributional metrics [26], SCALE and P2P optimize and report effect-focused correlation [27,28], and VCLMU reports a delta-PCC / delta-PCC@20 joint plane [29]. The VCBench benchmark likewise argues that global PCC lacks discriminative power and proposes effect-level metrics across three generalization scenarios [30]. Plausibility-is-not-prediction shows that LLM-based perturbation reasoning underperforms simple gene-frequency baselines [31]. Locked evaluation reveals transfer 4failure under distribution shift [32]. Relative to these delta-space metrics, this paper makes three additional contributions. First, we quantify the magnitude of the delta gap (13x on Replogle, 1.6x on Norman) under a standard training protocol that optimizes no delta-space objective, showing how large the gap can be when a model is trained on global expression reconstruction alone. Second, we decompose the gap: a trivial baseline (predicting the mean training delta) outperforms GEARS by 3.5x on delta Pearson, and gene-level stratification shows the global score is driven by absolute-expression predictability. Third, we show the gap is partially addressable: RWR and kNN post-hoc corrections recover a substantial fraction, and an oracle upper bound (0.592) localizes the residual gap to perturbation representation geometry. Finally, the metric-definition analysis in Sec. 4.3 shows that delta-space scores are highly sensitive to how the delta is defined and whether the training objective aligns with it - which explains why recent delta-aligned models report much higher delta correlations than the 0.076 we measure for GEARS. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic quantification of the delta gap and the first localization of the residual gap to representation geometry via an oracle upper bound. Our work differs from these prior critiques in three ways. First, we systematically quantify the gap between global and delta-space performance (13x on Replogle, 1.6x on Norman) across two datasets and three model architectures (GEARS, MLP, LinearPerturb). Second, we show that a trivial baseline (predicting the mean training delta for every perturbation) outperforms GEARS by 3.5x on delta Pearson. Third, we introduce two post-hoc corrections (RWR on co-expression graphs and kNN matching on predicted profiles) and use oracle kNN to quantify that 2.8x of the remaining gap lies in perturbation representation geometry, not output decoder capacity. 

# 3 Method 

3.1 Problem formulation 

We frame perturbation response prediction as learning a cellular world model: a function that maps a cellular state s and an action a (a genetic perturbation) to the next state s’ the cell would occupy after the perturbation. Concretely, let C = { c1, ..., cN } be a set of cells, each with gene expression vector xi in R^G where G is the number of genes. Each cell has a perturbation label pi indicating which gene was perturbed. We denote the control expression as xctrl = mean({ xi : pi = control}). For each perturbation p, the mean post-perturbation expression is xp = mean({ xi : pi = p}). In this view the perturbation label p is the action, the control state xctrl is the initial state s, and the post-perturbation expression xp is the next state s’. A perturbation response model f takes as input a perturbation label p and outputs a predicted post-perturbation expression ˆxp = f(p); it is thus a candidate world model. The standard evaluation metric is global Pearson correlation between 

ˆxp and xp over all G genes. The value of a world model lies in whether it reproduces the state transition induced by the action, not in whether it memorizes the default state. We therefore define the perturbation-induced transition, or delta, as the displacement between the perturbed and control states: 

δobs (p) = xp − xctrl , δpred (p) = ˆ xp − xctrl (1) and propose evaluating Pearson( δpred (p), δobs (p)) on this transition as the primary metric (Eq. 1): we score candidate world models by how accurately they reproduce the action-induced transition rather than the absolute state. We also report Pearson on the top-20 most differentially expressed genes (by | δobs |) per perturbation, and direction accuracy: the fraction of genes where sign( δpred ) = 5sign( δobs ). 

3.2 RWR on co-expression graph 

Our RWR approach is related to PageRank [33], which propagates importance scores along a web graph. We construct a gene-gene co-expression graph A from control cells only. For each gene pair (i, j), Aij = corr( xctrl ,i , xctrl ,j ) if |corr| > 0.3, else 0. Each gene keeps at most 20 positive neighbors. We row-normalize A to obtain Anorm .Given the model’s one-step delta δ0 = δpred (p), we propagate for T iterations: 

δ(t+1) = (1 − α) · δ0 + α · Anorm · δ(t), t = 0 , 1, . . . , T − 1 (2) where α = 0.7 is the restart probability and T = 10 (Eq. 2). The final RWR-corrected delta is 

δT . Computational complexity: O(T · |E|) per perturbation, where |E| is the number of edges in the co-expression graph. The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1. From a graph-signal-processing perspective, the closed-form RWR solution in Eq. 2 admits a spectral interpretation: propagation along the co-expression graph acts as a low-pass filter on the gene signal. With the normalized Laplacian L = I - Anorm and its eigen-decomposition, the RWR operator equals the personalized-PageRank operator on the gene graph - a connection made explicit in spectral analyses of PageRank-type dynamics [12] - and any RWR-type correction can therefore be written as a spectral filter (Eq. 3): 

r = Φ diag( g(λ)) Φ⊤s (3) where g is a filter function of the eigenvalues and s the gene signal; GeneGeoFlow adopts the same spectral-filter formalism for virtual-cell perturbation modeling [36]. This equivalence shows RWR is not an ad-hoc heuristic but the canonical low-pass operator on the co-expression graph: the restart probability alpha controls the filter bandwidth, so larger alpha corresponds to stronger low-passing, consistent with the biological prior that perturbation effects are smooth over gene modules.

Metric definition sensitivity. Table 3 explains why recent models report delta-space correlations (0.70-0.75) an order of magnitude higher than the 0.076 we measure for GEARS. Two factors dominate. First, training protocol: SCALE and P2P explicitly align their training objectives with delta-space prediction (an endpoint-transport loss and an effect-centric term with the largest weight, respectively), whereas GEARS is trained on global expression reconstruction with no delta-space term [27,28]. GeneGeoFlow reports Pearson delta 0.90 on Norman under the same delta-aligned protocol, providing cross-dataset confirmation [36]. Second, metric granularity: definitions differ in how the control baseline is subtracted and how gene-condition pairs are pooled. Neither factor weakens our conclusions; on the contrary, the fact that delta-aligned training raises delta Pearson from 0.076 to 0.70-0.75 confirms that the delta gap of GEARS is not an intrinsic difficulty of the problem but a consequence of optimizing the wrong objective. We therefore advocate that delta-space metrics be reported alongside global Pearson (as P2P, VCLMU, and Lingshu-Cell also do [26,28,29]), and that metric definitions be stated explicitly. 11 Two algebraic identities from recent work make the training-protocol explanation precise. First, because the control endpoint is subtracted from both sides, the endpoint prediction error of a transport model equals its delta-space prediction error (Eq. 12): 

∥ˆz1 − z1∥2 = ∥∆ˆ z − ∆z∥2 (12) where the hatted variables are the predicted post-perturbation state and its delta, and the unhatted ones are the observed values. This identity, central to the endpoint-transport objective of SCALE [27], shows that an endpoint loss already is a delta-space loss; the reason SCALE reaches PDCorr 0.751 while GEARS reaches 0.076 is therefore the training signal, not the metric. Second, the population-level delta is a conditional moment rather than a per-cell quantity (Eq. 13): 

E [¯ yv − ¯cv | p, s, d ] = δp,s,d (13) where the bars denote mean expression of the perturbed and control populations, and the subscripted delta is the population effect for perturbation p under condition s and dosage d, following the population-denoising formulation of P2P [28]. This identity clarifies the statistical object that rdelta measures - a group-mean difference - which is exactly the quantity that mean-response predictors (GEARS one-step, LinearPerturb) are trained to reproduce, and the quantity in which they still fail. Figure 5 quantifies the amplitude collapse behind the delta gap. The ECDF of GEARS predicted delta amplitudes is far left of the observed distribution: the model outputs near-zero deltas for the vast majority of gene-condition pairs. LinearPerturb, because it predicts a non-zero training-set average for every condition, produces intermediate amplitudes that overlap the observed range more substantially. This confirms that GEARS does not merely mis-rank perturbations but systematically under-predicts their magnitude. 

4.4 Multi-seed results 

RWR consistently improves delta Pearson by +0.027 (from 0.076 to 0.103) across three seeds with standard deviation 0.004. The improvement is stable and not an artifact of one random split. A paired t-test across the three seeds yields p = 0.009 for all-gene delta Pearson (significant at p <

0.01), with Cohen’s d = 7.42 (very large effect size). The top-20 DE improvement does not reach significance (p = 0.109), likely due to the small number of seeds (n=3) and higher variance in the top-20 subset. Direction accuracy also improves slightly from 0.520 to 0.523. 

4.5 kNN matching results 

kNN matching with k=50 and alpha=0 (pure kNN) achieves 0.211 delta Pearson, a 3.1x improvement over GEARS one-step (0.069) and 2.1x over RWR (0.103). Top-20 DE Pearson reaches 0.280. Increasing k from 1 to 50 steadily improves performance, suggesting that averaging over more similar perturbations reduces noise. Stability across training runs and accuracy on the transition are therefore separable properties of a cellular world model, and benchmarks should measure both.

5.1 Why global Pearson is inflated: theoretical analysis 

We formalize why global Pearson overestimates perturbation prediction accuracy. Consider a perturbation p with G genes. Let SDE be the set of differentially expressed genes (size k), and S be the set of non-responsive genes (size G-k). For genes in SDE , the true delta is approximately zero: 

δobs (g) ≈ 0. For genes in S, δobs (g) is nonzero. The global Pearson correlation between predicted and observed expression is: 

rglobal = cov ( xpred , x obs )

σpred · σobs 

(15) Since xpred = xctrl + δpred and xobs = xctrl + δobs (Eq. 15), and both predicted and observed are centered around xctrl (which dominates for non-responsive genes), the covariance is dominated by the shared control component xctrl .19 More precisely, decompose the covariance (Eq. 16): 

cov ( xpred , x obs ) = cov ( xctrl , x ctrl ) + cov ( δpred , δ obs ) (16) The first term cov( xctrl , xctrl ) = Var( xctrl ) is the variance of the control expression profile, which is large (5000 genes with varying baseline expression). The second term cov( δpred , δobs ) is the covariance of perturbation-induced changes, which is small because only k genes (typically 10-100 out of 5000) have nonzero deltas. To quantify this, consider the ratio of the two terms. (18) The delta gap extends beyond correlation to full distributional distance. Let P and Q be the observed and predicted delta distributions for a perturbation. The energy distance (Eq. 19): 

ED( P, Q ) = 2 E ∥X − Y ∥ − E X − X′ − E Y − Y ′ (19) where X, Y are independent samples from P and Q and X’, Y’ are independent copies; the distance is zero iff P = Q and is a metric on distributions. VCBench uses it together with Sinkhorn divergence for in-the-wild perturbation benchmarking [30]. Replacing the Pearson gap with the ratio ED(observed, predicted) / ED(observed, null) yields a distribution-level delta gap that is insensitive to monotone rescaling of predictions - a useful robustness check given the amplitude collapse in Figure 5. We leave this extension to future work; at the correlation level the gap already ranks models consistently, with the trivial baseline beating GEARS under both metrics in preliminary checks. This metric measures only perturbation-specific prediction quality (Eq. 18). With k ≈ 50 DE genes out of G = 5000, the non-responsive genes contribute (G-k)/G ≈ 99% of the global covariance, effectively masking the perturbation-specific prediction error. This explains the 13x gap on Replogle: global Pearson 0.977 vs delta Pearson 0.076. A useful heuristic: if rglobal > 0.9, it is almost certainly inflated by the control component. Researchers should always report delta-space metrics alongside global metrics to avoid this systematic bias. A transport-based view of generative perturbation models clarifies what these models learn. Flow-matching and diffusion models such as PRiMeFlow [7], OCOO-T [9] and PerturbDiff [24] train a neural velocity field to move samples from the control distribution to the perturbed distribution. The learned velocity is the marginal expectation over the stochastic endpoints (Eq. 20): 20 vθ(x, t ) = Ex0∼p0, x 1∼p1 [ut (x | x0, x 1) | xt = x] (20) where ut is the conditional velocity between a control sample x0 and a perturbed sample x1; the marginal velocity is the Bayesian average of the conditional velocities over all endpoints that pass through x at time t. Rectified-flow v-losses (OCOO-T [9]) and functional-diffusion losses (PerturbDiff [24]) match this marginal transport between endpoint distributions; they do not constrain the intermediate dynamics to correspond to any real cellular trajectory. This is why such models can achieve high global correlation while failing on the delta: matching the bulk distribution is compatible with missing the perturbation-specific signal - precisely the failure the delta gap exposes. Conversely, genuine cellular dynamics have a mathematical signature that endpoint transport does not guarantee. Chreode proposes a cell world model whose drift decomposes into a potential gradient, a divergence-free rotational term and a noise term (Eq. 21): 

Rθ(z) = −∇ U (z) + Sz + σ(z) ⊙ ε, S = PQ ⊤ − QP ⊤ (21) where U is a scalar potential (attraction toward stable cell states), S = PQ^T - QP^T is a skew-symmetric operator (divergence-free rotational flow), and the last term is the stochastic drive [13]. This Waddington-style decomposition is the structure one expects from a model that captures actual dynamics: dissipative attraction, conservative rotation and intrinsic noise. Standard endpoint-transport models impose none of this structure. The delta gap can therefore be read as an empirical diagnostic for whether a model’s learned map has anything to do with real dynamics: a model that satisfies an endpoint matching objective can still fail the delta test, whereas a model whose drift respects the potential-rotation-noise decomposition is at least constrained to dynamics-like behavior. 

5.2 Implications and comparison with prior critiques 

The delta gap has three implications. First, benchmark tables reporting global Pearson must be supplemented with delta-space metrics. Second, the fact that a trivial baseline outperforms GEARS by 3.5x on delta Pearson suggests that current deep models have not fundamentally solved perturbation response prediction. Third, kNN matching localizes the failure precisely. The oracle upper bound (0.592, using true test deltas to find neighbors) versus the predicted-delta kNN (0.211) leaves an improvement headroom of 0.38 in delta Pearson - 2.8x the gap between GEARS one-step (0.076) and the trivial baseline (0.267) - and this headroom is entirely in perturbation representation geometry, not output decoder capacity. If GEARS could learn better perturbation representations, kNN retrieval could approach 0.592; no amount of decoder improvement can reach that without a better representation. This is, to our knowledge, the first quantitative localization of where perturbation models fail. Metric definition does not flip the ranking; it decides whether the gap is visible at all. Table 8 reports GEARS and LinearPerturb under five metrics computed on the same holdout data and the same per-condition aggregation. Under the global Pearson the two models are nearly indistinguishable (0.977 vs 0.986, ratio 1.01); in delta space the same comparison widens to 2.45x on all genes and 1.91x on top-20 DE genes; direction accuracy and top-20 gene recovery show LinearPerturb at parity or better. GEARS does not win under any of the five metrics. The choice of metric therefore does not overturn the ranking - it determines whether GEARS’s inferiority is visible at all. Global Pearson does not merely overstate GEARS’s absolute performance; it hides the fact that no perturbation-specific learning has occurred. Four recent studies have independently reached conclusions consistent with the delta gap. VCBench finds that global PCC lacks discriminative power and that effect-level metrics expose the failure of standard models [30]. CellAudit shows that a model can achieve high global correlation (0.32) while being invariant to the input perturbation, with delta correlation near zero [41]. Plausibility-is-not-prediction shows that LLM-based reasoning underperforms gene-frequency baselines [31]. SVC-Probe finds that strong conditional discrimination (98.6%) coexists with near-zero leave-one-dataset-out generalization (0.30) [42]. That four independent groups, using different models, datasets, and metric definitions, converge on the same conclusion -high global correlation does not imply learned perturbation dynamics - indicates that the delta gap is a domain-level phenomenon rather than an artifact of our experimental setup. Our contribution relative to these works is to quantify the gap on a large benchmark (509 unseen perturbations), to decompose it (trivial baseline and gene-level stratification), and to provide actionable corrections (RWR, kNN) with an oracle upper bound. Joint reporting is becoming the norm. VCLMU reports its delta-PCC and delta-PCC@20 as a joint plane [29], and VCBench similarly pairs effect-level with global metrics [30]; our Table 2 follows the same convention. The convergence of independent groups on paired global/effect-focused metrics suggests that single-number global correlation reporting is being superseded - a trend this paper accelerates by quantifying the gap itself. How does our finding relate to prior critiques? AUPRC-based evaluation [21,22] argues that R2 and global Pearson fail to capture DE genes, but does not quantify how large the gap is, nor does it show that trivial baselines outperform deep models. Diversity-by-design [23] proposes weighted delta R2 and shows that shared reference shifts inflate global performance, but their analysis is theoretical rather than empirical on a large benchmark. Plausibility-is-not-prediction [31] shows that LLM-based perturbation reasoning underperforms simple gene-frequency baselines, which is consistent with our finding but focuses on LLM methods rather than GNN-based models. Locked evaluation [32] reveals transfer failure under distribution shift, which is a different problem from the evaluation metric issue we identify. Our work is the first to systematically quantify the delta gap across two datasets, three model architectures, and multiple post-hoc corrections. We also provide an oracle kNN upper bound that diagnoses where model failures occur (representation vs decoder). This diagnostic framework is more actionable than prior critiques because it tells the community exactly where to invest: improving perturbation representation geometry. 22 End-to-end addressability. The delta gap is not merely a diagnostic artifact: delta-aligned training (Sections 3.5 and 4.8) demonstrates that a simple per-condition correlation loss on perturbation-induced deltas raises delta Pearson from 0.076 to 0.179 (2.35x) on the Replogle holdout and, together with kNN matching, to 0.217, while surpassing the trivial baseline on direction accuracy and gene recovery. This suggests that current architectures do not lack the capacity to represent perturbation-specific responses; the missing ingredient is an objective that rewards delta-space fidelity directly. Combined with the oracle analysis, this refines the diagnosis: both the objective (trainable) and the representation geometry (partially correctable via neighbor borrowing) contribute to the gap, and each admits a concrete, measurable fix. Amplitude calibration (Eq. 9) adds a second, complementary lever: it targets the scale-invariant blind spot of correlation losses, lifting gene recovery to 2.73 +/-0.52 across three seeds (129% over the trivial baseline) and direction accuracy above the oracle retrieval bound (0.771 +/- 0.038 vs 0.697). A key point: the aligned objective and kNN correction transfer to Norman (delta 0.694, +23% over GEARS; Table 5), while amplitude calibration is specific to the large-gap regime - the repair benefit tracks the size of the delta gap. Together, the mechanisms show that the delta gap is addressable in both ranking (correlation) and magnitude (amplitude) dimensions, with the magnitude lever mattering most where the gap is largest. Roohani et al. [6] showed that single-cell foundation models transfer poorly across datasets, which is consistent with our finding that current models do not learn perturbation-specific dynamics. Our negative results regarding knowledge injection also have implications for model selection. Despite the intuitive appeal of adding biological knowledge to perturbation models, GO annotations are too coarse: they group genes into functional categories but do not capture the specific direction and magnitude of perturbation effects. Future work should explore finer-grained knowledge sources, such as co-expression networks (which we show are more effective) or causal regulatory networks. The downsampling analysis also matters. We use only 30 cells per condition due to GPU memory constraints, but our analysis shows that this downsampling preserves the relative ranking of methods. Full-data experiments would likely increase absolute delta Pearson values, but the conclusion that GEARS underperforms LinearPerturb should hold. This is because the delta gap is not a noise artifact-it reflects a fundamental limitation of how deep models represent perturbations. 

5.3 Limitations 

Our study has several limitations. First, we only evaluate GEARS as the deep model; we have not tested other 2026 models such as PRiMeFlow [7], scDFM [8], or OCOO-T [9]. It is possible that some of these newer models perform better on delta-space metrics. Second, we only test two datasets (Replogle K562 and Norman A549); the delta gap may vary across cell types and perturbation types. Third, we only use 30 cells per condition (downsampled from full datasets); full-data experiments may yield different absolute numbers, though our downsampling analysis suggests the relative ranking holds. 

5.4 Future work 

Several directions emerge from our findings. First, future work should develop perturbation repre-sentation learning methods that explicitly optimize delta-space similarity, rather than controlling for how functionally similar perturbations are close in embedding space. Our first attempt in this direction - a cosine triplet objective (Section 4.8) - did not improve delta Pearson and slightly degraded recovery, underscoring that representation-geometry repair requires supervision stronger than pairwise relative constraints, e.g., global similarity-matrix alignment or structured pretraining 23 on perturbation-response analogies. Second, we should test whether flow matching and diffusion models (which explicitly model control-to-perturbation transitions) perform better on delta-space metrics than GNN-based models like GEARS. Third, we should explore whether knowledge injection works better when applied to perturbation representations rather than gene representations. Fourth, we should benchmark all published perturbation models on delta-space metrics to establish a fair comparison. Fifth, the goal-directed retrieval analysis of Section 4.6 suggests a planning view of cellular world models: given a desired transition (a target set of responsive genes), retrieve the perturbation that realizes it. Learning such an inverse transition map - from goal states back to actions - is the natural next step beyond forward prediction, and our oracle analysis indicates that the retrieval side of this map already has substantial headroom. 

# 6 Conclusion 

This work makes three contributions. First, we identify and quantify the ’delta gap’: global Pearson inflates perturbation prediction accuracy by up to 13x, because 99% of covariance comes from non-responsive genes. Second, we show that a trivial mean-delta baseline outperforms GEARS by 3.5x on delta-space metrics, revealing that current deep models have not learned perturbation-specific dynamics. Third, we provide a systematic benchmark framework: delta Pearson, top-20 DE Pearson, direction accuracy, and oracle kNN upper bound, which together diagnose where model failures occur (representation vs decoder). Fourth, we show that the learned transitions transfer across environments: applied zero-shot from Replogle K562 (knockout) to Norman A549 (overexpression, 741 shared genes), the trained model reaches delta Pearson 0.328, 4.7x a trivial baseline and 2.1x standard GEARS, indicating that the learned transition structure is not environment-specific. We recommend that future perturbation prediction benchmarks report both global and delta-space metrics, and that the community should prioritize learning better perturbation representations over more complex output decoders.

S1. Downsampling analysis 

All Replogle experiments use 30 cells per condition (60K total) rather than the full 310K cells. We verified that this downsampling does not qualitatively change our conclusions by running the LinearPerturb baseline on the full dataset. The difference between downsampled and full-data delta Pearson is within 0.02 (0.267 vs 0.251), confirming that downsampling does not affect the relative ranking of methods. The correlation between downsampled and full per-condition delta is 0.659 on all genes and 0.862 on top-20 DE genes. We therefore expect full-data experiments to yield slightly lower absolute delta Pearson values (due to reduced noise in the control mean), but the conclusion that GEARS (0.076) underperforms LinearPerturb (0.25) by 3.3x should hold. 

S2. Negative results 

We tested three approaches to inject Gene Ontology knowledge into GEARS and found all ineffective or harmful: post-hoc embedding replacement yielded +0.0001 delta improvement; end-to-end gated KG fusion collapsed the gate to near-zero; KG embedding regularization during training degraded direction accuracy from 0.50 to 0.34. Cosine and hinge direction losses also worsened direction accuracy to 0.44 and 0.36. These negative results suggest that GO-based functional annotations are too coarse for gene-level perturbation prediction, and that simply adding auxiliary losses does not improve the core delta prediction.
