Abstract 
Large histology archives capture morphological correlates of molecular tissue states but lack 
direct spatial protein measurements. In this study, we show that routine hematoxylin and 
eosin (H&E) histology can provide a queryable, morphology-based representation of spatial 
protein expression that remains informative from pixels to patients. To achieve this, we 
developed QUEST, a model framework that represents protein markers as semantic queries 
for flexible multiplex immunofluorescence (MIF) prediction. Trained on a heterogeneous atlas 
of paired H&E and MIF images comprising over 10,000 tissue regions, QUEST captured 
morphological patterns associated with protein markers and reconstructed their expression 
intensities across diverse tissue types and cohorts. Its utility extended beyond image 
reconstruction and preserved biologically meaningful information across multiple scales: 
virtual staining preserved protein marker expression at the cellular and tissue scales, 
supported cell phenotyping, cellular neighborhood analysis, and characterization of spatial 
tissue organization, retained clinically relevant signals for patient stratification, and enabled 
H&E-based queries to retrieve MIF references with similar molecular profiles. These findings 
position virtual staining not simply as image generation but as a flexible interface between 
routine histology and spatial proteomics, expanding the analytical value of existing H&E 
archives.  
Introduction 
Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining-based histology, which provides detailed views of 
tissue architecture and cellular morphology, is foundational to the diagnosis of diverse 
diseases. Beneath these morphological patterns lies a layer of molecular organization that 
shapes tissue behavior, including different immune cell lineages, programs related to 
activation, immune checkpoints, exhaustion, and stromal function. Although some molecular 
programs correlate with morphological patterns, they are not directly measured by H&E 
staining.  Multiplex tissue imaging complements morphology by profiling protein expression 
in situ and revealing recurrent cellular neighborhoods, tumor-host interactions, and diverse 
niches linked to disease progression and treatment response (Hickey et al. 2023; Keren et 
al. 2018; Schürch et al. 2020; Asrir et al. 2022; J.-R. Lin et al. 2023). 
Generating protein expression maps remains expensive and resource-intensive, limiting their 
applications in large cohorts. By contrast, H&E staining is routinely performed and therefore 
widely available in both clinical practice and tissue archives. Importantly, previous studies 
have demonstrated that deep learning can identify morphological patterns in H&E-stained 
images that are associated with genetic alterations and molecular phenotypes (Coudray et 
al. 2018; Kather et al. 2019, 2020). These findings suggest that some aspects of the 
underlying molecular landscape are reflected in tissue morphology. Together, the widespread 
availability of H&E-stained images and growing evidence that tissue morphology contains 
correlates of the underlying molecular state have motivated efforts to predict spatial 
protein-expression maps directly from histology. 
Recent efforts have demonstrated the feasibility of predicting multiplex immunofluorescence 
(MIF) images from H&E images across increasingly diverse cohorts (Wu et al. 2025; 
Valanarasu et al. 2026; Balezo et al. 2026; Li et al. 2026). However, most virtual MIF staining 
methods remain formulated as image translation to a predefined marker panel. This design

limits their reuse across studies with different marker panels and leaves generalization to 
new markers largely unresolved. Robust generalization across datasets also remains 
challenging, as tissue type, disease context, staining platform, and marker prevalence can  
influence the relationship between H&E-based morphology and protein expression. Beyond 
these limitations, although several studies have explored the downstream biological and 
clinical applications of virtual MIF staining, such capabilities have not been systematically 
benchmarked across models, cohorts, and tasks. Further, the potential of virtual MIF staining 
to enable cross-modal applications that connect H&E-based histology with archives of 
existing spatial proteomics data also remains underexplored.  
In this study, we developed QUEST (QUEry-based virtual STaining) to address these gaps. 
Rather than assigning each marker to a fixed output panel, QUEST represents requested 
markers as queries that interact with H&E features and share a common spatial readout. 
This formulation allows the requested panel to be specified at inference and creates a 
natural route for transfer to markers not observed during training. QUEST was trained and 
evaluated on a large, heterogeneous atlas of paired H&E and multiplex immunofluorescence 
images comprising over 2.4 million patches spanning diverse tissues and marker panels. 
We systematically evaluated the predictive fidelity and biological utility of QUEST across 
held-out and external cohorts at multiple scales, ranging from pixels and cells to tissue 
regions and patients. Beyond image reconstruction, we examined whether virtual MIF 
preserved biologically meaningful information for cell phenotyping, spatial tissue 
characterization, and downstream clinical analyses. We further evaluated the utility of virtual 
MIF for cross-modal applications. Our results show that virtual MIF preserves biologically 
and clinically relevant information across scales, from cellular phenotypes and tissue 
organization to patient-level endpoints. Together, we envision QUEST transforming virtual 
MIF staining from fixed-panel image translation into a queryable bridge between routine H&E 
and spatial proteomics, opening a novel path to molecularly informed analysis of the vast 
H&E histology collections already available in research and clinical practice. 
Results 
Heterogeneous H&E-to-MIF datasets for model development and 
benchmarking 
To support the development of H&E-to-MIF virtual staining models across diverse tissue 
contexts and marker panels, we assembled a large, heterogeneous collection of spatially 
co-registered paired H&E and MIF images (Figure 1a). Whole-slide images and tissue 
microarrays were cropped into non-overlapping image patches for model training and 
held-out evaluation (Vorontsov et al. 2024; R. J. Chen et al. 2024). We curated data 
spanning diverse tissue types and imaging markers. The collection for model development 
and evaluation comprised 12,880 tissue regions from 10,891 patients, from which more than 
2.4 million image patches were curated, and 93% of the regions were obtained from cancer 
tissues. In total, the collection contained 129 distinct biomarkers, with a detailed list provided 
in Supplementary Data 1. Among these, 29 broadly expressed markers were assayed 
across all tissue types (Figure 1b, Supplementary Figure 1a). Marker panels varied across 
regions, with a median panel size of 53 (Supplementary Figure 1b). Colon and rectum 
represented the largest tissue type category, comprising more than 1,500 regions, whereas

We organized the evaluation into three task categories (Figure 1d). First, multi-scale fidelity

signals for patient stratification and survival prediction. Third, cross-modality retrieval 
assessed whether virtual MIF can link an H&E query image to galleries of real MIF images. 
Further details of the held-out test datasets, external evaluation cohorts, and task-specific 
protocols are provided in Methods. 
Model development of QUEST 
The substantial variation in protein marker composition across cohorts and studies presents 
a fundamental challenge for existing H&E-to-MIF virtual staining models. We developed 
models for flexible prediction of spatial proteomics maps from H&E images, named QUEST 
(QUEry-based virtual STaining), a dynamic, query-based architecture designed specifically 
for virtual MIF staining. 
QUEST receives an H&E image together with a specified set of marker queries and 
generates one spatial intensity map for each requested marker (Supplementary Figure 2a). 
Three model variants of QUEST were developed: QUEST-semantic and QUEST-ID used the 
pretrained pathology foundation model UNI2-h (R. J. Chen et al. 2024) as their feature 
backbone. QUEST-Eva leveraged Eva (Yufan Liu et al. 2025), a masked autoencoder 
framework based on the Vision Transformer (ViT) model pretrained jointly on spatial 
proteomics and matched histopathology, as its backbone. Briefly, marker queries interact 
with the image representations through attention-based mechanisms, and a readout decoder 
uses the integrated embeddings to generate the corresponding virtual MIF maps. To capture 
the heterogeneous marker panels, QUEST-ID uses learned marker-specific embeddings and 
is therefore restricted to markers encountered during training. QUEST-semantic leverages 
marker representations defined from fixed text-derived embeddings within a shared semantic 
space, providing a route to predicting markers absent from the training vocabulary 
(Methods).

(PCC) and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (Spearman’s ρ) at the patch level,

QUEST models achieved higher pixel-level PCC and Spearman’s ρ than previous methods 
in most datasets, and their performance advantages were retained after grid-level 
aggregation (Figure 2a). Grid-level correlations were generally higher than their pixel-level 
counterparts, consistent with local spatial averaging reducing sensitivity to minor pixel-level 
misalignment and high frequency intensity variation. As existing methods were developed 
using different marker panels, we repeated the analysis using the intersection marker sets  
for each benchmarked method. Across comparisons, QUEST models generally achieved 
higher overall PCC (Supplementary Figure 3a). Notably, ORION-CRC was used to train 
MIPHEI-ViT (Balezo et al. 2026). 
Prediction accuracy varied considerably across markers (Figure 2b). QUEST models 
achieved strong per-marker PCC across cancer, stromal, endothelial, immune-lineage, and 
immune-functional groups, although absolute performance remained dependent on the 
abundance, morphological correlates, and predictability of each marker. Markers associated 
with visually distinct cellular or tissue structures generally achieved higher correlations, 
including DAPI, PCNA, TP63, EpCAM, and Vimentin. By contrast, sparse immune and 
functional markers, such as CD8, CD163, and CD39, remained more difficult to infer from 
H&E morphology. To this end, we further compared QUEST models with independently 
trained single-marker U-Net models (Methods, Ronneberger et al. 2015). Independently 
trained single-marker models provided an empirical estimate of marker-level predictability. 
QUEST models generally matched or exceeded the specialized single-marker models 
(Figure 2c). Our results indicate that some markers have limited predictability from H&E 
morphology rather than being limited by our modeling architectures. 
MIF images are often sparse, with marker-positive signals occupying only a small fraction of 
the image. Consequently, the structural similarity index measure (SSIM, Wang et al. 2004), a 
different assessment from the correlation-based metrics, may be biased (Figure 2d). An 
all-black prediction containing no marker signal achieved SSIM values approaching or 
exceeding those of trained virtual MIF staining models, because large background regions 
dominated the metric even when biologically relevant signals were missed. We therefore 
complemented the evaluations with foreground-centric analyses that assessed both intensity 
agreement within positive signals and discrimination between foreground and background.  
We defined foreground and background using Otsu thresholding (Methods, Otsu 1979). 
Foreground PCC, calculated within marker-positive pixels of the real MIF image, showed 
that QUEST models outperformed the corresponding baselines in most datasets 
(Supplementary Figure 3b). Pixel-wise scatter plots further showed closer agreement 
between predicted and measured signal intensities (Supplementary Figure 3c). We 
additionally evaluated foreground–background discrimination using F1 score and AUPRC, 
which capture both missed foreground signals and false positive predictions in background 
regions. QUEST models generally outperformed other virtual MIF staining methods on both 
metrics (Supplementary Figure 4a–b), and their predicted foreground masks more closely

Pan-cancer training improves tissue-specific predictions 
To evaluate the benefits of pan-cancer training, we compared the pan-cancer QUEST model 
with identically configured models trained exclusively on colon or lung tissues using the 
same QUEST-semantic architecture. On tissue-specific marker panels, pan-cancer training 
increased macro PCC from 0.352 to 0.383 in colon and from 0.183 to 0.228 in lung. 
Supplementary Figure 5a shows the corresponding per-marker changes (ΔPCC). Among 
the 57 markers shared between the two tissues, the per-marker gains were positively related 
(PCC = 0.695), indicating that the benefits of pan-cancer training were reproducible across 
tissue contexts. The largest gains occurred for vascular and stromal markers, including 
CD34, CD31, and aSMA, whose associated morphological structures are broadly shared 
across tissues.  
We next tested whether supervision for a marker in other tissues could generalize to its 
prediction in a target tissue where the marker was not observed. We selected five markers 
measured in both the colon and lung panels, including EpCAM, BCL2, PDL1, Ki67, and 
FOXP3, and constructed controlled target-tissue marker holdouts. For each tissue-specific 
model, these markers were removed from all training data and therefore evaluated as 
zero-shot markers. For the corresponding pan-cancer model, the same markers remained 
excluded from the target tissue but were supervised in other cancer types (Methods). 
Supervision from other tissues significantly improved all five markers in colon and three of 
five markers in lung, whereas Ki67 and FOXP3 in lung showed no detectable difference 
(Supplementary Figure 5b). 
Virtual MIF preserves region-level fidelity and supports cell-level 
profiling 
Having evaluated pixel-level fidelity of virtual MIF, we next evaluated the fidelity of virtual MIF 
staining across diverse tissue types using held-out test data (Methods). We summarized the 
correlations between virtual and real MIF signals across 21 tissue categories (Figure 3a, 
Methods). QUEST-Eva achieved the highest PCC of 0.275 across tissue types, followed by 
QUEST-ID and QUEST-semantic, outperforming existing virtual MIF staining methods 
(0.079–0.218). Performance varied across tissue types, with higher average correlations in 
colon and rectum, breast and stomach, and lower correlations in skin and bone marrow. We 
further demonstrated virtual MIF staining on whole slide images (WSI) from the held-out 
dataset and TCGA (Supplementary Figure 6a-b). 
We next evaluated whether virtual MIF preserved marker expression at cellular resolution. 
Cells were identified by a DAPI-based segmentation method (Greenwald et al. 2022), with 
measured and predicted marker intensities aggregated within each cell (Figure 3b,

Virtual MIF recapitulates the clinical utility of real MIF and 
complements H&E-based prediction 
Beyond image-level fidelity and cell-level profiling, we investigated whether virtual MIF could 
provide useful or complementary information for patient-level clinical predictions. First, we 
tested whether virtual MIF retained the downstream predictive utility of real MIF. In the 
held-out test cohorts with paired H&E and real MIF, we compared the downstream utility of 
virtual and real MIF using the same marker panels on 4 distinct clinical tasks. QUEST-based 
virtual MIF staining achieved performance comparable to real MIF (Figure 4a). For 
melanoma stage classification, QUEST-ID and QUEST-semantic achieved the highest 
AUROCs of 0.817 and 0.801, as compared to the real MIF reference of 0.780. For lung 
cancer stage classification, QUEST models led with AUROCs of 0.714–0.754. For CRC 
metastatic survival modeling, real MIF remained the strongest modality with a C-index of 
0.758, followed by QUEST-ID and QUEST-semantic at 0.725 and 0.717, respectively, 
whereas existing methods achieved 0.515–0.640. For breast cancer grade classification, 
real MIF achieved the highest AUROC of 0.719, while QUEST models remained the leading 
virtual MIF staining methods at 0.667–0.689. 
We further evaluated the value of virtual MIF in complementing H&E-based predictions using 
public TCGA cohorts. The benefit of combining virtual MIF with H&E varied across tasks. For 
TCGA-NSCLC stage classification, H&E enhanced with QUEST-generated virtual MIF 
significantly improved upon the H&E-only baseline. Similarly, QUEST models yielded 
significant improvements in TCGA-COAD’s  progression-free survival prediction over the 
H&E-only baseline. In TCGA-NSCLC Lung Adenocarcinoma/Lung Squamous Cell 
Carcinoma (LUAD/LUSC) classification, H&E alone achieved an AUC of 0.978, and only 
marginal gains were observed from virtual MIF. Modest gains were observed for 
TCGA-COAD KRAS mutation prediction and consensus molecular subtype (CMS) 
classification, whereas performance for TCGA-ESCA overall survival remained similar to the 
H&E-only baseline (Figure 4b). These results show that virtual MIF can improve H&E-based 
prediction for selected clinical endpoints, although the magnitude of benefit remains highly 
task-dependent. 
To identify the driving factors of survival predictions, we quantified patch-level contributions 
by ablating each patch and measuring the resulting change in predicted risk. In the held-out 
metastatic CRC survival cohort, risk-raising regions showed stromal morphology 
accompanied by prominent CD68-associated signals in both virtual and real MIF, whereas 
risk-lowering regions were more tumor-rich (Figure 4c and Supplementary Figure 12a). In 
TCGA-COAD progression-free survival, risk-increasing patches appeared predominantly

prognostic relevance of stromal programs and specific tumor-associated macrophage states 
in CRC (Joanito et al. 2022; Yedan Liu et al. 2022). In the more clinically heterogeneous

remain the major informative correlate of progression risk.

Motivated by the tumor–immune contrast observed in the patch-level contribution maps, we 
next asked whether individual virtual marker signals were associated with patient outcomes 
in TCGA-COAD. Kaplan–Meier analyses showed that higher virtual CD68, CD8, and CD20 
signals were associated with longer progression-free survival, whereas virtual DAPI and 
PanCK were not associated with prognosis (Supplementary Figure 13a). The favorable 
association observed for CD8, together with the broader prognostic relevance of 
quantitatively assessed immune infiltration, is consistent with large-scale studies of CRC trial 
cohorts (Frei et al. 2024). We further evaluated the virtual marker signal CD8/PanCK ratio as 
a simple, interpretable, and MIL-independent feature representing cytotoxic T-cell infiltration 
relative to epithelial tumor content. A higher CD8/PanCK ratio significantly stratified 
progression-free survival in Kaplan–Meier analysis (log-rank P = 0.010) and remained 
independently protective in multivariable Cox regression after adjustment for the virtual-MIF 
MIL score, age, sex, stage, and MSI status. The MIL score also remained independently 
associated with increased risk, indicating that it captured prognostic information 
complementary to the CD8/PanCK ratio (Supplementary Figure 13b). 
Cross-modal retrieval bridged by virtual MIF staining 
In clinical practice, MIF is rarely acquired, which limits its direct applications in clinical 
decision-making for new patients. However, the growing number of public and institutional 
collections makes it increasingly feasible to assemble large-scale MIF reference galleries of 
tissues (HuBMAP Consortium 2019; Rozenblatt-Rosen et al. 2020). Such galleries could 
potentially provide molecular contexts for H&E-stained samples by linking them with existing 
MIF references when their exact counterparts are unavailable. We therefore investigated 
whether virtual MIF staining could serve as the bridge between the two modalities by 
mapping an H&E query into MIF space and retrieving reference MIF patches with similar 
protein expression patterns and cellular composition. 
First, we used paired H&E–MIF patches from the held-out Mixed 10K dataset. For each H&E 
query, the patch was translated into virtual MIF, which was then encoded using a frozen 
foundation model (Yufan Liu et al. 2025) to obtain the query embedding. Candidate patches 
from the MIF gallery were encoded through the same procedure and ranked by their cosine 
similarity to the query (Figure 5a). The gallery comprised the MIF counterparts of all eligible 
patches in Mixed 10K, with each paired patch serving once as an H&E query during 
evaluation. Patches from the query’s source region were excluded from the gallery. We 
evaluated the retrieval efficacy of different virtual MIF staining models along with a 
specialized cross-modal retrieval model, Haiku (Cui et al. 2026), using similarity metrics in 
the protein expression and cellular composition space. QUEST models produced concordant 
retrievals among the evaluated approaches. QUEST-semantic and QUEST-ID achieved a 
top-1 expression cosine similarity of 0.52, compared with 0.15–0.37 for previous virtual MIF 
staining methods and 0.38 for Haiku (Figure 5b).  In addition, QUEST-semantic yielded the 
lowest L2-distance distribution, which differed significantly (p < 0.001) from those of all other 
methods except QUEST-ID by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (Supplementary Figure 14a). 
Consistently, QUEST models achieved the highest precision profiles across retrieval depths, 
with QUEST-semantic generally performing the best (Supplementary Figure 14b).

compositions. This analysis used regions from the held-out test data, comprising 
gastrointestinal stromal tumor and pancreas cancer tissues with cell segmentation and

Discussion 
Inferring spatial protein expression from routine H&E could extend molecularly informed 
tissue analysis to cohorts where direct measurements of molecular data are impractical. 
Existing virtual MIF staining methods are typically restricted to predefined marker panels, 
and their utility for downstream biological and clinical analyses remains insufficiently 
understood. In this study, we developed QUEST, a class of models that dynamically take the 
requested markers as query inputs and generate their corresponding expression intensity 
maps. This query-based formulation allows a single model to accommodate heterogeneous 
marker panels rather than being tied to a predefined output panel. We evaluated the 
resulting virtual MIF across biological scales, from pixels and cells to tissue regions and 
patients, as well as heterogeneous marker panels and imaging platforms.  
Multi-scale evaluations suggested that virtual MIF staining cannot be adequately assessed 
by a single image-level metric. In particular, our analysis exposed a failure mode of SSIM 
(Wang et al. 2004) in sparse MIF images, where empty predictions received deceptively high 
scores. We therefore complemented standard similarity and correlation measures with 
foreground intensities and foreground-background discrimination analyses. Across metrics, 
QUEST generated virtual MIF that achieved strong agreement with real MIF in held-out and 
external datasets, reconstructing signals across diverse tissue categories and marker 
panels. We further showed that pan-cancer training improved tissue-specific prediction and 
supported cross-tissue transfer of marker supervision. Virtual MIF preserved relative marker 
expression intensities and marker-positive populations at the cell level, supporting 
multi-marker cell typing, cellular neighborhood profiling, and characterization of their spatial 
organization, such as T-cell proximity to vascular structures and local cell-type diversity. 
Notably, the end-to-end workflow based on virtual DAPI and virtual marker expression 
reproduced major cellular neighborhood patterns. These findings demonstrate the biological 
fidelity of virtual MIF captured through a multi-scale evaluation of marker-positive signals, 
cellular composition, and tissue organization. 
At the patient level, virtual MIF could retain clinically relevant signals captured by real MIF 
and could augment H&E-based prediction in the absence of molecular signals. Concretely, 
virtual MIF is inferred from H&E, and these improvements should not be interpreted as 
recovery of independent molecular measurements. Instead, virtual MIF staining exposes

Several limitations remain. Although the training atlas consisted primarily of PhenoCycler 
and PhenoCycler Fusion data, external evaluations on ORION-CRC, HEMIT, and 
HuBMAP-intestine provided preliminary evidence of generalization across additional imaging 
platforms and staining protocols. These external datasets nevertheless remain limited in 
scale and marker coverage, and larger cross-platform evaluations will be needed to further 
establish the robustness of virtual MIF. Evaluations of virtual MIF prediction are bounded by 
the quality of experimental ground truth, and future benchmarking and model development 
will benefit from curated, gold-standard datasets with stringent quality control and 
autofluorescence/noise correction to establish the true limits of virtual MIF staining fidelity.

per-marker evaluations and independently trained single-marker U-Net references showed 
substantial variation in predictability across proteins, with markers associated with visually 
distinct cellular or tissue structures generally being more predictable than sparse immune or

To surpass these bottlenecks while maintaining clinical feasibility, integrating accessible

may be a promising path towards revealing the nuanced molecular states. Further, future

confidence estimates for individual marker predictions. These advances will help determine 
which virtual markers are sufficiently reliable for downstream analysis and which still require 
experimental validation.

of clinical utility, and further validation in larger, independent external cohorts with paired 
H&E–MIF assays and well-annotated outcomes will be required. Moreover, while we 
demonstrated that virtual MIF can support cell typing and CN annotation, deploying it for 
larger-scale discovery of novel disease-associated tumor-microenvironment biomarkers 
remains a challenging and resource-intensive application reserved for future investigations. 
Overall, we established QUEST to transform virtual MIF staining from fixed-panel image 
translation into a flexible interface between H&E-based histology and spatial proteomics. 
QUEST dynamically generated virtual MIF intensity maps for the queried markers, which 
preserved information relevant to cellular phenotyping, tissue organization, patient-level 
analysis, and cross-modal retrieval. With broader prospective validation and reliable 
uncertainty estimation, QUEST could transform vast H&E archives into queryable, 
molecularly informed resources, enabling population-scale mapping of tissue ecosystems, 
accelerating biomarker discovery, and extending spatially informed precision medicine far 
beyond the scale currently accessible to direct MIF profiling.

Atlas for model development 
In this study, we curated a large-scale paired H&E–multiplex immunofluorescence (MIF) 
atlas across marker panels and tissues. The atlas contains 12,880 tissue regions with 
resolution of 0.3775–0.5 µm/pixel from 21 tissue categories and collectively covers 129 
protein markers. Most regions were generated using the Akoya PhenoCycler platform. Each 
region has a matched H&E image and a region-specific MIF panel. Marker panels are not 
identical across samples, with a median of 53 measured markers per region 
(Supplementary Figure 1a-b). Model inputs and targets were represented as spatially 
paired non-overlapping 224 224 patches, with pixel intensities normalized to [0, 1] 
×
independently for each marker within each tissue region. The split was performed at the 
patient level within each tissue category. A total of 11,068 regions were assigned to model 
development, comprising 9,791 training regions and 1,277 validation regions. The remaining 
1,812 regions formed the held-out evaluation pool. The indexed training, validation, and test 
portions contained 1,926,041, 213,943, and 260,352 paired patches, respectively.  
The non-public portion of the atlas used in this study comprised de-identified H&E and 
multiplex immunofluorescence images and associated clinical metadata derived from 
archival human tissue specimens. All specimens were obtained and used by Enable 
Medicine in accordance with applicable informed-consent or consent-waiver requirements, 
institutional review board or ethics committee oversight, and relevant regulatory guidelines. 
Enable Medicine generated derivative imaging and analytical data from these specimens. All 
images and associated metadata were de-identified before computational model 
development and analysis. 
Image-level evaluation datasets

described above. None of these H&E images, MIF images, or clinical labels were used to 
optimize models in this study. The multi-tissue Mixed 10K benchmark was formed by

Fine-tuning of QUEST-Eva 
QUEST-Eva was obtained by fine-tuning the pretrained Eva (Yufan Liu et al. 2025) masked 
autoencoder following the H&E-to-MIF adaptation procedure described in the Eva study. To 
ensure consistency with the data partition used in this study and prevent information 
leakage, we repeated the fine-tuning using only our designated training set, with checkpoint 
selection performed on the corresponding validation set. All model inputs, channel 
embeddings, masking strategy, reconstruction objective, and inference procedure were 
otherwise identical to those described in Eva. Briefly, the RGB H&E images were treated as 
separate channels and concatenated with multi-channel MIF images, with channel identities 
encoded using the pretrained GenePT (Y. Chen and Zou 2025) marker embeddings. All MIF 
spatial tokens were masked while the 3 H&E channels remained visible, and reconstruction 
loss was computed only over the masked MIF targets. The pretrained initialization 
checkpoint is available at https://huggingface.co/yandrewl/Eva.

Evaluation tasks and metrics 
All model selection and hyperparameter tuning were performed using the training and 
validation partitions. The final weights were evaluated on held-out data or external cohorts 
that were not used for model optimization. Marker names were harmonized to the QUEST 
vocabulary before comparison, with only straightforward nomenclature aliases resolved, 
such as Hoechst to DAPI, CD8a to CD8, Pan-CK/CK to PanCK, SMA to aSMA, E-cadherin 
to ECad, PD-1 to PD1, PD-L1 to PDL1, and FOXP3 to FoxP3. No biological proxy 
substitutions were made. Unless otherwise stated, image-level metrics were calculated 
separately for each marker within each patch, averaged across markers to obtain a

Cell-level evaluation 
Cells were segmented with Mesmer (Greenwald et al. 2022) from the DAPI channels of real 
MIF. The resulting cell masks were applied identically to the real and predicted MIF images. 
For each cell and marker, the continuous cell-level signal was defined as the mean pixel 
intensity within the corresponding cell mask. For the continuous cell-level fidelity analysis, 
PCC between real and predicted cell-level intensities restricted to the cell mask was 
calculated for each region–marker pair and then averaged across regions to obtain the 
per-marker result. 
We additionally evaluated recovery of marker-positive cells as a binary task. A marker- and 
region-specific percentile-normalized threshold was applied to the measured cell-level 
intensities to assign each cell a reference positive or negative label, whereas the continuous 
virtual MIF cell intensities were retained as prediction scores. Cell-level discrimination was 
quantified by AUROC, which evaluates the ranking of positive versus negative cells across 
all possible decision thresholds. For visualization, thresholding the real signal yielded K 
positive cells for a given marker and region. The real MIF map displays these K positive 
cells, while each virtual MIF map displays the K cells with the highest predicted intensities.

Regional contribution and feature-associated survival analysis 
To identify patches that influenced MIL survival predictions, we removed one patch at a time 
from each region bag and recomputed the predicted risk score while keeping the trained 
model fixed. Patch contribution was measured as the change from the risk score obtained 
with the complete bag to the score obtained after removing that patch. A positive contribution 
indicates that including the patch increased the predicted risk, whereas a negative 
contribution indicates that it decreased the predicted risk. This procedure used repeated 
forward inference, and the model was not retrained. This analysis was applied to the 
held-out metastatic colorectal cancer survival and to TCGA-COAD PFS cohorts. Virtual MIF 
signals used in these analyses were generated using QUEST-semantic. 
Feature-associated survival analyses were performed  in TCGA-COAD using PFS as the 
endpoint. Patient-level virtual MIF summaries were generated for DAPI, PanCK, CD68, CD8,

Cross-modality retrieval evaluation 
We evaluated whether an H&E query could retrieve a biologically similar MIF patch from a 
reference gallery. For each query, a virtual staining model first generated the requested MIF 
panel from H&E. A frozen Eva (Yufan Liu et al. 2025) spatial proteomics encoder then 
converted the virtual MIF into a query embedding. Candidate real MIF patches were 
encoded with the same Eva encoder, and the gallery was ranked by cosine similarity 
between the query and candidate embeddings. All patches originating from the query's 
source region were excluded from its gallery, preventing retrieval of a spatially adjacent or 
duplicate field. The real MIF paired with the query H&E was retained as an evaluation 
reference and was not used to rank candidates. 
Haiku (Cui et al. 2026) was evaluated as a specialized cross-modal retrieval baseline. For 
Haiku, the H&E query and real MIF gallery images were encoded with its released 
modality-specific encoders and projection heads, and the resulting embeddings were 
normalized, and gallery images were ranked by cosine similarity. Haiku therefore did not 
generate an intermediate virtual MIF image.  
Patch-level expression retrieval similarity was evaluated on Mixed 10K. For each query, 
marker expression vectors were obtained from its paired MIF reference and from the 
top-ranked MIF candidate. Retrieval quality was quantified by the cosine similarity and 
Euclidean (L2) distance between these expression vectors. Expression similarity was 
calculated using a fixed marker panel shared across patches in Mixed 10K, including CD8, 
CD4, CD3e, CD68, FoxP3, PDL1, PD1, Ki67, CD31, and DAPI. Differences between the 
L2-distance distribution of QUEST-semantic and another method were assessed with a 
two-sided two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. 
Cellular retrieval was evaluated in held-out gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) and 
pancreas cancer (PC) cohorts with cell segmentation and cell type annotations. The GIST 
cohort contained 1,319 patches from 25 regions, and the PC cohort contained 2,514 patches 
from 29 regions. Retrieval was performed separately within each cohort, and the two 
candidate galleries were not pooled. For each patch, a cell composition vector was 
constructed from the fraction of cells assigned to each cell type, and the dominant cell type 
was defined as the type with the largest fraction. For each query, we calculated the PCC 
between the cell-composition vectors of the paired MIF and the top-ranked retrieved MIF 
patch. We also reported top-1 dominant cell type accuracy, defined as whether the dominant 
cell type in the retrieved patch matched that in the paired MIF. Precision@k was defined as 
the proportion of the top-k retrieved patches whose dominant cell type matched that of the 
paired reference. All metrics were first calculated separately within each cohort and then 
averaged across the GIST and PC cohorts.

Zero-shot marker prediction 
We evaluated zero-shot marker prediction with QUEST-semantic by completely excluding 24 
markers from both model training and validation. Two zero-shot QUEST variants were 
trained from scratch using only the remaining markers, and neither model contained 
learnable marker-identity embeddings. 
The two variants differed only in the text prompts used to represent markers throughout both 
training and inference. In the function prompt variant, the marker was conditioned on a 
description of its biological role. In the drawing prompt variant, the marker was instead 
conditioned on a description of its expected staining morphology, subcellular localization, 
and spatial distribution. Marker descriptions were encoded into 3,072-dimensional 
embeddings 
using

the shared marker-query space. At inference, unseen markers were requested using the 
same prompt type on which the corresponding variant had been trained. Zero-shot 
performance was evaluated on a fixed set of 10,000 patches sampled from the held-out test 
set. For each of the 24 unseen markers, pixel-level PCC between the virtual MIF prediction 
and real MIF reference was calculated and averaged across patches to obtain the 
per-marker result.  
3D virtual MIF construction 
To construct a three-dimensional virtual MIF volume, we used the c001_A prostate cancer 
biopsy from the nondestructive 3D pathology dataset (Xie et al. 2022). The dataset contains 
an optically cleared intact tissue volume imaged using TO-PRO-3 nuclear and eosin 
fluorescence channels. The fused volume had an in-plane resolution of approximately 0.88 
μm/pixel, and every second optical plane was converted into pseudo-H&E images to reduce 
redundancy and computational cost while preserving tissue continuity. Each H&E plane was 
processed independently by QUEST-semantic using overlapping 224 224 pixel sliding 
×
windows with a stride of 112 pixels. The predicted planes were then stacked in their original 
order to form the 3D virtual MIF volume. We showcased the resulting reconstructions for 
DAPI, CD31, EpCAM, and aSMA. No inter-plane registration was required because the 
planes were acquired through continuous optical sampling of the same intact tissue.

Implementation of related models 
To benchmark virtual staining performance across datasets, we compared our models with 
recent methods applicable to H&E-to-MIF translation, including ROSIE, MIPHEI-ViT, 
GigaTIME, GigaTIME-flash, and Eva. Each H&E patch was resized to the spatial input 
dimensions required by the respective model. The predicted MIF maps were then resized 
back to 224 224 patch before evaluation. For cross-modal retrieval, we additionally 
×
compared against Haiku, a model specifically designed for this task. DINOv3 was used as 
the MIF feature extractor for downstream patient-level analyses. For all comparison 
methods, we used the publicly released weights provided by the original authors and 
followed their official inference procedures. 
ROSIE 
ROSIE (Wu et al. 2025) uses a ConvNeXt (Z. Liu et al. 2022) backbone with a regression 
head to predict a 50-marker expression vector from each local H&E patch. Following the 
implementation, we applied the released model weights in a sliding window fashion across 
each input image and combined overlapping local predictions using a Gaussian weighting 
strategy, thereby converting the patch-level expression predictions into dense marker maps. 
The released weights of ROSIE can be found at  https://huggingface.co/ericwu09/ROSIE. 
MIPHEI-ViT 
MIPHEI-ViT (Balezo et al. 2026) combines a frozen H-optimus-0 (Saillard et al. 2024), 
ViT-g/14 pathology foundation-model backbone, LoRA adaptation, and a decoder to predict 
a fixed 16-marker MIF panel from H&E. The outputs of MIPHEI-ViT were mapped to the 
intensity range before evaluation following the released inference implementation. As 
MIPHEI-ViT was supervised on the ORION-CRC dataset, we treated it as an in-domain 
reference for that cohort rather than as a strictly zero-shot baseline. The released weights of 
MIPHEI-ViT can be found at  https://huggingface.co/Estabousi/MIPHEI-vit. 
GigaTIME 
GigaTIME (Valanarasu et al. 2026) is a UNet++-based (Zhou et al. 2020) model that 
translates H&E images into a fixed multiplex panel. Its original output is optimized for binary 
prediction, and we adapted the post-sigmoid outputs to evaluate the model's pixel-level 
performance. The resulting probability maps were used throughout the same multi-scale 
benchmark as the other virtual-staining models. The released weights of GigaTIME can be 
found at https://huggingface.co/prov-gigatime/GigaTIME. 
GigaTIME-flash 
GigaTIME-flash (Usuyama et al. 2026) is the efficient variant of GigaTIME, using a 
LoRA-adapted DINOv2 (Oquab et al. 2023) ViT-Small encoder and a convolutional decoder 
in place of the UNet++ architecture. We loaded the released weights and used the native 
marker positivity outputs without additional intensity matching. It shares the same marker 
panel and channel ordering as GigaTIME. The released weights of GigaTIME-flash can be 
found at https://huggingface.co/prov-gigatime/gigatime-flash.

Eva 
Eva  (Yufan Liu et al. 2025) was used in two distinct roles, which were not assigned separate 
display names in the preceding text and figures but can be distinguished by task. The 
pretrained masked autoencoder checkpoint “Eva_model.ckpt” was used as the MIF feature 
encoder in the cross-modality retrieval task, which produced a 768-dimensional MIF 
embedding. The embeddings were used to construct the query and gallery representation. 
For virtual staining, we instead used the released H&E-to-MIF fine-tuned checkpoint 
“Eva_ft.ckpt”. In this mode, the marker names and H&E channels were provided as 
observed inputs, and the model reconstructed continuous intensity maps for the requested 
markers. The released weights of Eva can be found at https://huggingface.co/yandrewl/Eva. 
DINOv3 
For patient-level downstream analyses, real and virtual MIF maps were encoded with a 
frozen DINOv3 (Siméoni et al. 2025) ViT-Base/16 model. Each single-channel marker image 
was replicated along the channel dimension to form a three-channel input compatible with 
the model’s input format. DINOv3 produced one 768-dimensional feature vector per marker 
and patch. Patch-level marker features were then aggregated and fed into the MIL models to 
obtain patient-level features for downstream tasks. We applied the released weights of 
DINOv3 built in PyTorch Image Models (timm) at https://huggingface.co/timm. 
UNI2-h 
UNI (R. J. Chen et al. 2024) is a general-purpose pathology foundation model developed 
using the DINOv2 self-supervised learning framework. For the H&E-only reference in 
applicable patient-level tasks, we used its second-generation variant, UNI2-h, a ViT-H/14 
model pretrained on more than 350,000 H&E and IHC slides. Each H&E patch was 
represented by the 1,536-dimensional class-token embedding returned by the frozen 
encoder, and these patch-level features were subsequently used for case-level prediction.

Haiku 
Haiku (Cui et al. 2026) is a pretrained tri-modal representation model that aligns H&E, MIF,
