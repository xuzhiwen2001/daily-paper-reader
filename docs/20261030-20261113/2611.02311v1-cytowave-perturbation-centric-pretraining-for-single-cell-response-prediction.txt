Abstract 

Predicting gene expression under perturbations from single-cell data is a funda-mental task for modeling cellular responses, with applications in discovering gene function and designing therapeutics. However, existing methods often struggle to generalize across datasets due to differences in experimental protocols, biological contexts, and species. We present CytoWave , a perturbation-centric pretraining framework for single-cell genetic perturbation response prediction. We leverage large-scale cross-species perturbation datasets unified via orthology and train Cy-toWave in a two-stage manner. In the first stage, we learn reference control cell states via masked gene expression recovery on unperturbed cells. In the second stage, we pretrain the model to predict perturbed gene expression given control states and perturbation signals, together with a contrastive objective that aligns latent representations of perturbation responses with perturbation signals. We per-form strict dataset-held-out evaluation across multiple condition combinations and case studies of pathway activity changes, demonstrating consistent and effective performance of CytoWave in predicting single-cell genetic perturbation responses. 

1 Introduction 

Single-cell genetic perturbation experiments measure transcriptomic responses to targeted gene perturbations at single-cell resolution, providing a rich data source for characterizing gene function, regulatory relationships, and cellular state variation. Compared to unperturbed single-cell atlases, per-turbation data links perturbation targets to expression responses, enabling comparison of perturbation responses across cellular contexts and experimental conditions [1, 2]. These perturbation–response data provide a rich basis for learning generalizable representations of gene function and cellular response patterns [3, 4]. Advances in Perturb-seq and CRISPR-based technologies have led to the accumulation of large-scale perturbation datasets with increasing gene coverage and experimental diversity, supporting cross-dataset and cross-condition pretraining [1, 2, 5, 6]. In this context, a variety of methods have been developed for modeling single-cell perturbation responses. Existing methods for single-cell perturbation modeling have demonstrated strong potential in captur-ing gene expression responses and highlight the importance of accurately predicting cellular responses after genetic perturbations [3, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These approaches are often developed for specific datasets or experimental conditions using supervised training, or applied from general single-cell models to perturbation tasks through downstream fine-tuning. This evolving data landscape makes it possible and promising to extend existing approaches toward perturbation-centric pretraining, motivating the unification of large-scale datasets across diverse experimental conditions, gene identifiers, and species. With this growing data landscape, an important question is how to represent perturbation responses in a way that supports generalization across diverse cellular contexts. Modeling single-cell genetic perturbations requires going beyond fitting individual gene expression responses. Biologically, the transcriptional state of a cell is governed by gene regulatory programs that shape expression pat-terns across functionally related genes [17, 18]. Perturbations propagate through these programs to induce global transcriptional responses rather than isolated gene-level changes [1, 14]. Regulatory programs are often shared across cellular contexts, and perturbations acting through similar regulatory mechanisms can induce related patterns of cellular response across different cells. These observa-tions suggest that perturbation responses depend on both the initial cellular state and the applied perturbation. Rather than modeling this relationship independently for each cell, we parameterize per-turbation responses within a shared latent representation space, where perturbations are represented through cellular-state-dependent response patterns. This provides a unified way to represent related perturbation response patterns across different cellular contexts. To address these challenges, we propose CytoWave , a perturbation-centric pretraining framework for single-cell genetic perturbation modeling. We begin by systematically collecting and organizing large-scale perturbation datasets across studies and species, and unify them through orthology-based gene alignment and standardized metadata vocabularies. This enables pretraining on diverse perturbation data under a unified setting, with evaluation datasets held out from pretraining. Building on this unified data foundation, CytoWave models perturbation-dependent changes in latent cellular states. The model is trained in a two-stage manner. First, it learns reference control cell representations from unperturbed data. It then predicts perturbed gene expression from control states and perturbation signals, together with a contrastive objective that aligns latent state changes with perturbation signals. Together, these design choices define a unified framework for learning perturbation-aware representations and support generalization across genes and experimental conditions. The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows: • We collect and unify 42 single-cell genetic perturbation datasets across 19 studies and 2 species using orthology-based gene alignment and standardized metadata, resulting in a corpus of 24.7 million cells for pretraining and dataset-held-out benchmarking. • We introduce CytoWave, a perturbation-centric pretraining framework learning perturbation-aware representations by modeling perturbation-dependent changes in latent cellular states. • CytoWave generalizes under dataset-held-out evaluation, accurately predicting genetic perturbation responses and capturing perturbation-associated pathway activity changes.

2.2 Genetic Perturbation Models 

Many existing methods for single-cell perturbation modeling are formulated as dataset-specific supervised models, typically trained and evaluated within a single dataset and experimental set-ting [8, 9, 24, 10, 25]. CPA introduces a compositional representation in latent space, where perturba-tion responses are factorized and combined to support prediction under complex and combinatorial perturbation settings [8]. GEARS focuses on generalizing to unseen multi-gene perturbations by incor-porating gene interaction networks to capture dependencies among perturbed genes [9]. AttentionPert models interactions among perturbed genes through attention mechanisms, enabling it to capture joint effects under multiplexed perturbations [24]. In addition to these deep learning approaches, simple baselines including linear models and mean-based predictors are commonly included in perturbation benchmarks, and recent studies have evaluated their performance alongside more complex models under diverse settings [25, 26]. Beyond direct prediction of expression responses, another line of work models perturbation responses at the distribution level. Optimal transport-based methods learn population-level mappings between control and perturbed cells [27]. Flow-based approaches model population-level response distributions in expression space [28]. Probabilistic methods rely on latent variable formulations to describe perturbation responses and provide uncertainty-aware representations [29]. These approaches offer complementary perspectives by modeling perturbation-dependent changes beyond pointwise prediction. Building on these advances, CytoWave extends the modeling framework by introducing perturbation-centric pretraining across diverse datasets and condition combinations. Instead of focusing on a single experimental setting, it incorporates diverse large-scale perturbation data to learn perturbation-aware representations, improving robustness and generalization across various biological contexts. 33 Methods 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

Let G denote a gene vocabulary with |G| = G genes, and let C denote a shared vocabulary of categorical metadata tokens (e.g., cell line, species, perturbation technology, and other experimental conditions). We study large-scale pretraining for modeling single-cell genetic perturbation responses from a unified perturbation corpus. Each perturbation example is represented as a tuple (xi, yi, ui, ci),where xi ∈ RG

Control cells within the same experiment are treated as a reference population for perturbed cells under matched conditions. For each perturbed cell, a reference control profile is sampled from control cells sharing the same experimental context and metadata group (e.g., cell type and stimulation condition). These reference controls are used to define baseline cellular states for perturbation response modeling and evaluation. ui ⊆ G is the set of perturbed genes (single- or multi-gene), and ci = ( ci, 1, . . . , c i,S i ) is a sequence of metadata tokens. The objective of pretraining is to learn transferable representations of cellular state and perturbation responses that generalize across datasets, biological conditions, and perturbation combinations. For downstream perturbation response prediction, the model takes (xi, ui, ci) as input and predicts the post-perturbation expression yi. For evaluation, perturbation responses are measured as expression changes relative to the control profile. This formulation aims to learn perturbation responses that generalize across diverse cellular contexts, datasets, and perturbation combinations. 

3.2 CytoWave Architecture 

The model is implemented as a Transformer-based encoder–operator–decoder architecture. This design decomposes perturbation modeling into encoding cellular states, modeling perturbation-dependent changes, and decoding gene-level responses. As shown in Figure 1, CytoWave consists of a shared encoder E(·) that maps expression profiles and metadata to a latent cell state z ∈ Rd,a perturbation embedder S(·) that encodes perturbation sets ui, a perturbation operator T (·) that models perturbation-dependent changes in latent cellular states, and a query-based decoder D(·) that predicts gene expression values from latent states.

3.3 Training Objectives 

CytoWave is pretrained using a two-stage framework. In Stage 1, the model learns representations of cellular state by reconstructing masked gene expression from control cells. In Stage 2, the model learns perturbation-dependent state changes between reference control states and perturbed cellular states, with bidirectional objectives that combine direct perturbation response prediction and reverse perturbation signal alignment. 4.2 Baselines 

CytoWave is compared with a diverse set of baselines covering different modeling paradigms. Simple statistical baselines include Linear [25] and TrainMean, while basic machine learning baselines include BaseReg and BaseMLP [45]. Supervised perturbation-specific models include CPA [8] and GEARS [9]. Pretrained single-cell models are also evaluated on perturbation tasks. scGPT [7] is included to represent the finetuning performance of single-cell pretrained models. All pretrained models, including CytoWave, are finetuned on the downstream training split before evaluation. Representation-based transfer methods include GenePert [10] and scouter [20]. Recently proposed generative and pretrained frameworks, including scLDM [29], LPM [23], and GeneFormer [22], are additionally included to provide a comprehensive comparison across modeling paradigms. 

4.3 Results 

CytoWave is evaluated on gene-level perturbation response prediction. Performance is assessed using three complementary metrics. ∆PCC measures the correlation between predicted and ground-truth expression changes. ∆PCC 20 focuses on the most responsive genes. MAE quantifies the magnitude of prediction errors over genes with measurable expression activity in either the control or perturbed profile. A pathway-level analysis based on AUCell is included as a biological case study to examine whether predicted responses recover known pathway activities. An ablation study analyzes the contributions of different pretraining stages and metadata inputs. Results are averaged over three random seeds (0, 1, and 2). 5 Conclusion 

We present CytoWave, a large-scale perturbation-centric pretraining framework for single-cell genetic perturbation modeling. By unifying cross-species perturbation datasets through orthology alignment and adopting a two-stage self-supervised pretraining strategy, CytoWave learns perturbation-aware representations that generalize across genes and experimental contexts. Across multiple bench-marks, CytoWave achieves strong and consistent performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of perturbation-centric pretraining. These results suggest that incorporating perturbation data during pretraining improves the modeling of transcriptional responses and supports the capture of structured biological response patterns. B Data Curation and Preprocessing 

This section describes the data curation, identifier harmonization, metadata normalization, and preprocessing pipeline used to construct the orthology-aligned corpus for CytoWave, with evalua-tion datasets held out from pretraining. All steps are applied uniformly across datasets to ensure reproducibility, cross-study consistency, and compatibility across species. 

B.1 Gene Identifier Normalization and Reference Mapping 

Gene identifiers in raw scRNA-seq datasets are provided using heterogeneous naming conventions, including inconsistent capitalization and isoform-specific suffixes. To standardize gene identities, gene symbols are first normalized by removing isoform suffixes and extraneous formatting. Normalized gene symbols are mapped to species-specific Ensembl Gene IDs using organism-specific reference annotations. Human genes are mapped using the Human Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) reference, while mouse genes are mapped using Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) annota-tions together with Ensembl GRCm39 gene models. Only genes with valid Ensembl identifiers and orthology assignments are retained. This mapping establishes a stable and widely accepted identifier space for downstream processing. To enable cross-species integration, Ensembl Gene IDs are further mapped to orthology-level identi-fiers using Ensembl Compara protein homology annotations (release 115). Gene models are defined using Ensembl reference genome annotations for human (GRCh38, Ensembl release 113) and mouse (GRCm39, Ensembl release 113). Each retained gene is thus associated with a unified ortholog identifier shared across species. Genes without a valid orthology assignment are excluded from the dataset. 

B.2 Perturbation Target Harmonization 

Perturbation annotations are harmonized using the same identifier and orthology references to ensure alignment between perturbation targets and gene expression features. Cells annotated as negative controls are assigned a dedicated CONTROL perturbation token. For perturbed cells, target gene symbols are first normalized and then mapped to species-specific Ensembl Gene IDs using HGNC references for human datasets and MGI/Ensembl annotations for mouse datasets. Cross-species alignment is subsequently performed through Ensembl Compara protein homology annotations (release 115), which map species-specific Ensembl gene identifiers into a shared orthology space. Cells whose perturbation targets cannot be mapped to a valid ortholog identifier are removed. This procedure guarantees that all perturbations are represented in the same orthology-aligned token space as gene expression inputs. 

B.3 Metadata Standardization and Context Tokenization 

In addition to gene expression and perturbation labels, we curate a unified set of metadata tokens capturing biological and experimental context, including species, cell context, cell line, lineage, perturbation technology, disease status, stimulation condition, and stimulation time. Metadata fields are normalized using rule-based string matching against predefined keyword dic-tionaries. Each metadata category is mapped to a controlled vocabulary of discrete tokens. When dataset-specific metadata fields are missing or ambiguous, predefined default tokens are assigned. This design yields a fixed-length metadata token representation per cell, independent of the original dataset schema. 14 B.4 Cell and Gene Quality Control 

We apply several quality-control filters to remove low-quality or ambiguous observations: • Genes with zero expression across all cells are removed. • Genes without valid orthology assignments are removed. • Cells associated with unmapped perturbation targets are removed. • Cells with fewer than 500 detected genes are excluded. After filtering, all retained genes have valid orthology identifiers and non-zero expression in at least one cell, and all retained cells have valid perturbation annotations. 

B.5 Expression Normalization and Transformation 

Raw gene expression counts are normalized by library size on a per-cell basis. Specifically, counts are scaled such that the total expression per cell matches the median total count across the dataset. Median-based normalization reduces sensitivity to extreme library sizes while preserving relative expression magnitudes. Normalized expression values are subsequently transformed using a log(1 + x) function to stabilize variance and compress dynamic range. Post-normalization expression distributions are inspected to confirm that total counts are centered around the dataset median and that log-transformed values fall within a stable numerical range. 

B.6 Pathway Gene Set Resources 

For pathway-level analyses, we use the Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), release 2025.1. Human Hallmark gene sets are mapped to the same orthology space using Ensembl gene models (GRCh38, release 113) and Ensembl Compara homologies (release 115), enabling consistent pathway analysis across species. 

B.7 Preprocessing Validation and Provenance Tracking 

To ensure correctness and reproducibility, we perform systematic validation checks, including verification of reported versus computed numbers of detected genes per cell, verification of reported versus computed total counts per cell, and sanity checks on normalization scaling and log-transformed expression values. All preprocessing steps, filtering statistics, and reference resource versions are recorded as structured metadata within each processed dataset. The resulting curated datasets are stored in standardized 

.h5ad formats and used as inputs for downstream sparse tokenization and model pretraining. 

B.8 Conversion to Foundation-Friendly Zarr Representation 

After preprocessing and curation in .h5ad format, we further convert each dataset into a foundation-model-oriented zarr representation. This conversion is motivated by the need to support scalable, distributed pretraining over tens of millions of cells while maintaining strict schema consistency and efficient access to sparse gene expression and tokenized metadata. 

Motivation. While .h5ad provides a flexible container for exploratory analysis, it is not optimized for large-scale foundation model pretraining, which requires: (i) streaming-friendly access patterns, (ii) efficient storage of sparse expression matrices, (iii) explicit separation between continuous expres-sion values and discrete tokenized attributes, and (iv) strict schema guarantees across heterogeneous datasets. The zarr format satisfies these requirements by enabling chunked, compressed, and parallel I/O while remaining framework-agnostic.

B.13 Dataset Attributes and Provenance 

Each zarr dataset records global attributes including dataset name, number of cells, number of genes, creation timestamp, and a description of the storage format. These attributes enable transparent provenance tracking and facilitate automated dataset auditing. 

B.14 Consistency Verification and “Zarr as Ground Truth” 

To guarantee correctness of the conversion process, we enforce a strict consistency check between the source .h5ad file and the generated zarr representation. Summary statistics include numbers of cells, genes, unique orthology tokens, unique perturbations, and unique values for each metadata field. These are computed independently from both representations and required to match exactly. Only datasets that pass all consistency checks are admitted into the curated corpus used for pretraining and evaluation, with evaluation datasets held out from pretraining. The zarr representation is treated as the authoritative ground truth for all subsequent stages of data loading, tokenization, and model training. 

Summary Construction. For each dataset, we construct a structured summary capturing dataset scale, vocabulary cardinalities, and metadata diversity. These summaries are aggregated across datasets to provide a global overview of corpus composition and to support downstream analysis and reporting. 

B.15 Gene Coverage In Pretraining 

Figure 5 shows the distribution of gene occurrence frequencies during pretraining under sparse tokenization. In Stage 1, where training is performed on control cells, gene coverage is broadly distributed across the vocabulary, reflecting general transcriptional patterns. In Stage 2, perturbation-aware training shifts the distribution toward genes involved in perturbation responses, resulting in increased coverage of functionally relevant genes. Despite the use of sparse tokenization, the overall gene vocabulary remains well covered across both stages. These results indicate that the pretraining process captures both general gene expression structure and perturbation-specific signals, supporting the ability of CytoWave to generalize across genes and experimental settings.

C.5 Evaluation Dataset Selection 

To comprehensively evaluate the generalization capability of CytoWave, we select a diverse set of downstream evaluation datasets that span a wide range of biological contexts, perturbation scales, and experimental designs (Table 5). These datasets are strictly excluded from the pretraining corpus. Our selection criteria are guided by three principles: (i) coverage of heterogeneous biological conditions, (ii) diversity in perturbation complexity and data scale, and (iii) compatibility with standardized perturbation prediction tasks. Specifically, the chosen datasets include both cell-line and primary-cell experiments, multiple CRISPR modalities (CRISPR-KO and CRISPRi), and varying perturbation densities, ranging from small-scale transcription factor screens (e.g., DatlingerBock2017) to large-scale enhancer and regulatory perturbation studies (e.g., GasperiniShendure2019). Together, these datasets cover perturbation regimes with as few as single-digit cells per perturbation and as many as over one thousand cells per perturbation, enabling robust evaluation across low- and high-data regimes. We further include datasets with substantially different perturbation cardinalities, from fewer than ten unique perturbations (PapalexiSatija2021) to several hundred perturbations (GasperiniShendure2019), in order to assess the stability of model performance under both narrow and broad perturbation vocabularies. This diversity is critical for evaluating whether a model can generalize beyond densely sampled perturbations and avoid overfitting to dataset-specific perturbation distributions.

Pretraining Setup CytoWave is pretrained by a two-stage self-supervised strategy. In Stage 1, the model learns perturbation-agnostic representations from control cells through masked gene expression recovery with a masking ratio of 15%. The encoder is implemented as an 8-layer Transformer with 8 attention heads and model dimension d = 512 . CytoWave is pretrained 20 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 2e-4, weight decay of 0.01, and gradient clipping at 1.0. A cosine learning rate schedule with linear warmup is applied. In Stage 2, CytoWave conducts perturbation-aware representation learning on top of the pretrained encoder from stage 1. We use a 30% masking ratio in perturbation response expression mask and recovery. The designed masking first includes the top 300 gene expressions with the largest changes after perturbations and then randomly samples from the rest. The model predicts post-perturbation expression values for this selected gene set, which are later evaluated through expression changes relative to the control profile while simultaneously learning to recover masked perturbation targets. The total training loss is defined as the sum of the expression regression loss and the perturbation recovery loss, with a weighting factor of 5.0 applied to the latter. Stage 2 is trained for 10 epochs with a batch size of 24 and gradient accumulation of 4, using the same optimizer and learning rate as Stage 1. The InfoNCE denominator is computed within each mini-batch and does not gather negatives across gradient accumulation steps. Mixed-precision training with bfloat16 is enabled to improve training efficiency. All pretraining experiments were conducted on a cluster with 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs using distributed data parallel training. The complete two-stage pretraining pipeline required approximately two weeks of training time. 

G Limitation 

This study suggests that genetic perturbation data provides a qualitatively different and richer training signal than unperturbed single-cell atlases, as perturbations directly encode how cellular states respond under controlled perturbations. Representing perturbation responses in a shared latent space provides a structured approach to distinguish between intrinsic cell-state variation and perturbation-dependent responses. A key implication of this design is improved generalization in settings where direct supervision is scarce. By learning perturbation-dependent responses as latent state changes rather than dataset-specific conditional regressions, the model can reuse and compose learned response patterns across experimental conditions. Several limitations should be noted. Perturbation coverage remains uneven across genes and cell types, which may limit the model’s ability to capture gene-specific responses consistently across different contexts. Because CytoWave uses an orthology-aligned vocabulary, genes without valid orthology assignments are excluded, which may limit modeling of species-specific regulatory programs. In addition, while the learned representations reflect perturbation-associated patterns at the gene and pathway levels, they do not enforce explicit mechanistic constraints. Understanding how these representations relate to known regulatory or causal structures remains an open question for future investigation. Future work may extend CytoWave along several directions, including expanding perturbation coverage, incorporating multi-omic measurements, and exploring richer perturbation operators that capture temporal and nonlinear responses. We believe that such developments are essential for building foundation models that not only predict perturbation outcomes, but also support mechanistic understanding and hypothesis generation in complex biological systems. 25 H Broader Impact 

CytoWave is designed for modeling single-cell genetic perturbation responses and may support applications in functional genomics, disease modeling, and therapeutic discovery. By improving the ability to predict perturbation-associated transcriptional responses, such models may help prioritize experimental hypotheses and reduce experimental cost in biomedical research. We do not identify direct negative societal impacts associated with the proposed method beyond those generally related to machine learning models used in biological research. Incorrect perturbation predictions may nevertheless lead to misleading biological hypotheses if used without experimental validation. CytoWave is intended as a research tool and does not provide clinical or medical decision-making recommendations.
