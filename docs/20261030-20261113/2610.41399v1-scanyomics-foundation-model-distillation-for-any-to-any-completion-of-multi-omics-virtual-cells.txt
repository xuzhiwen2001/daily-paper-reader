# Abstract 

Recent advances in single-cell technologies have enabled the profiling of multiple molecular modal-ities, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and chromatin accessibility, providing a more compre-hensive understanding of cellular states. However, integrating heterogeneous modalities and predict-ing missing molecular information remain chal-lenging, particularly when paired measurements are limited. Here, we introduce scAnyOmics ,a generative framework that distills knowledge from a frozen single-cell foundation model into a multimodal student model. scAnyOmics inte-grates scRNA-seq, protein-marker measurements, and scATAC-seq into a shared representation, en-abling cross-modal prediction and reconstruction of missing modalities. We assembled 690,880 cells from six publicly available studies, cov-ering both paired and partially paired multi-omics measurements, to systematically evaluate its predictive performance. We further evaluated scAnyOmics on held-out donor data, where it outperformed baseline methods across multiple evaluation metrics. Beyond cross-modal recon-struction, we explored its applications in cell-type annotation, perturbation-response prediction, and biological age estimation. Overall, scAnyOmics provides a unified framework for integrating in-complete single-cell multi-omics data and trans-ferring pretrained biological knowledge, support-ing the development of virtual cell models across diverse cellular contexts and experimental plat-forms.

# 1. Introduction 

Cells are complex biological systems whose states are shaped by interactions among multiple molecular layers. Single-cell sequencing technologies now allow researchers to examine these layers, including gene expression, pro-tein markers, and chromatin accessibility, at unprecedented resolution. Each modality provides a different perspective on cellular identity and function. Gene expression reflects transcriptional activity, chromatin accessibility provides in-formation about gene regulation, and protein measurements reveal important functional characteristics of cells. Integrat-ing these measurements can therefore provide a more com-prehensive understanding of cellular states. However, most single-cell datasets contain only a subset of these modalities, making it difficult to build a unified representation of cells and predict molecular information that has not been directly measured. The growing interest in virtual cell models offers a new direction for addressing this challenge. Virtual cells aim to represent biological systems computationally and pre-dict cellular properties or responses under different condi-tions (Bunne et al., 2024). Recent studies, such as State and ProteinTalks, have demonstrated how computational models can predict cellular responses to perturbations and capture changes in molecular states (Adduri et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). These advances highlight the potential of computational models to support biological discovery and 

in silico experiments. An important step toward this goal is to develop models that can integrate different molecular measurements and reconstruct missing modalities. Such capabilities would allow researchers to study cellular states more comprehensively, even when only partial molecular information is available. Recent advances in single-cell multi-omics technologies have created opportunities to develop these models. However, generating fully paired multi-omics datasets remains technically demanding, and many existing datasets contain only partially overlapping modal-ities. Differences in measurement platforms and protein panels also make it difficult to combine data across studies. Consequently, computational models must be able to learn from both paired and unpaired measurements while dis-tinguishing genuinely missing information from observed values. Several computational methods have been developed to ad-dress multimodal integration and missing-data prediction. For example, totalVI jointly models RNA and protein mea-surements while accounting for technical variation (Gayoso et al., 2021). MultiVI extends this approach to paired and unpaired multimodal datasets and enables missing-modality inference (Ashuach et al., 2023). Similarly, scVAEIT incor-porates missingness information to integrate gene expres-sion, protein abundance, and chromatin accessibility across heterogeneous datasets (Du et al., 2022). These methods have established important foundations for single-cell multi-omics integration. Nevertheless, a key opportunity remains to investigate whether knowledge learned from large-scale pretrained models can further improve cross-modal predic-tion, particularly when training data are incomplete or vary across experimental platforms. Single-cell foundation models provide a promising way to incorporate such knowledge. Models including Geneformer and scGPT have shown that representations learned from large single-cell datasets can be transferred to downstream biological tasks (Theodoris et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2024). Universal Cell Embedding (UCE) further demonstrates how gene-expression profiles from different tissues and species can be mapped into a shared representation space (Rosen et al., 2026). More recently, HoloCell has explored multi-modal foundation modelling for integrating gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and protein markers, including the generation of missing modalities (Jiang et al., 2026). gether, these developments suggest that pretrained repre-sentations can provide useful biological information for multimodal learning. However, effectively transferring this information into a specialized model remains an important challenge, particularly when the goal is to make accurate predictions from heterogeneous and partially observed mea-surements. Knowledge distillation offers a practical approach to this challenge. Originally developed to transfer information from a pretrained teacher model to a student model (Hinton et al., 2015), knowledge distillation allows the student to learn from rich representations without requiring the teacher during inference. In single-cell multi-omics analysis, this approach could enable a student model to combine informa-tion from different molecular modalities while benefiting from the biological knowledge encoded by a foundation model. By keeping the teacher frozen and training a sepa-rate multimodal student, it becomes possible to investigate whether pretrained knowledge improves the reconstruction of missing molecular measurements. This strategy also creates an opportunity to distinguish the contribution of foundation-model representations from that of the student’s own multimodal learning. Evaluating these models requires careful attention to pre-diction accuracy and generalization. Previous studies have shown that single-cell foundation models do not consis-tently outperform simpler approaches across all datasets and tasks (Kedzierska et al., 2025). Similarly, recent perturbation-prediction benchmarks have demonstrated the importance of selecting appropriate baselines and evaluation metrics (Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025; Miller et al., 2026). Single-cell datasets also contain biological and technical dependencies, such as multiple cells originating from the same donor, which can affect performance estimates if they are not considered during evaluation (Zimmerman et al., 2021). Therefore, reliable assessment of multimodal predic-tion should include comparisons with established methods, evaluation across different molecular targets, and testing on independent biological samples or datasets. Here, we introduce scAnyOmics , a multi-teacher knowl-edge distillation framework for integrating single-cell multi-omics data and predicting missing molecular modalities. scAnyOmics leverages three frozen foundation models— UCE for gene expression, scLinguist for surface proteins, and Atacformer for chromatin accessibility—to transfer modality-specific knowledge into a unified multimodal stu-dent model. Through knowledge distillation, masked mul-timodal learning, and self-distillation, scAnyOmics learns shared cellular representations from incomplete molecular measurements, enabling cross-modal prediction and recon-struction. We assembled 690,880 cells from six publicly available studies , covering paired and partially paired mea-surements of gene expression, protein markers, and chro-matin accessibility. Using these datasets, we systematically evaluate cross-modal prediction and investigate how pre-trained representations and different learning strategies con-tribute to model performance. We further assess the quality of learned cellular representations through linear and non-linear prediction probes and evaluate model generalization across independent samples. Overall, scAnyOmics provides a unified framework for transferring knowledge from mul-tiple foundation models into multimodal cellular represen-tations, supporting the reconstruction of missing molecular information and the development of computational virtual cell models. 

# 2. Results 

2.1. Cross-modal reconstruction from incomplete multi-omics profiles 

We evaluated the scAnyOmics ability to reconstruct un-seen modalities from one or two available modalities. Us-ing 28,239 cells with matched RNA, antibody-derived tag (ADT), and chromatin-accessibility (ATAC) measurements in held-out samples, we masked the target modality and assessed all nine input–target combinations (Figure 2) . Re-construction accuracy was summarized by the median Spear-man correlation across 2,000 genes or 166 antibodies for RNA and ADT, respectively, and the median area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) across 8,000 genomic bins for ATAC. We compared the UCE-distilled student, trained on 482,102 cells from the original four-study assembly, with an otherwise comparable student without teacher distillation and with the better of ridge re-gression and 15-nearest-neighbour prediction for each task. The UCE-distilled model outperformed the simple baselines in all six tasks with RNA or ADT as the reconstruction target (Figure 2). Across these tasks, the improvement in median Spearman correlation was approximately 0.05–0.12. The largest difference occurred when ATAC was the sole input for ADT prediction ( ρ = 0 .22 versus 0.10 for the best simple baseline). ATAC-to-RNA prediction also improved (ρ = 0 .25 versus 0.16). Combining two observed modali-ties further improved RNA and ADT reconstruction in the evaluated settings: ADT prediction increased from 0.25 with RNA alone to 0.28 with RNA and ATAC, while RNA prediction reached 0.31 with ADT and ATAC, compared with 0.29 using ADT alone. The teacher-free student scored 0.07–0.14 lower than the UCE-distilled model across all six RNA and ADT tasks and was outperformed by the simple baselines in four of them. For ATAC prediction, scAnyOmics achieved above-chance performance across different input modalities, with median AUROC values ranging from 0.53 to 0.56 (Figure 2). 2.2. Antibody-level variation in RNA-to-protein prediction 

We next examined how RNA-to-ADT prediction varied across individual protein markers. In an analysis of 10,000 trimodal test cells, scAnyOmics achieved a higher Spear-man correlation than ridge regression for 125 of the 166 an-tibodies (75.3%; Figure 3a). The median correlation across antibodies was 0.25 for scAnyOmics and 0.19 for ridge re-gression. The improvement was concentrated among mark-ers with intermediate prediction accuracy, whereas several well-predicted lineage-associated markers showed similar performance under both methods. For CD19, the correla-tions were 0.81 for both models; for CD3, they were 0.79 and 0.77; and for CD14, 0.79 and 0.80, respectively. Ridge regression retained an advantage for 41 antibodies, includ-ing CD24. Both methods performed poorly for CX3CR1 (ρ = −0.26 for scAnyOmics and −0.30 for ridge regres-sion). The measured-versus-predicted profiles further character-ized the resolution of RNA-based protein reconstruction (Figure 3b–e). For CD19, CD3, and CD14, predicted ex-pression separated groups of cells with different measured marker levels, consistent with the high correlations observed for these antibodies. Within those groups, however, predic-tions occupied narrower ranges than the measurements. This pattern indicates that performance for these markers was driven largely by differences between cellular populations, while variation among cells with similar marker expression profiles was recovered less accurately. CX3CR1 showed weak correspondence between predicted and measured val-ues, consistent with its negative rank correlation. These results demonstrate substantial antibody-to-antibody hetero-geneity in reconstruction accuracy and distinguish recovery of broad population-level patterns from prediction of finer within-population variation. 

2.3. Effects of modality-specific teacher models and self-distillation 

To investigate how different foundation models contribute to cross-modal reconstruction, we compared scAnyOmics trained with each teacher separately (UCE, scLinguist, and Atacformer), with all three teachers combined, and with additional self-distillation (Figure 4). We used a student model without knowledge distillation as the baseline. Fig-ure 4 shows the improvement in prediction performance for each configuration, measured by changes in median Spear-man correlation for RNA and ADT and median AUROC for ATAC. Among the three foundation models, UCE provided the most consistent improvements across prediction tasks. Us-ing UCE alone improved all six RNA and ADT reconstruc-tion tasks, with gains of 0.07–0.14 in Spearman correlation. The largest improvement was observed when predicting ADT from RNA and ATAC together (+0.14). UCE also improved RNA prediction from ATAC alone (+0.11) and ADT prediction from RNA alone (+0.11). These results highlight the value of pretrained transcriptomic representa-tions for predicting molecular information across different 5scAnyOmics: Foundation-Model Distillation for Any-to-Any Completion of Multi-omics Virtual Cells 

modalities. Combining all three teachers also improved RNA and ADT reconstruction across all six tasks. The gains ranged from 0.07 to 0.11 for RNA prediction and from 0.04 to 0.10 for ADT prediction. Atacformer provided an additional advantage for chromatin accessibility, improving RNA-to-ATAC prediction by 0.06 AUROC when used alone. Self-distillation further improved RNA-to-ATAC prediction ,increasing the gain from +0.03 with all three teachers to +0.08, corresponding to an AUROC of 0.62 compared with 0.54 for the teacher-free baseline. The self-distillation model also maintained improvements of 0.08–0.11 across all three RNA reconstruction tasks. The effects of knowledge distillation varied across modal-ities. While UCE consistently improved RNA and ADT predictions, scLinguist alone did not improve reconstruction performance in the evaluated tasks. Adding self-distillation also reduced performance in some ADT prediction settings. Overall, these results demonstrate that foundation-model knowledge can improve cross-modal reconstruction, with UCE contributing the most consistent benefits and self-distillation providing further gains in chromatin accessibility prediction. 

# 3. Methods 

3.1. Study design and prediction task 

We designed scAnyOmics as a virtual cell framework to integrate single-cell multi-omics data, including gene ex-pression (RNA), surface proteins (ADT), and chromatin ac-cessibility (ATAC), as presented in Figure 1A . scAnyOmics learns a shared representation of cellular states from these modalities, enabling the prediction of missing molecular measurements when only partial information is available. For each cell, we used 2,000 genes, 166 protein markers, and 8,000 chromatin-accessibility bins. Since different datasets contain different combinations of modalities, we used mea-surement masks to distinguish available data from missing modality. During training, measured modalities could also be hidden from the model and used as prediction targets. For protein measurements, we further recorded which pro-teins were measured in each cell, ensuring that unmeasured proteins were excluded from training and evaluation. scAnyOmics uses separate encoders to process each modal-ity and combines their information into a shared cellular representation (Figure 1C) . A Transformer incorporates in-formation from other cells within the same sample group, while knowledge distillation transfers pretrained representa-tions from respective frozen foundation model to the student model, in which we choose UCE for RNA, scLinguist for ADT and Atacformer for ATAC (Figure 1B) . Separate de-coders then reconstruct the molecular measurements for each modality. Once trained, the student model can infer missing modalities without requiring the teacher-foundation model. After knowledge-distillation training, scAnyOmics also aims to resolve the downstream tasks including cell-type annotation and perturbation response (Figure 1D) .

3.2. Datasets 

We assembled 690,880 cells from six Gene Expression Om-nibus (GEO) series profiling human peripheral blood (Ta-ble 1) : GSE158013 (59,151 cells), GSE200417 (68,138), GSE214546 (367,955), GSE236422 (181,518), GSE156478 (5,527) and GSE166188 (8,591). Four series (GSE158013, GSE214546, GSE236422 and GSE166188) profiled periph-eral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) or blood-derived T cells with trimodal assays (TEA-seq or DOGMA-seq). GSE200417 compared DOGMA-seq with CITE-seq in activated pan-T cells, and GSE156478 contributed one CITE-seq library. The cohorts differ in biological state. GSE158013 and GSE214546 profiled unstimulated PBMCs. GSE200417 profiled T cells stimulated for 12 h with CD3/CD28/CD2 activator, IL-1 β and IL-23, with PGE2 and/or TGF-β. GSE236422 profiled healthy-donor T cells treated in vitro with bortezomib, lenalidomide, dexametha-sone or DMSO for 4, 24 or 72 h, plus an untreated 0 h sample. The GSE156478 and GSE166188 libraries were the control (16 h IL-2) arms of a CD3/CD28 stimulation experiment. The data were organized into 47 sample-directory blocks, which correspond to source libraries or processing units, not to confirmed donors. The 47 blocks derive from 44 GEO sample (GSM) accessions; for three GSE200417 DOGMA-seq libraries, cells lacking an ADT barcode match were stored as separate RNA–accessibility blocks. Donor iden-tity was available from GEO for only two series. All five GSE158013 TEA-seq libraries and both 10x Multiome li-braries came from a single donor (HMN200910). The 19 GSE214546 libraries came from 16 donors and one control individual. The donors comprised eight adults aged 55–63 years and eight 11-year-old children, all female; the con-trol individual (IMM19, male, age not reported) contributed three libraries. Donor identity could not be resolved for the other four series. The combined matrix contained 309,724 cells with all three modalities, 353,018 RNA–ADT cells, 26,094 RNA– accessibility cells and 2,044 RNA-only cells (Table 2) .The RNA-only cells were GSE158013 10x Multiome cells whose barcodes could not be matched to fragment records. RNA–ADT cells arose in two ways. Some came from CITE-seq libraries (GSE200417 GSM6032900 and GSE156478). The others were trimodal-assay cells without matched frag-ments. We trained B, B-norm, and B-tech separately for 30 epochs using random seed 17 and the AdamW optimizer. The learning rate was 10 −3 and weight decay was 10 −4. We clipped gradient norms at five. All three configurations used the same 477,439 training cells, validation and test sets, UCE cache, random seed, and training budget. We selected the checkpoint with the highest mean across datasets of median Spearman correlation for centered-log ADT predictions from RNA-only inputs. The selected epochs were 11 for B, 28 for B-norm, and 20 for B-tech. We then used those checkpoints to evaluate ATAC prediction as well. Each configuration was trained with one random seed.

# 4. Discussion 

We set out to test whether knowledge from a pretrained single-cell foundation model can help a smaller model fill in missing molecular measurements. Our results say yes, but with clear limits. The main finding is that distillation from UCE made a real difference. The student trained with UCE predicted RNA and ADT better than ridge regression and nearest-neighbour averaging in all six RNA and ADT tasks. The same stu-dent trained without a teacher did worse than these simple methods in four of the six. The improvement therefore does not come from the student’s architecture alone; it depends on what the student learns from the teacher. This matters because recent benchmarks have found that complex single-cell models often fail to beat simple baselines (Kedzierska et al., 2025; Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025). In our setting, the simple baselines were beaten only once pretrained knowl-edge was added. The gains were largest when chromatin accessibility was the input. On their own, ATAC data are sparse and binary, and linear models or nearest neighbours make little use of them. Once ATAC was mapped into the representation shaped by the RNA teacher, it predicted protein and RNA levels much better. In practice, scAnyOmics may be most useful for datasets that measured chromatin accessibility but not RNA or proteins. The per-antibody analysis shows what this level of accuracy means in practice. Median correlations of 0.2 to 0.3 hide a wide range: lineage markers such as CD19, CD3 and CD14 were predicted well, while some antibodies were not predicted at all. Even for the well-predicted markers, the model mainly placed each cell in the right population and reproduced less of the variation between similar cells. Predicted protein levels are therefore suited to analyses at the level of cell populations, such as labelling cell types or checking which markers a population expresses. They should not replace measured values for individual cells. Predicting chromatin accessibility remains the weakest part. Every method we tested, including ours, reached AUROC values only slightly above chance. Single-cell accessibility at 10-kb resolution is very sparse, and much of the difference between cells may be technical noise that no input can predict. Predicting accessibility for groups of similar cells, or for regions near genes, may be a more realistic goal than predicting every bin in every cell. Not every teacher helped. UCE improved RNA and ADT completion, and Atacformer and self-distillation improved RNA-to-ATAC prediction, but scLinguist lowered perfor-mance in every task. One possible reason is that antibody panels differ widely between studies, from 45 to 154 an-tibodies, so the protein teacher sees a different and often small part of the panel in each cell. Adding more teachers is therefore not automatically better. Each teacher needs to be checked on its own, and the weight given to each one needs tuning. This study has several limitations. First, the test cells came from held-out samples, but donor identity was unknown for most studies and one donor appeared in both training and 13 scAnyOmics: Foundation-Model Distillation for Any-to-Any Completion of Multi-omics Virtual Cells 

test samples, so we cannot claim that the results carry over to new individuals. Second, each model was trained once with a single random seed, so small differences, of about 0.01 to 0.02, should not be over-interpreted. Third, we com-pared scAnyOmics only with simple baselines; comparisons with established methods such as totalVI, MultiVI and sc-VAEIT are still needed (Gayoso et al., 2021; Ashuach et al., 2023; Du et al., 2022). Fourth, all data came from human peripheral blood, much of it from stimulated or drug-treated T cells, and it is not yet known how well the approach works in other tissues. 

# 5. Conclusion 

scAnyOmics combines a multimodal student model with frozen single-cell foundation models to fill in missing RNA, protein and chromatin-accessibility measurements. Distilla-tion from the UCE RNA model improved RNA and protein completion for every combination of inputs we tested and lifted the student above simple baselines, while chromatin accessibility remained hard to predict for all methods. The completed profiles capture differences between cell popula-tions better than differences between individual cells. With donor-level evaluation, comparisons with established meth-ods and better use of the protein and chromatin teachers, this approach could make partially measured single-cell datasets more useful for building virtual cell models.
