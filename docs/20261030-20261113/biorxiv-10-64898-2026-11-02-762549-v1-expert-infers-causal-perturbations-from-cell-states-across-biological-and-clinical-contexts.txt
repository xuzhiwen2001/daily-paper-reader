ABSTRACT 
Pooled single-cell CRISPR screens have driven substantial effort toward models predicting the 
response to a perturbation. We posed the inverse question: which perturbation produced an observed 
cell state? Here we developed ExPert (Extracting Perturbations), which learns one prototype per 
perturbation in a latent space shared by 12 cellular contexts, trained on 8,964 perturbations and 19.5 
million cells, and ranks 19,895 candidate genes per query, over half absent from training. ExPert more 
than doubled held-out-context recall over the best existing method with 1,000-fold less compute, and 
transferred without retraining to drugs, protein degradation and combinations. In alcohol-associated 
liver disease it implicated mRNA export, unchanged in expression, and we experimentally confirmed 
nuclear poly(A) retention and isoform switching. In primary mitochondrial disease it recovered the

INTRODUCTION 
A cell that has drifted out of its normal state, whether in a patient biopsy or a culture dish, arrives as an 
endpoint whose cause is unlabeled and often inaccessible. The default for characterizing such an 
endpoint against an appropriate control is computing differentially expressed genes, which reflects the 
downstream consequences of a perturbation and not necessarily the upstream driver(s) that produced 
it. 
 
Such endpoints are most often interpreted against static single-cell atlases, which now span hundreds 
of millions of cells across tissues, organisms, and conditions1–3. Models trained on them learn 
low-dimensional representations of cell state that support cell-type classification, reconstruct 
developmental hierarchies, identify disease states, and transfer across species4–8. In specific cases, 
such approaches have recovered aspects of causal structure from observational data alone, through 
in silico gene deletion and network-based simulations6,9. However, these models do not always 
outperform linear baselines in predicting measured perturbation responses10, a limitation attributed in 
part to the training data rather than model capacity. Pooled single-cell CRISPR screens break that 
limitation by measuring the consequences of an intervention directly, coupling a genetic perturbation to 
a single-cell transcriptomic readout11–18. They now operate at genome scale across millions of cells 
and several thousand targeted genes19, with comparable screens extending to primary human cells20, 
chemical perturbations21,22, cytokine stimulation23, and in vivo screens24. Unlike a static atlas, every 
state in a perturbation atlas has a known cause, so an unexplained state can be matched against it to 
ask which measured perturbation most likely produced it, the question we tackle in this study. 
 
Almost all modeling built on these data has addressed the forward problem: given a perturbation, 
predict the resulting gene expression state. Early approaches regressed expression onto perturbation 
identity13 or factorized responses into gene programs25,26. More recent approaches have pursued 
broader predictive capabilities, including compositional autoencoders27–29, graph-based propagation 
through gene networks30,31, optimal-transport methods32, transformers pretrained on observational 
data33,34, language-model embeddings of gene function35, and response models trained on 
perturbation data at scale36. Although prediction accuracy has improved, it is often at the expense of 
linear interpretability. Others derive perturbation responses from quantities that are directly 
interpretable, such as gene–gene covariances in unperturbed cells37. Across these approaches, 
generalization to unseen contexts and combinations remains limited10, and none is designed to infer 
which perturbation produced a given state.  
 
Disease presents the inverse problem, yet far less attention has been paid to it. Two recent methods 
attempt this problem with Perturb-seq reference data, assigning query cells to reference perturbations 
under explicit uncertainty38 or reconstructing a cell-state signature as a combination of perturbation 
effects20. Both build on the Connectivity Map and its L1000 expansion, which match a query 
transcriptional signature against a library of bulk perturbational profiles39,40. Each fits its reference 
within a single screen rather than across screens, and thus both report that perturbation effects 
transfer poorly between cellular contexts20,38.  
 
Here we present ExPert (Extracting Perturbations), a single model trained across 22 single-cell 
CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screens spanning 12 cellular contexts (different cell lines, stimulation 
conditions, and human donors), comprising 61,445 perturbation–context pairs, 8,964 unique 
perturbations, and 19.5 million cells. ExPert learns a shared latent representation, or “prototype,” for 
each perturbation across different contexts. By incorporating prior knowledge of gene function, it can 
also score and predict the effects of perturbing genes not tested in existing screens. ExPert more than 
doubled the top-10 recall of the best existing method in contexts held out from training, used three 
orders of magnitude less compute, and ranked 19,895 candidate gene perturbations for every query,

RESULTS 
A single corpus spans cellular contexts and perturbation screens 
A perturbation’s effect in one context may be different in another20,41. We reasoned that a model 
trained across diverse contexts could learn the shared perturbation component capable of 
generalizing to new contexts. We therefore set out to maximize the diversity of cellular contexts (cell 
line, stimulation condition, and donor) a single model sees rather than the depth of any one screen. 
We pooled 22 large-scale single-cell CRISPRi screens into one corpus (Figure 1A; Figure S1; Table 
S1A): ten screens in nine cell lines (K562, HEK293T, HCT116, KOLF2.1J, hESC, TeloHAEC, Jurkat, 
RPE1, and HepG2) and twelve in primary CD4⁺ T cells from four donors in three stimulation states, 
giving 12 contexts and 31.8 million perturbed cells19,20,41–45. Input features to ExPert were the 3,096 
genes that best separated perturbations within each dataset (Figure S1F, Supplementary note 2; 
Methods). Of 19,191 perturbations measured in at least one screen, we retained the 8,964 present in 
at least 60% of contexts (Figure 1A, right), so that every perturbation the model learns has been 
observed in the majority of contexts. The retained perturbations span transcription factors, signaling 
components, chromatin regulators, metabolic enzymes, and protein-complex subunits (Table S1C). 
The 19.5 million cells carrying these perturbations formed the training set (Methods), comprising 
61,445 perturbation–context pairs. 
 
Perturbations were unevenly represented, ranging from tens of cell–dataset observations to more than 
ten thousand (Figure S1A). Common essential genes (DepMap co-essentiality46,47) accounted for 47% 
of the 1,004 least-represented perturbations, compared with 6% of the remaining 7,960 (Figure S1B). 
For these, sparsity likely reflected perturbation-induced lethality rather than a technical problem with 
the screen. Rather than removing these perturbations, we treated low representation as a signal and 
used external knowledge to better position them and recover their biological context (Methods). 
 
ExPert learns one prototype per perturbation 
We developed ExPert to learn a single representation, per perturbation, that holds across contexts 
(Figure 1B,C). We define that representation as the perturbation’s “prototype”: one direction in a latent 
space shared by every context, toward which all cells carrying that perturbation are drawn. Four 
features of the data make this goal challenging. First, baseline gene expression state of each cell type 
and the technical signature of each screen dominate the transcriptome signal, such that cells group 
largely by dataset rather than by perturbation. Second, a perturbation’s effect is only partly shared 
across contexts, since the same knockdown can act differently in distinct cell types (e.g., a T cell 
versus a hepatocyte) (Figure S1C). Third, responses to a single-gene perturbation are weak relative 
to a cell’s global expression profile (Figure S1D). Fourth, the effect size of a perturbation also varies 
from cell-to-cell, so cells cannot be treated equally in training (Figure S1E).

We reasoned that a model constrained to a single prototype per perturbation would be forced to find 
the common component across contexts. Relaxing that constraint in favor of a conventional classifier 
cost held-out accuracy under an otherwise identical model (Table S2A; Table S3A,D). We assigned 
each of the 8,964 perturbations in the training data a prototype in a 2,048-dimensional latent space 
(Figure 1D), with dimension and encoder depth set by a parameter sweep of both (Table S2B). 
 
ExPert compresses each cell’s transcriptomic profile into a vector in that latent space (encoding) and 
reconstructs the observed gene expression counts from this vector (decoding). Throughout this work, 
ablations are measured by held-out top-10 recall (top-10 hereafter), always on contexts unseen during 
training. We centered each cell on the control cells of its own dataset before encoding, removing the 
baseline expression of its cell type and screen19,48. An uncentered input cost fit (-28%) and held-out 
transfer (-57%), even with an invariance term to compensate (Table S2D). Z-scoring or projecting the 
dataset-specific offset out of the latent space were each worse than centering (Table S2D). We 
conditioned the decoder on a context embedding to absorb the remaining technical variation, rather 
than removing context adversarially. Adversarial removal cost up to 7.3% of held-out top-10 and fit its 
own training cells worse by up to 31.7%, consistent with capacity diverted into invariance rather than a 
failure to converge (Table S2C). 
 
To draw each cell toward the prototype of its perturbation and away from all others, we deployed a 
Proxy Anchor objective49. We chose this approach over a softmax head because softmax stopped 
learning once the correct prototype won by a clear margin, whereas Proxy Anchor kept pushing the 
other prototypes away (Figure S10). A reconstruction term kept the latent space tethered to the 
observed gene expression counts the model had to decode and remained the largest term in the loss 
even at half weight; cutting its share of the gradient made the model overfit, gaining fit (34%) and 
losing transfer (-40%) (Table S2A). We weighted cells by knockdown effect size measured with 
Mixscale⁴⁰ (weighted between 0.1-1; Methods) rather than discarding cells with weak effects. Without 
the weighting, the model gained fit (20%) and lost transfer (-12%), suggesting that it was overfitting to 
low-effect-size cells when they were not downweighted (Tables S3B, S2B). We also predicted per-cell 
effect size as an auxiliary output, without the perturbation label and differential-expression step that 
Mixscale requires. The prediction recovered the measured ground-truth value with an average 
correlation of 0.74 on essential screens (Figure S11A), so every query cell received an estimate of 
perturbation strength alongside its ranking. 
 
So far each prototype was positioned only by the cells carrying its perturbation, which could leave 
perturbations with few or weakly perturbed cells poorly placed. We therefore drew on external 
knowledge of gene function as priors: language-model embeddings of gene annotation (e.g. 
GenePT50), STRING protein-protein interactions51,52 and DepMap co-essentiality46,47; how these were 
built and selected is described with the zero-shot results (Methods). We supplied them only as 
“neighborhoods”, that is, the set of genes most similar to each perturbation in the prior, rather than as 
coordinates for where its prototype should sit.  
 
For each gene perturbation present in training, we took its 25 nearest neighbors in each prior and 
pulled the prototype toward the mean of those neighbors’ prototypes weighted by similarity. We scored 
each perturbation by the number of training cells it contributed multiplied by the number of datasets 
those cells came from, and swept how far down that ranking this pull should reach. Held-out top-10 
hits rose steeply over the first few percent of that ranking, flattened between 6 and 11%, and fell away 
once better-supported perturbations began to be moved, meaning the priors helped where training 
support was low and hurt where it was not. We therefore applied the pull to the 1,004 least-supported 
perturbations (11.2% of 8,964; Figure S2A). How the pull was parameterized made little difference: a 
weight decaying with evidence, a gate keyed on prior agreement, three evidence statistics, and five 
pull strengths all fell within run-to-run variation (Table S2D). Only extreme pull weights reversed the 
gain (Table S2D). Pulling every trained prototype toward its neighbors raised top-10 hits on held-out 
queries for the K562 cell type by 2.2% (0.1889 versus 0.1849, p = 0.042), and restricting that pull to

During training, the priors improved top-10 performance only when the ground-truth data set each 
prototype’s position and the prior adjusted it, not when the prior set the position and the data adjusted 
it afterward. Both alternatives of the latter cost accuracy: fixing prototypes to the prior embeddings and 
learning a mapping onto them fell 4.8% below a conventional classifier head, which the prototype 
constraint outperformed (Tables S2A, S3C), and initializing the prototype table from the priors cost 
7.0% against random initialization (Table S3B).  
 
Since the priors helped only in specific cases, we asked how the learned prototype structure relates to 
the structure the priors encode. Pairwise similarity in the strongest prior tracked similarity among 
learned prototypes at only Spearman ρ = 0.17, yet the two agreed on nearest neighbors 32 times 
more often than chance (Figure 1E). The priors therefore carried local neighbor identity but not global 
geometry. The priors could not leak into the rest of the model: they carried no gradient and moved only 
the 1,004 least-supported prototypes, leaving the other 7,960 positioned by expression alone 
(Methods). Because that local agreement is what the neighborhood pull already relies on, the same 
graph can place a perturbation the model never saw, provided the prior covers it: we set its prototype 
to the mean of its nearest trained neighbors and call these filled prototypes, or fills (Figure 1B; gray 
diamonds). Filling added 10,931 perturbations to the 8,964 learned during training, without retraining; 
9,874 of the fills were measured in at least one screen and are used for evaluation below (Methods). 
 
The result is a single table of 19,895 prototypes, one per gene perturbation, in a 2,048 dimensional 
latent space shared by every context. For inferring perturbation from cell state, ExPert requires only 
this table and the encoder. A query cell is centered on its own controls, encoded to a latent vector, and 
scored against all prototypes in one pass by cosine similarity (Methods), without access to the training 
cells and without retraining. The model, the prototype table, and the code are available as a Python 
package (expert, PyPI) and at https://github.com/GoyalLab/ExPert. Model hyperparameters are 
documented in Table S4. 
 
ExPert recovers perturbations in held-out cellular contexts at scale 
We first asked whether ExPert recovers single-gene perturbations from cells it had not seen, both in 
contexts (cell line, stimulation condition, or donor) it was trained on, and in contexts it was not. We 
benchmarked different methods on the largest subset of the corpus that every method could complete 
within a realistic compute budget, 1,157 perturbations and 2,741,678 cells, and evaluated across 
twelve “folds” (Methods). Each “fold” is one train/held-out split of the same corpus, withholding entire 
datasets or contexts rather than a random sample of cells to avoid leakage. Held-out cells were scored 
either in distribution (ID), from a dataset seen in training, or out of distribution (OOD), where the whole 
context had been withheld. We report macro-F1, mean reciprocal rank (MRR), and top-k (5, 10, 100) 
recall per dataset and fold, scoring pseudobulks of held-out cells unless stated otherwise (Figure 2). 
 
Scoring existing methods on this task required two accommodations. Of the methods we compared, 
only RNA fingerprinting38 is designed explicitly for the inverse direction, mapping query cells onto a 
reference of measured perturbation responses. The rest predict forward, so we adapted each method 
to run in the inverse direction, predicting the state of every candidate perturbation (forward problem) 
and ranking candidates by similarity to the query (Methods). Most are also designed to be fit to a 
single screen (RNA fingerprinting38, CellOT32, GEARS31, Scouter35 and GenePert53). For all methods, 
we trained one model for each dataset-fold combination, and scored test cells without providing cell 
origin information to the method (Methods). Models that can train across datasets (ExPert, XGBoost54, 
logistic regression, scGPT34 and the pooled centroid) were fit once per fold on all datasets and scored 
in a single pass over the shared class space (Methods). We also included three baselines that bound 
what is achievable without a model: Random, which ranks candidates uniformly; Frequency, which

ExPert ranked first on every ID and OOD metric (Figure 2A). In distribution (ID), several methods fit 
the training screens well: the RNA fingerprinting (ensemble), the nearest-centroid baseline, and 
XGBoost reached top-10 recall of up to 0.44 on held-out cells of seen datasets, compared to ExPert's 
0.75 (Figure 2A). None of the methods retained their performance once a context was withheld. OOD, 
ExPert reached an F1 of 0.27, MRR of 0.38, and top-10 recall of 0.48, against 0.12, 0.17 and 0.21 for 
RNA fingerprinting (ensemble), with every other method at or below the nearest-centroid baseline 
(0.07, 0.13 and 0.19). Measured against random ranking of the 1,157 candidates, ExPert's OOD 
performance was 56-fold above chance for top-10 recall, 57-fold for MRR, and 315-fold for F1, more 
than twice the enrichment of the next-best method (RNA fingerprinting ensemble 24-, 25-, and 
138-fold) (Figure 2B). 
 
The twelve folds tested generalization along three axes: a new stimulation condition within an 
otherwise seen context (same donor, different timepoint), a new cell line or cell type, and a new donor. 
Held-out stimulation conditions were the easiest case for all methods and preserved the same 
ordering (ExPert top-10 recall 0.75, MRR 0.63, F1 0.50; RNA fingerprinting (ensemble) 0.36, 0.31, 
0.24; Figure S3A). Generalization to unseen cell lines and cell types was more difficult, but ExPert 
maintained a wide margin on evaluation metrics: top-10 recall 0.43, MRR 0.31 and F1 0.20, ~2.3-fold 
higher than the best other method on recall (nearest-centroid baseline, 0.18) and roughly 3-fold RNA 
fingerprinting (ensemble) on F1 (0.07), whose recall (0.17) did not exceed the baseline (Figure 2C, 
left). Held-out donors were the hardest split for every method, ExPert included, consistent with the 
donor-specific responses reported in the original study20. However, ExPert’s margin persisted: top-10 
recall 0.26 against 0.11 for the next-best method, MRR 0.18 against 0.07, and F1 0.11 against 0.04 
(Figure 2C, right). Across folds, ExPert's median increase in top-10 recall over the next-best method 
was 2.14 times ID and 2.38 times OOD (Figure S3B). Masking the CRISPRi target gene in each 
query cell lowered recovery for all methods, including ExPert (6–11%), but ExPert still maintained a 
2.1-fold lead over the next-best method (Figure S3C). The learned prototype space therefore 
transferred along every axis of the corpus. 
 
ExPert’s margin over other methods held at the single-cell level, though absolute recovery fell for 
every method: ExPert’s median OOD top-10 recall on unseen cell lines fell from 0.43 on pseudobulks 
to 0.22, and RNA fingerprinting (ensemble) from 0.16 to 0.07 (Figure S3D). Further analysis revealed 
that the classification failures concentrated in weakly perturbed cells. Restricting the OOD evaluation 
to cells above increasing thresholds of measured knockdown efficiency raised ExPert's top-10 recall 
from 0.19 to 0.27, MRR from 0.12 to 0.21, and F1 from 0.07 to 0.11, roughly twice the improvement 
over any other method (Figure 2D, left). Within cells, knockdown effect size predicted retrieval for 
every learned method far above a shuffled null, and translated into the largest absolute gain for 
ExPert: its top-10 hit rate rose from 6% of cells in the weakest knockdown quartile to 30% in the 
strongest, compared with 2% to 12% for RNA fingerprinting (ensemble) (Figure 2D, right). A random 
subsample of the same sample size at each threshold showed no such rise (Figure S3E), so the 
effect can be attributed to perturbation strength and not to sample size. RNA fingerprinting left many 
cells unassigned (20% ID, 54% OOD), but ExPert’s lead held when both methods were restricted to 
the cells it assigned, and at every confidence threshold (Figures S4A–S4C). ExPert predicts 
perturbation strength for every query cell, including cells from unseen contexts (Figure 1B, Figure 
S11), so each call carries its own estimate of confidence.

ExPert zero-shots perturbations absent from training 
We placed a perturbation prototype absent from training at the mean of its nearest trained neighbors in 
the prior (Figure 3A). We reasoned that if the priors carry information about response, that placement 
should land near where the gene’s own prototype would have been; if they do not, it is arbitrary, and 
retrieval would be no better than random ranking. This is zero-shot identification in the sense used for 
image classes described only by attributes55,56, with gene priors taking their role.  
 
ExPert placed each perturbation absent from training by the same pull toward prior neighbors used to 
augment weakly supported prototypes during training (Figure 1B). Genes not measured in any screen 
carried no evidence, so the rotation ran to completion and their prototypes took the mean of their 
neighbors. Those neighbors came only from the remaining 7,960 best-supported trained perturbations 
(cross-fitted; Methods). These filled perturbations were excluded from training by the 
context-coverage filter, but 9,874 of them were measured in at least one screen, so ground-truth labels 
existed for them (Figure 1C; Methods). We asked whether ExPert ranked the correct gene in the 
top-10, against the full 19,895-gene vocabulary (chance 10/19,895), with a null in which each fill was 
assigned to a shuffled gene. Perturbations absent from training were recovered at 26.2-fold over 
chance overall, with the shuffled null at chance (Figure 3B). The gain depended on the screen the 
query came from: 4–17-fold in the primary T cell, iPSC, and genome-wide HEK293T and HCT116 
screens, where most such genes have weak effects, and 520–900-fold in the essential-gene panels of 
HepG2, RPE1, Jurkat, and K562, whose perturbations are strong (Figure 3B). Top-10 recovery held 
when the query's cell line, donor, or condition had also been withheld from training, at 13.5-fold overall 
(Figure 3B, OOD). 
 
The 10,931 prior-defined prototypes were not confined to a separate region of the space. Each sat at 
the similarity-weighted average of its 25 nearest neighbors’ prototypes, and together they spread 
across almost all clusters of the trained-prototype tree (Figure 3C). Although overall similar, missing 
prototypes lay closer to the mean prototype direction than trained prototypes did (average angle 85.8° 
vs 89°), which bounds how finely two genes absent from training can be discriminated (Figure S6A). 
Concentrating weight on the nearest neighbors mattered more than how many neighbors were used, 
raising zero-shot recovery 9% ID and 16% OOD at essentially no cost to trained perturbations 
(Figures S6B and S6C; Supplementary note 3). Fills had to be present during training as negatives; 
adding them only afterwards displaced trained perturbations from the top-10 (Figure S6E).

produced, testing five raw text sources, three description generators, and thirteen embedding

ExPert recovers combinatorial perturbations without training on them 
We questioned whether ExPert, trained only on single perturbations, recognizes combinations of 
perturbations, using four double-perturbation datasets. On two double-knockdown screens using 
CRISPRi (Replogle et al.60, n = 10) or Cas13d (CaRPool-Seq61, n = 124), queried without retraining 
(Figure 3G), we scored whether either constituent ranked in the top-k of the 8,347 candidates 
common to all methods. ExPert ranked at least one constituent first for 14.9% of queries, 1.7-fold more 
often than RNA fingerprinting38, and within the top-500 for 75%, while centroids and random ranking 
were near zero at the top ranks (Figure 3H).  
 
We then asked whether the prototypes extend to gain of function. CRISPRa, which activates rather 
than represses its targets, tested a direction of perturbation absent from ExPert’s training. On 142 
single CRISPRa perturbations from two screens, ExPert ranked the activated gene in the top-10 of 
8,347 candidates 15.5% of the time, against 8.5% for RNA fingerprinting and 0.12% by chance 
(Figure S5A,B). Given that ExPert could read CRISPRa singles, we next predicted double-gene 
activations. Over two CRISPRa double-perturbation screens (Norman et al.62, n = 77; ProPer-seq63, n 
= 56), ExPert ranked at least one constituent first for 5.3% of queries, 3.5-fold more often than RNA 
fingerprinting, and within the top-500 for 56%, while centroids were indistinguishable from random 
(Figure 3I). Recovery was lower than for knockdown, consistent with activation not being the mirror 
image of inhibition: raising a gene’s expression can engage different programs than lowering it. There 
were too few untrained genes (23 total) across these screens to test zero-shot recovery of 
combinations. We found the same performance ordering for CRISPRi and CRISPRa doubles when 
requiring both constituent perturbations in the top-k, with every method lower in absolute terms 
(Figure S5C-F).

molecules act on proteins rather than transcripts, so a drug screen separates the consequence of 
losing a gene's encoded protein function from transcriptional repression.If the prototypes capture the

prototype of the gene encoding it, although no small molecule compounds appear in training. We

across 50 cell lines, 49 of which are absent from our corpus. We queried each drug and dose against 
trained prototypes (Figure 4A) without retraining, fine-tuning, or recalibrating the model.

ExPert recognizes loss of function delivered by protein degradation 
CRISPRi suppresses gene transcription while small molecules typically inhibit protein activity without 
necessarily degrading the protein. Neither directly removes the protein while leaving the corresponding 
gene’s transcription unaffected. To test whether ExPert reads the loss of a protein’s function 
independently of how that loss is produced, we generated a degron dataset for this study by pooling 
clones carrying auxin-inducible degron tags on different proteins, inducing degradation with 5-Ph-IAA, 
and profiling them by single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) after 24 hours (Figure 4I, left; 
Methods). Western blots confirmed that the tagged protein was depleted within hours of induction and 
was unchanged in the vehicle (representative example in Figure 4I, right). CRISPR screens typically 
profile cells several days after perturbation19,70, so this experiment also tests how early the signature 
becomes readable. Eighteen of the 25 degron-tagged genes showed a reproducible transcriptional 
response across clones; the remaining seven were indistinguishable from vehicle-treated controls and 
were excluded (Methods). ExPert placed the correct perturbation in the top 10 for 3 of them, against 
0.05% expected by chance (332-fold, p = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷). SMARCA5 and SUZ12 each ranked first out of 
19,895 candidates (Figure 4J). MED28 and NUP62 were never perturbed in training, yet ExPert 
ranked them 2nd and 63rd, extending its zero-shot capability to a new assay and cellular context 
(Figure 4J). Interestingly, ExPert ranked SOX2 236 of 19,895 but placed it on the gain side rather than 
the loss side which could be a property of the auxin-induced degron system or the documented SOX2 
autoregulation71,72 (Supplemental note 7).  
 
ExPert uncovers aberrant mRNA export and splicing in alcohol-associated liver disease 
We next applied ExPert to human disease to test whether it could identify regulatory processes not 
apparent from differential expression alone. ALD is the leading cause of chronic liver disease and its 
incidence is rising73. Abstinence remains the only proven cure, and once liver failure develops, 
transplantation is the only life-saving intervention74. We profiled nuclei from healthy liver (n=5) and 
from patients with alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH, n=5) or cirrhosis (AC, n=5) by snRNAseq73, and 
scored hepatocyte nuclei against the ExPert prototype table (Figure 5A). The highest-scoring 
perturbations for ALD nuclei included nucleoporins and nuclear transport factors (NUP153, THOC7, 
RANGAP1), splicing factors (SRSF6), and chromatin regulators (SMARCA4, RAD21, SMC1A, 
CSNK2B) (Figure 5B). Ranking genes by log2 fold change and ExPert score gave no overlap 
(Spearman ρ = −0.04 in AC and −0.03 in AH; Figure 5C):  ExPert’s top calls sat near the origin of both 
volcano plots while the top differentially expressed genes sat at the extremes (Figure 5D). ExPert’s 
ranking was therefore uncorrelated with differential expression.

instead returned inflammatory and metabolic programs such as complement cascade and neutrophil 
degranulation, more consistent with downstream injury than with drivers (Figure 5F, Table S10B); 
neither mRNA export nor splicing was among them. Single-nucleus counts are sparse, so a null for the 
genes named by ExPert could reflect a lack of power rather than a lack of change. We therefore 
compared these results to deeply sequenced bulk RNA from the same cohort of healthy, AH and AC

ExPert recovers affected genetic programs in patients with primary mitochondrial disease 
We next asked whether ExPert could identify the affected machinery in a disease with established 
genetic causes. Primary mitochondrial diseases (PMDs) are a heterogeneous group of genetic 
disorders that affect 1 in 5,000 live births, with significant neurologic morbidity in childhood, and have 
known current cures90. PMD arises from gene variants that cause mitochondrial dysfunction, including 
lesions in the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) complexes and their supporting 
machinery91. We profiled balanced PBMCs from four patients with PMD, and five controls with 
non-PMD conditions by scRNA-seq, then encoded every cell with ExPert and asked which 
perturbations best explain its state (Figure 6A; Methods). As in ALD, ExPert’s ranking was 
uncorrelated with differential expression (Spearman ρ = −0.04; Figure 6B). ExPert’s top calls were 
genes that cause PMD, nearly all scored as loss: mitochondrial ETC subunits, mitochondrial ribosomal 
proteins, regulators of mtDNA maintenance, and mitochondrial dynamics. Among them were 
NDUFAB1 and NDUFB7 (mitochondrial complex I), UQCRC1 (mitochondrial complex III), ATP5F1A 
(mitochondrial complex V), and TFAM (mtDNA maintenance). The same genes sat near zero by 
differential expression, paradoxically with a slight bias toward positive fold-change. These patients 
carry variants expected to impair oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), so the modest increase in 
OXPHOS-related transcripts would be consistent with compensation rather than with a primary driver 
of disease. By contrast, ExPert scored those same top genes as strong loss of function, which is 
consistent with the genetics, biochemistry, physiology, and clinical presentation of these diseases. 
 
Scoring each cell for OXPHOS loss placed three PMD donors with known PMD at the high end of the 
distribution, together with donor PT67, who had been enrolled in the study as a non-PMD control 
(Figure 6C). The placement of PT67 with the PMD patients was unexpected. PT67 presented with 
severe neurodegenerative disease in the first months of life that had been attributed to a 
non-mitochondrial metabolic disorder initially. Re-review of the patient’s genetics identified compound 
heterozygous variants in pyruvate carboxylase (PC), including a known pathogenic variant. PC is 
localized to the mitochondria and is required for the conversion of pyruvate to oxaloacetate. Although 
PC deficiency is typically described as a syndrome with three distinct forms, it clearly falls within the 
broader family of PMDs. PT54, who showed the most severe clinical phenotype (Table S11), scored 
highest by a clear margin. Hierarchical clustering on ExPert scores then recovered the groups exactly 
(adjusted Rand index (ARI) 1.00, p = 0.008), whereas clustering on the 2,000 most variable genes did 
not (ARI −0.01, p = 0.524; Figure 6D).  
 
ExPert also identified specific mitochondrial processes affected in each patient. Scored by gene group, 
every patient showed loss across the mitochondrial ETC complexes, strongest in PT54 (Figure 6E). 
Scored gene-by-gene, the patients' lesion genes scored as loss in their own monocytes, most clearly 
PC in PT67, which scored higher for PC than any other donor, and NDUFS8 in PT54 (Figure 6E). 
PT67’s lesion lies outside the respiratory chain, yet ExPert scored respiratory-chain loss strongly, 
similar to the three patients carrying disease-driving variants in the mitochondrial ETC complexes. PC 
deficiency has been reported to produce a secondary mitochondrial complex IV deficiency, in one case 
leading to an initial diagnosis of PMD second to a mitochondrial complex IV defect before the PC 
lesion was found92, suggesting that ExPert may also identify secondary pathologic consequences in 
PMD.

DISCUSSION 
Considerable effort has gone into predicting how a cell responds to a perturbation. Here we asked the 
inverse question, which perturbation produced an observed state, and built ExPert to answer it. 
Trained across 22 CRISPRi screens spanning 12 cellular contexts, 19.5 million cells, and 61,445 
perturbation–context pairs covering 8,964 perturbations, ExPert more than doubled held-out-context 
recall over the best existing forward or inverse method at a fraction of their compute, and extended to 
19,895 genes, most never seen by the model in training. The same prototype table recovered 
annotated drug targets, degron-induced protein loss, and combinatorial perturbations it was never 
trained on. Applied to two diseases, ExPert implicated genes which differential expression did not: 
isoform-level remodeling of mRNA export in alcohol-associated liver disease, confirmed by poly(A) 
FISH and RT-PCR, and respiratory-chain complexes in PMD, concordant with genetics and clinical 
presentation of PMD. 
 
ExPert’s learned prototype embedding was built for inference but may be useful for the forward 
problem of perturbation response prediction. In principle, the prototypes could be supplied to a forward 
model in place of a text or annotation embedding, or the reconstruction objective could be reframed 
during training so each prototype also generates the state it names. Other extensions depend on data 
that does not yet exist at scale. Variational generative models already combine transcriptome and 
chromatin accessibility per cell100, and the same encoder could take both, but multimodal and spatial 
CRISPR screens are not yet available at scale, though both are expanding rapidly17,101–103. Our model 
recovered combinations without explicitly training ExPert on combinatorial data; as those data expand, 
the model could be trained on the combinations directly. Furthermore, ExPert was trained only on 
CRISPRi knockdown, yet recovered CRISPRa perturbations, albeit at lower accuracy. Activating and 
repressing perturbations do not produce symmetric responses, and the two carry complementary 
information about gene function104,105, so emerging large-scale CRISPRa data will improve the 
biological grounding of the model and its generalizability.

In both drug screens and disease samples, ExPert recovered biology that annotations did not contain. 
It highly ranked GSK3 for abemaciclib (Figure 4G), an off-target the compound’s label omits. In PMD, 
it scored one of six participants enrolled as a control with the disease group, and re-review of that 
patient’s genetics reclassified them as PMD, so the discordance reflected an error with the label that 
ExPert corrected. The respiratory-chain score also tracked clinical severity, which a larger cohort could 
test as a prognostic marker. Any disease whose drivers are unknown can be queried the same way, 
since the procedure needs only a transcriptomic cell state and ExPert’s reference prototype table. In 
principle, the same prediction could be applied to patient tissue to select the most strongly affected 
cells, where no measured efficiency is available. What limits ExPert’s performance is the extent to 
which a perturbation’s response is shared between the screened contexts and the patient’s cells. 
Held-out donors were the hardest split in our benchmark, and performance along this axis should 
improve as Perturb-seq data from larger donor cohorts accumulate. 
 
ExPert ranks perturbations whose transcriptional signature matches a query. Rank is not proof of 
causality, and in disease settings the evidence that the top calls are causal must come from outside 
the model: DNA sequencing in PMD, and the FISH and splicing data in ALD were used herein. 
Decomposing diseases arising from many simultaneous lesions, such as cancer, may require 
combinatorial training data, though constrained optimization over inferred networks offers another 
route to expressing a disease state as a combination of perturbations106. ExPert also returns ranks 
rather than calibrated probabilities, which score-based diffusion models that infer perturbation identity 
probabilistically could supply107. 
 
Practically, the ranking converts an unexplained cell state into a short list of experiments: in both 
diseases here, the top calls—out of 19,895 candidates—were few enough to test directly, and those 
tests found biology which differential expression had missed. As perturbation atlases expand to more 
contexts and donors, more of each response becomes identifiable as shared, and these rankings will 
only improve.

METHODS 
 
Training a single model across heterogeneous perturbation screens required a common 
representation of gene expression and perturbation identity. We therefore harmonized 22 single-cell 
CRISPRi datasets, standardizing gene identifiers, perturbation labels, and quality-control criteria. The 
resulting database contained 31.8 million cells after filtering and control subsampling, of which 19.5 
million perturbed cells spanning 8,964 perturbations formed the pool used for model training and 
evaluation. Below, we describe database construction and benchmark splits, the ExPert model and its 
training procedure, and the evaluation of perturbation retrieval across methods. 
 
Training database construction 
Data sources. We assembled 22 published Perturb-seq datasets from eight studies: 
ReplogleWeissman202219 (K562 genome-wide and essential, RPE1), ReplogleWeissman202542 
(HepG2, Jurkat), Marson202520 (twelve datasets spanning four donors and three stimulation 
conditions in primary human CD4⁺ T cells), XAtlas202543 (HCT116, HEK293T), Mali202645 
(KOLF2.1J), Wong202644 (TeloHAEC) and Zhuang202641 (hESC). Datasets are published in a 
machine-readable datasheet that records the source URL, assay metadata, cell line, donor and 
condition annotations, and the axis declarations used for benchmark fold generation.

Quality control 
Cells were filtered with a median-absolute-deviation (MAD) outlier protocol computed from per-cell 
scalars119. A cell was discarded if it was an outlier at five MADs on any of the log-transformed total 
counts, log-transformed detected genes, or the fraction of counts in the top 50 genes; an outlier at 
three MADs on mitochondrial fraction; or above an absolute mitochondrial fraction of 20% for 
cancer-derived lines or 12% otherwise, assigned from the datasheet. Mitochondrial gene membership 
was derived from the Ensembl vocabulary rather than from per-dataset symbol flags, which are 
inconsistent across studies. Only passing cells were stored; the per-cell metrics were retained so that 
alternative thresholds can be re-derived without reading the expression matrix again. 
 
Control cells 
Up to 10,000 control cells per dataset were sampled, retaining 189,365 in total. The sampled barcodes 
are stored rather than re-drawn at each build. Sampling without replacement depends on the length 
and ordering of the candidate array as well as on the seed, so a draw is not reproducible from the 
seed once the input set changes: with the seed fixed, a 5% change in pool size produced only 2,018 
of 10,000 overlapping barcodes. Because the control draw propagates into the QC thresholds and 
from there into every downstream selection, the barcodes are stored. 
 
Perturbation effect size 
Each perturbed cell was assigned a continuous perturbation-effect-size score with a custom Python 
implementation of Mixscale120, restructured so that global fits are computed once per dataset and 
pinned. For each dataset, we selected the top 3,000 highly variable genes from stored per-dataset 
statistics and pinned the normalization and per-gene scaling; fitted a 40-component principal 
component basis on control cells only; and scored each perturbed cell by projecting it into that basis 
and subtracting the mean of its 20 nearest control neighbors. Perturbations with fewer than five 
differentially expressed genes received a score of 0.0. Highly variable gene statistics were computed 
with a streaming reimplementation of the seurat_v3121 procedure, verified to give results identical to 
the reference implementation 121. 
 
We persist the raw signed z-score rather than a rescaled value, deferring any scaling to selection time. 
This preserves both the sign and the tail: per-dataset maxima range from 26.5 to 96,199 against 
medians of 0.3 to 2.3, reflecting a near-zero-standard-deviation blow-up that is visible and correctable 
downstream but would be silently clipped by saturating at write time. In total, 28.2 million of 31.8 
million cells (88.8%) received a score; the remainder are controls or belong to perturbations below the 
differential-expression threshold. 
 
Gene panel selection 
The input panel was selected to maximize perturbation separability rather than overall variability. 
Within each training dataset, cells of each perturbation were split at random into two pseudobulk 
replicates over the full 38,638-gene vocabulary, with perturbations of fewer than 20 cells giving a 
single profile. Profiles were normalized to counts per million, log-transformed and expressed relative to 
the dataset’s control. Each gene measured in a dataset was scored by the ratio of between- to 
within-perturbation variance across replicates (an unmoderated F-statistic), and the panel is the union 
of each dataset’s 500 highest-scoring genes (3,096 genes).

similarity, over the selected genes, to reference profiles of all scored perturbations, each reference

Perturbation pool and database composition 
A perturbation entered the shared pool if it had at least 10 cells in a dataset of at least 60% of the 
voting groups (6 of 10) and at least 50 cells after pooling. Voting groups were defined by screen: each 
Replogle and X-Atlas screen formed its own group (K562 genome-wide, K562 essential, RPE1, 
HepG2, Jurkat, HCT116, HEK293T), and the Marson2025 datasets formed one group per stimulation 
condition (resting, 8 h, 48 h; four donors pooled). The single-dataset studies Zhuang2026, Wong2026 
and Mali2026 did not vote, but their cells were retained for every pooled perturbation. This yielded 
8,964 perturbations. Only single perturbations were retained. Letting all 13 groups vote would have 
produced 8,652 perturbations, sharing 8,380 with the shipped pool (+272 / −584). 
 
Perturbed cells whose perturbation the pool excluded were written to a separate zero-shot evaluation 
set, capped at 10 cells per dataset-and-perturbation group. 
 
The resulting database comprises 19,513,869 perturbed cells and 189,365 control cells over 3,096 
genes, stored as CSR count matrices with per-cell annotation for dataset, perturbation, label, control 
status and scaled efficiency. Three subsets are derived from the same build and used for 
benchmarking: a 1,157-perturbation subset of 2,741,678 cells, a 200-perturbation subset of 671,242 
cells, and a zero-shot set of 683,651 cells carrying the 9,874 perturbations with no training cells. 
 
Because each dataset measures only a subset of the vocabulary and the panel is a union, the 
database carries two per-dataset boolean layers: the genes a dataset measured, and the genes the 
separability selection assigned to it. 
 
Benchmark fold generation 
Folds are declared in the datasheet rather than inferred from dataset names. Any datasheet column 
naming a holdout axis defines one, and each distinct value becomes a fold in which every cell carrying 
that value is held out. Three axes are declared: cell line, donor, and condition. 
 
Each donor fold additionally holds out one cell line, which is consumed from the cell-line axis so that 
no context is held out twice. Pairing is inverse by size, so that the largest donor takes the smallest cell 
line and donor folds are not trivially small, and no fold removes more than 35% of cells. This yields 
twelve folds: four donor-and-cell-line folds, five cell-line folds and three condition folds (Table S5C). 
 
Within each fold, the remaining cells were partitioned into training, validation, and in-distribution test 
sets, with validation and test capped at 10 cells per dataset-and-perturbation group and no cap on the 
number of perturbations, so that every perturbation is represented in evaluation and no single large 
dataset dominates it. All remaining cells become training data. Each fold's four index sets are written 
as positional indices, with an assertion that they partition every cell exactly. Split generation used seed

Implementation 
The pipeline is a Snakemake122 workflow in which no stage loads a complete expression matrix; all 
primitives operate on row bands of CSR matrices read directly from HDF5, so that memory scales with 
chunk size rather than with dataset size. Peak resident memory for the heaviest stage is 12.5 GB 
across all 22 datasets and is close to flat in dataset size (5.2 GB at 545 million non-zero entries; 12.5 
GB at 25.4 billion, a 47-fold increase in data for a 2.4-fold increase in memory). Total build time for the 
final database is 1 h 24 min. Artifacts are content-addressed under two independent configuration 
hashes, separating per-dataset parameters from the dataset list, so that adding a dataset reuses all 
existing per-dataset artifacts. Every stage is deterministic given the configuration and seed, with the 
exception of the control draw, which is why sampled barcodes are stored rather than re-derived. Each 
stage has a validation gate run against real rather than synthetic data; streaming highly variable gene 
selection was verified to agree exactly with the reference implementation, and parallel and serial 
materialization were verified bit-identical.

Training procedure. The production model (v0.1.0) was trained for 100 epochs of 1,500 batches of 
2,048 cells, 150,000 steps in all. Batches were drawn with replacement from the training cells by a 
weighted random sampler, each cell weighted by (n + 1)−1/2, where n is the number of training cells 
sharing its perturbation and dataset, with weights normalized to a mean of 1. A perturbation–dataset 
group is therefore sampled roughly in proportion to √n. Batches mix datasets and perturbations, with 
no structured or contrastive composition. Optimization used AdamW at a constant learning rate of 5 × 
10⁻³, weight decay 10⁻⁵ and ε = 0.01, with gradients clipped to a global norm of 1.0, in fp32. The 
prototype embedding and the cross-fit permutation were seeded (seed 0); no global seed was set. 
Internal validation used a fixed subset of at most 5 cells per perturbation and dataset, drawn once with 
a fixed seed and held constant across epochs, from which up to 50,000 cells were cached for the 
per-epoch monitor, macro-averaged top-10 recall excluding datasets using a “Marson2025”-prefix. The 
validation subsets served only to monitor training; it played no part in checkpoint or hyperparameter 
selection or in the benchmarks of Figure 2, and the same split was used for every benchmarked 
model. Early stopping was configured but never fired within the production run, and the final state at 
epoch 99 was taken rather than the best-monitored checkpoint. The zero-shot fill and the effect-size 
readout were installed after training (see Unseen perturbations; Perturbation effect size). The 12 fold 
models followed the same procedure over 80 epochs instead of 100. 
Inference. A query cell is centered on the control mean of its own dataset at the input, encoded with 
the posterior mean, and centered in the latent as during training, giving u. It is scored against every 
prototype in one pass by sp = cos(u, ap). Ranking runs over whichever subset of the table the question 
admits: the 8,964 trained prototypes for a screened candidate set, or all 19,895 rows when 
perturbations absent from training are candidates. Scores are signed, so the ranking can be read from 
either end. The highest cosines name perturbations whose knockdown the cell resembles (loss tail), 
and the most negative name perturbations whose knockdown it opposes (gain tail). No hubness 
correction was applied. 
Input control means are refreshed from the query’s own control cells where a dataset contributes at 
least 10, and otherwise the trained baseline is kept. A dataset absent from training has no stored 
latent mean, so its latent is centered on the mean of its control cells in the same call, or on the mean 
of all its cells if none are labeled as controls. Scores for such a dataset therefore depend on which of 
its cells are scored together, and a dataset must not be split across calls. In benchmarking, control 
means were refreshed once from the pooled control cells and prediction run per whole dataset.

Datasets and analyses 
Benchmarking against perturbation-prediction methods 
 
Most published perturbation models are formulated in the opposite direction, mapping a perturbation to 
an expected expression response. Only RNA fingerprinting38 and the non-learned reference floors are 
natively inverse; CellOT32, GEARS31, scGPT34, scouter35 and GenePert53 are forward models and must 
be inverted to be compared. Each inversion is stated explicitly below, and in each case we chose the 
form most favorable to that method that its published formulation supports. Every method emits the 
same artifact, a matrix of per-cell scores over the candidate perturbations together with the row 
positions of the scored cells in the source data, and a single collector scores all matrices with identical 
code; ground-truth labels are read from the source annotation, never from the method's output. 
 
Benchmark data and folds 
All methods were run on subsets of the database described above, over the same 22 datasets and 
3,096-gene union panel. The primary benchmark comprises the 1,157 perturbations assayed in at 
least seven of the ten cellular contexts (2,741,678 cells); a 200-perturbation subset (671,242 cells) is 
used only to measure how cost scales with the number of candidates. Each of 12 folds holds out one 
to four datasets: folds 0–3 hold out one T cell donor (all three conditions) together with one cell line, 
folds 4–8 a cell line, and folds 9–11 a stimulation condition across all donors. Per fold, the 
1,157-perturbation subset has on average 2.17 million training, 131,639 in-distribution (ID) test and 
17,523 out-of-distribution (OOD) test cells; OOD test cells are capped at 10 per dataset and 
perturbation. 
 
ExPert scores 
ExPert models were trained outside the benchmark using the ExPert python package 
(https://github.com/GoyalLab/ExPert). We trained one model per fold of the split set over all 8,964 
perturbations. Benchmark and model folds were paired by held-out dataset set (an exact match, 
otherwise the smallest model fold whose held-out set contains the benchmark fold's); all 12 folds of 
the primary benchmark are covered, and 11 of 12 of the 200-perturbation subset. Benchmark cells 
present in the paired model's training set were excluded by cell name. The score is the cosine 
similarity between a cell's centered latent and each perturbation's learned prototype, restricted to the 
benchmark's candidates. Because a dataset absent from training is centered on its mean over the 
cells in the same prediction call, predictions were made on whole datasets. 
 
Per-dataset training and dataset-blind evaluation 
CellOT32, GEARS31, scouter35, GenePert53 and RNA fingerprinting38 are single-dataset methods, so we 
trained one model per dataset on that dataset's cells over the union panel and evaluated dataset-blind: 
because a query cell does not reveal which model applies, each test cell is scored by every dataset's 
model. Per-cell scores are converted to a softmax over each model's perturbations, averaged over the 
models whose class space contains the perturbation, and log-transformed; each perturbation column 
is then z-scored across cells, which removes per-perturbation baseline offsets without using labels or 
dataset identity. 
 
CellOT. A per-dataset scGen28 autoencoder with per-perturbation optimal-transport maps. Cells are 
scored by −ǁδc − Δpǁ, where Δp is the predicted latent shift and δc the cell’s latent shift from its own 
dataset’s controls. 
 
GEARS. A per-dataset graph network. Cells are scored by the Pearson correlation between the cell’s 
deviation from its own dataset’s control mean and each predicted response. 
 
scouter. A per-dataset model, scored on its predicted mean response as for GEARS.

Single-model comparisons 
scGPT34, XGBoost54 and linear regression train one model per fold across all datasets. scGPT was 
fine-tuned from scGPT_human (15 epochs of 2,500 steps, batch size 64, learning rate 10⁻⁴, mixed 
precision). For each test dataset and candidate, it predicts a profile from 32 control cells with the 
candidate's target-gene token flagged, and test cells are scored by cosine similarity to these profiles 
over the 3,096 panel genes (ID test cells uniformly subsampled to 25,000 per fold). Because scGPT 
can express only perturbations whose target gene has a token, it was run on a copy of the data 
carrying one additional column per target gene outside the panel (measured expression where 
available, Poisson draws otherwise), making 712 of 1,157 perturbations expressible; the added 
columns are excluded from scoring. XGBoost 38 (multi:softprob; 150 rounds, depth 4, learning rate 
0.2, column subsample 0.3, row subsample 0.5, early stopping after 20 rounds on the validation split) 
and linear regression (one-vs-rest logistic regression by stochastic gradient descent, α = 10⁻⁵, 30 
iterations) take CPM-normalized, log1p-transformed expression minus the cell's own dataset control 
mean; XGBoost training was capped at 500,000 cells for memory. 
 
Baselines 
Random emits uniform scores. Prior scores every cell with the log training frequency of each 
perturbation (additive smoothing). Centroid scores the cosine similarity between a cell's 
control-relative log-normalized profile and each perturbation's mean training profile, with that 
perturbation's target gene masked. Negative target scores each perturbation by how far its target gene 
falls below the dataset's control mean in the query cell; for the 882 of 1,157 perturbations whose target 
gene is not among the input genes, the same score is computed on a decoy gene (an input gene that 
is not the target of any candidate, drawn at random with a fixed seed and distinct for each 
perturbation), so that every perturbation competes on equal terms. Negative target is shown only in 
the target-masking analysis (Figure S3C). 
 
Metrics 
Score matrices are converted to log-probabilities, and perturbations a method cannot express receive 
a score of negative infinity. The rank r of the true perturbation among the 1,157 candidates counts ties 
as half; the top-ranked call breaks ties by one fixed random order of the candidates. Metrics are 
computed separately for each held-out dataset in each fold (a dataset by fold combination) and 
averaged over the combinations available for every compared method. Within a combination, each 
perturbation present contributes one vote: top-k recall is the fraction of its queries whose true 
perturbation ranks among the top-k, mean reciprocal rank (MRR) is the mean of 1/(r + 1), and F1 
combines its recall with its precision among all queries called as that perturbation. 
 
Queries are scored at three levels. For pseudobulks (Figures 2A–2C), the log-probabilities of all cells 
of one perturbation in one dataset are averaged and the pooled vector is ranked once. For single cells, 
each cell is ranked separately and cells are weighted by their knockdown effect size (a 
Mixscale120-style perturbation score; cells with non-positive or missing scores excluded) when metrics 
are averaged within a perturbation (Figure 2E). For the effect-size analysis (Figure 2D), single cells

Compute cost and scaling 
Costs were read from the scheduler's accounting database (SLURM126) and attributed to method, 
benchmark size and stage by workflow rule, counting only completed jobs, each by its last completed 
attempt. Compute is CPU core-hours plus GPU-hours at a one-to-one exchange, the cluster's billing 
weight. Figures 2E–2I report evaluation only; XGBoost, linear regression and GenePert train and 
evaluate in one job, so their costs include training and are marked with asterisks. ExPert's evaluation 
cost was measured on fold 0 (eight CPU cores) and multiplied by the number of folds, because the 
scheduled runs also loaded the training data, which inference does not require. 
 
Scaling compares the 200- and 1,157-perturbation subsets per fold, excluding scGPT, GEARS and 
CellOT, whose readout, hardware or code changed between the two sizes. For each quantity, the 
exponent b in cost ∝ Cb is the log-log slope between the two sizes (0, flat; 1, linear). The summary 
quantity is the evaluation burden, compute-hours multiplied by jobs per fold, so that a method keeping 
per-job cost flat by spreading work over more jobs still scales poorly. 
 
Implementation 
The benchmark is one Snakemake workflow over benchmark size, fold, method and split, with a single 
target that produces every method's score matrix and the scoring rollup; each rule declares its own 
pinned conda environment, which lets incompatible dependencies coexist (for example, CellOT on 
torch 1.11.0 and scGPT on torch 2.5.1), and ExPert runs in its own environment. A complete 12-fold 
pass comprises 24,029 scheduler jobs for the 1,157-perturbation subset and 6,386 for the 
200-perturbation subset. One versioned configuration file pins the data build, methods, 
hyperparameters and per-rule resources; the source data path is content-addressed, and split

The model vocabulary covers 19,895 genes. The 8,964 trained perturbations have reliable training

Analysis of unrecovered drug targets (Supplementary Note 5) 
To probe whether drugs whose annotated target ExPert did not recover were true misses, or instead 
reflected drug biology absent from their annotation, we used the responsive drugs, readout and 
complex partner sets of the target-recovery analysis above. A drug’s target was recovered when it 
ranked in the top ten in more of the drug’s responsive conditions than expected from size- and 
popularity-matched random gene sets (one-sided binomial test, p < 0.05); the remaining 142 of 155 
drugs were missed. Misses in which a CORUM-core partner of the target ranked in the top 10 more 
often than chance were not considered further, as partner recovery is assessed above (Figure 4F). 
Each remaining miss was assigned to the first criterion it met: (i) the target was recovered in the 
gain-of-function direction; (ii) the drug resembled another drug class, (iii) did not resemble its own 
class, or (iv) resembled its own class, measured as drug-class similarity, the mean Pearson correlation 
of the drug’s profile with the annotated members of the class, called at z ≥ 3 against 2,000 random 
drug sets of the same size from other plates, in both ExPert scores and gene expression; or (v) the 
same prototypes ranked highly at every active dose (FDR < 0.05 against pseudo-drugs assembled 
from other drugs’ wells on the same plates), counted only when a top-five prototype was a 
documented secondary target or a member of the target’s pathway whose loss is expected to mimic 
the drug. We then checked every flagged miss against the primary literature and removed 
explanations it did not support. During review, we also accepted two glucocorticoids, dexamethasone

Prototype neighborhoods between drug targets (Figure S9, Supplementary Note 6) 
We built a nearest-neighbor graph over the 8,964 trained prototypes. We joined each prototype to its 
eight nearest neighbors by cosine similarity, with edge length 1 − cosine. For every pair of the 156 
Tahoe-100M drugs with a trained target, we found the shortest route through the graph from any target 
of one drug to any target of the other; 11,744 of the 12,090 pairs were connected. For each gene we 
counted the drug pairs whose path passed through it, excluding the drugs' own targets. Because 
highly connected prototypes lie on many paths by construction, we compared each count with the 
count when each drug's targets were replaced by size- and popularity-matched random genes (200 
permutations, 20 popularity bins), and called a gene a bridge when it was traversed by at least 50 drug 
pairs at least twice as often as expected. 102 genes met this criterion, 89 of them at p < 0.05; Figure 
S9A shows the 24 with the largest excess over the null. To ask whether known biology also connects 
these genes, we built the same graph and ran the same test from each of seven independent gene 
priors (co-essentiality from DepMap46,47, literature-derived gene descriptions, STRING51,52 interactions, 
GO109 biological process, L1000 signatures39,40, ProteinTalks127 and cell morphology114); 9 of the 24 
were bridges at twice the matched-random rate in at least one prior (DepMap 6, gene descriptions 4, 
STRING 3, GO 2, L1000 1, morphology 1), and no single prior supported all 9. Genomic position was 
scored the same way as a negative control.

Auxin-inducible degron screen (Figure 4J) 
We pooled 40 clones, each carrying an auxin-inducible degron on one gene, treated them with DMSO 
or auxin for 24 h, and profiled nuclei in two separate 10x Multiome gene-expression libraries (26,956 
DMSO and 31,475 auxin nuclei after quality control, which removed nuclei with more than 20% 
mitochondrial counts). We assigned nuclei to clones by their 20-bp clone barcode, allowing one 
mismatch (3,971 assigned nuclei), and scored the 25 clones with at least 10 nuclei in each condition. 
We pseudobulked each clone and condition, masked mitochondrial genes and passed the 
pseudobulks through the ExPert encoder with DMSO as the control baseline. Because the two 
conditions were separate libraries, every clone shares an auxin shift that reflects the library and 
treatment rather than the depletion; we therefore took each clone's response as its 
auxin-minus-DMSO change minus the mean change over all clones. A clone was responsive when 
this response, measured in expression, exceeded the 95th percentile of split-half differences between 
its own DMSO nuclei (50 splits, rescaled to the depth of the comparison); 18 of 25 clones passed. We 
ranked all 19,895 prototypes — the 8,964 trained ones and the 10,931 zero-shot ones, so that the 
three clone genes never perturbed in training (MED28, NUP62 and SOX2) compete on the same list 
— in both directions with the readout above. For each clone, we compared the rank of its own 
depleted gene with a null rank: the median rank that the genes depleted in the other clones reached in 
the same clone's ranking, which reflects how highly an unrelated gene ranks in that response. We 
tested whether clones ranked their own gene above this null by a one-sided paired sign-flip 
permutation test on log₁₀ rank (20,000 permutations) and a paired t-test on log₁₀ rank (Figure 4J). 
 
Human liver samples and datasets 
Single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) of human liver was obtained from Chembazhi et al. (GEO 
GSE281574)73. The data comprise liver tissue from 15 individuals: five healthy donors, five patients 
with alcohol-associated cirrhosis (AC) and five with alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH). For each 
condition, the nuclei were pooled into two libraries, one of three individuals and one of two. The 
processed data carry pool labels only, so the pool was the replicate in all snRNA-seq analyses. We 
analyzed 26,807 nuclei of five cell types: hepatocytes, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSEC), 
Kupffer cells, hepatic stellate cells (HSC) and T cells; 205 plasma cells were excluded. 
 
Bulk RNA-seq of 16 livers (healthy, n = 4; AC, n = 6; AH, n = 6) from the same study and biobank73 
was used for differential expression and alternative splicing. Which bulk livers contributed to the 
snRNA-seq pools is not recorded, so the two datasets are not treated as independent. Liver samples 
for RNA-FISH, nuclear morphometry and RT-PCR came from the same biobank (patient explants and 
donor resections, Johns Hopkins Hospital)73. 
 
ExPert readout of the snRNA-seq data 
The trained ExPert model (v0.1.0) was applied without retraining. For each pool and cell type, one 
pseudobulk profile was formed as the mean raw counts of its nuclei; mitochondrial and metallothionein 
transcripts (MT-, MT1, MT2A, MT3, MT4) were set to zero. All pseudobulks were encoded in one call 
of predict(), with each cell type's input centered on its healthy pools (center_by = cell type, 
control_label = healthy pools) and no hubness correction; the number of nuclei behind each 
pseudobulk was passed as its depth. All scores below were computed from the returned latents. From 
each pool's latent, the unweighted mean latent of its cell type over all six pools was subtracted, 
removing an offset shared by all conditions (without this step, disease and healthy reads correlated at 
r = 0.95–1.00). Pools were then combined into condition by cell type groups (nucleus-weighted mean 
latent). Each group was scored by the cosine similarity of its latent to the 8,964 trained perturbation 
prototypes: a high score means that the state resembles loss of that gene (predicted loss), a low score 
that it resembles the opposite (predicted gain). Where a standardized score is shown, scores were 
z-scored over the prototypes of that condition. ALD denotes AC and AH together; for rankings, the AC 
and AH hepatocyte top-200 lists were combined.

Mitochondrial patient collection and storage 
Two 4 mL blood samples were collected in green sodium heparin tubes from consented patients in the 
Neurogenetics Clinic at the Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago, in accordance with 
an approved institutional IRB (NU IRB# 2017-1331). Samples were processed 2–5 hours after 
collection. Briefly, samples were diluted 1:1 with fresh dPBS + 2% FBS (cPBS), and PBMCs were 
isolated by layering samples on top of 3.5 mL Ficoll in a SepMate-15 tube (StemCell #85415) and 
spinning 1200g x 10 min at RT. Cells underwent one round of RBC lysis (3 min RT, BioLegend 
420302) and washing with cPBS (300g x 8 min at 4ºC) prior to counting with AOPI using a Cellometer 
K2 and controlled freezing in CryoStor CS-10 (StemCell 07930) at a concentration between 1×10⁶ and 
3×10⁶ cells/mL. PBMCs were frozen at −80ºC and transferred to vapor-phase liquid nitrogen storage 
the following day. Typical yields ranged from 5×10⁶ to 1.5×10⁷ total cells. 
 
Single-cell RNA-seq of PBMCs 
Nine patients (four with PMD and five reference donors; see "Donor groups" below) were processed in 
two batches. Frozen samples were agitated in a 37ºC water bath for 1–2 minutes and transferred to 10

420201) and incubated with 10 µL Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend 422301) for 10 min at 4ºC. They

(Miltenyi) into live T cells (CD3⁺), myeloid cells (CD3⁻CD20⁻CD56⁻), B cells (CD3⁻CD20⁺), and NK cells 
(CD3⁻CD20⁻CD56⁺). 
 
Sorted populations were recombined for each patient at a 4:4:1:1 ratio (T:myeloid:B:NK; up to 1×10⁵

balance pooling. Gene expression and hashtag libraries were then sequenced together on an Illumina 
NextSeq 2000 (P4 flow cell), and gene expression reads from both runs were combined for analysis.

were removed if HTODemux called them doublets or negatives, if DoubletFinder called them doublets,

Eight patient samples (five with PMD, three controls) were processed in four batches; three of the 
PMD patients (P43, P54, P67) are also in the single-cell cohort. Samples were thawed and prepared 
for Tyto sorting as described above. Antibodies used were anti-human CD3-BV510 (BioLegend 
317332), CD19-FITC (Invitrogen 11-0199-42), CD8a-PE-Cy7 (Invitrogen 25-0088-42), CD14-APC 
(Invitrogen 17-0149-42), and CD4-APC-eFluor 780 (Invitrogen 47-0047-41), all at 1:100. Cells were 
washed, resuspended in 2 mL Tyto buffer with DAPI (1:1000), and sequentially sorted into live CD4⁺ T 
cells (CD19⁻CD3⁺CD4⁺), CD8⁺ T cells (CD19⁻CD3⁺CD8⁺), and classical monocytes 
(CD19⁻CD3⁻CD14⁺). 
 
Sorted cells were resuspended at 1×10⁶ cells/mL in cHPLM with 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Gibco), 
supplemented with recombinant human IL-2 (50 U/mL; teceleukin) for T cells. Each population was 
split into two wells of a 96-well plate and rested for 30 min at 37ºC, 5% CO₂. T cells were stimulated 
with ImmunoCult Human CD3/CD28 T Cell Activator (StemCell 10971; 25 µL/mL) and monocytes with 
LPS (100 ng/mL), with matched unstimulated wells receiving vehicle. Input was matched between 
patient and control samples as closely as possible and initial plating densities were recorded. After 24 
hours, cells were collected by vigorous pipetting, while remaining adherent cells were lifted with 
CellStripper for 15 min at 37ºC and pooled. Cells were counted, aliquoted for flow cytometry (below), 
and the remainder was lysed in 350 µL Buffer RLT Plus (Qiagen) with 1% β-mercaptoethanol, 
vortexed for at least 30 s, and stored at −80ºC for RNA-seq. 
 
Bulk RNA-seq of sorted cells 
RNA was isolated from RLT Plus lysates (approximately 8×10³–1×10⁵ cells per sample; median 
~2.3×10⁴) using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen 74034). RNA was stored at −80ºC, and 
concentration and integrity were quantified with a High Sensitivity RNA kit (Thermo Fisher) on a Qubit 
2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher) and Agilent RNA ScreenTape on a 4200 TapeStation system 
(Agilent). Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Single Cell/Low Input RNA Library Prep Kit for 
Illumina (NEB E6420S) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were grouped into three 
tiers by estimated input, and cDNA amplification cycles were adjusted per tier (9, 11, and 15 cycles). 
Libraries were indexed with NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (Dual Index Primers Set 1; NEB 
E7600S), pooled, and sequenced using the NextSeq 1000/2000 XLEAP 100 cycle kit on a NextSeq 
2000 sequencer (Illumina) with a paired-end read configuration (28 bp for Read 1 and 90 bp for Read 
2), targeting 16 million read pairs per sample.

nf-core/rnaseq pipeline version 3.27.0 implemented in Nextflow 26.04.4 using Singularity 3.8.1 with the 
minimal command: nextflow run nf-core/rnaseq -r '3.27.0' -profile nu_genomics --fasta

Figure 1. ExPert learns cross-context perturbation prototypes for inverse inference 
 
(A) Training corpus. Left, corpus summary (22 datasets, 12 contexts, 19.5 million cells, 8,964 
perturbations). Outer ring, datasets colored by context, with the filled radial extent of each segment 
giving the percentage of its perturbations shared with other datasets; inner ring, primary CD4⁺ T cells 
(resting, 8 h and 48 h stimulation) and cell lines. Middle, perturbations (rows; n = 19,191 with ≥10 cells 
in at least one context, clustered) by context (columns); color, cells per perturbation and context (log 
scale); gray, measured but not selected for training. Dashed line separates the 8,964 perturbations 
selected for training. Top, cells per context (millions); CD4⁺ T cell columns are stacked by donor (n = 
4). Right, number of perturbations (rows as in the matrix) present in at least the indicated percentage 
of contexts; open circle, selection threshold (≥60% of contexts; Methods). 
 
(B) Model overview. Top, cells from multiple training contexts (x0, x1, x2), and query cells at inference 
(triangle) are embedded by a shared encoder into a common latent space in which each perturbation 
is represented by a prototype (diamonds). Three terms position the prototypes: proximity to the cells 
carrying that perturbation (Proxy Anchor loss, with each cell weighted by its measured knockdown 
effect; center); for perturbations with little training support, pull toward the mean of their neighbors' 
prototypes in gene-prior graphs built from gene text descriptions, STRING51,52 protein-protein 
interactions (PPI)108, and DepMap46,47 co-dependency (left); and reconstruction of the input through a 
context-conditioned decoder (bottom). Perturbations outside the training set are placed at the mean of 
their trained prior neighbors (gray diamonds) and serve as negatives during training. At inference 
(right), each query cell is encoded into the latent space and scored by cosine similarity against all 
19,895 prototypes in a single pass, giving a ranked list of perturbations; a separate head predicts the 
per-cell perturbation strength (|effect|). 
 
(C) Forward and inverse problems. Forward models predict the expression state of an unperturbed cell 
given a perturbation; ExPert addresses the inverse problem, identifying the perturbation that produced 
a measured state. The same trained model is applied without retraining to five classes of unseen 
measured states, ordered by increasing difficulty of generalization from the training data and 
increasing model utility: held-out contexts (cell lines, stimulation conditions, or donors), unseen 
perturbations, perturbation combinations, drug screens, and donor tissue (Figures 2–6). 
 
(D) Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP; cosine distance) of the 8,964 learned 
prototypes of the final model (one training run on all 22 datasets; also used in (E)). Prototypes are 
colored by Leiden cluster (79 clusters) where the cluster is enriched for a specific annotation term (44 
clusters; hypergeometric test against GO109, KEGG110, Reactome111, CORUM112 and MSigDB113 
Hallmark gene sets; Benjamini–Hochberg q < 0.05; term covering ≥20% of the cluster's annotated 
members); labels give the top term. Gray, 5,370 prototypes in clusters without a specific enrichment. 
 
(E) Agreement between prototype geometry and external gene priors. Left, overlap between each 
prototype's 10 nearest neighbors in the latent space and in the prior, relative to chance (log scale). 
Right, Spearman correlation (ρ) between pairwise prototype cosine similarity and pairwise prior 
similarity, over 300,000 random pairs of prototypes covered by the prior. Filled circles, all prototypes; 
open circles, prototypes not pulled toward priors during training; gray boxes, label-shuffled prior (mean 
± 2 SD over three shuffles). Bracket, priors used during training. GO BP, Gene Ontology biological 
process; STRING exp. and STRING coexp., STRING experimental and co-expression channels; 
morphology, JUMP114 Cell Painting CRISPR profiles; genomic neighbors, chromosomal position; 
L100040, LINCS L1000 consensus signatures. 
 
See also Figures S1, S10 and S11 and Tables S1–S3.

Figure 3. Prior gene knowledge extends ExPert to perturbations absent from its training 
screens 
 
(A) Schematic. Prior gene knowledge (text descriptions, protein interactions, genetic dependencies; 
top) places a prototype for every gene in the model's vocabulary (bottom): trained perturbations (dark 
gray) are learned from screens; unseen (zero-shot) perturbations (light gray) are positioned from their 
prior neighbors. 
 
(B) Zero-shot retrieval. Each query is the pseudobulk of up to 25 cells of one perturbation in one 
screen, ranked against all 19,895 prototypes. Score, top-10 recall weighted by the within-screen 
percentile of knockdown effect size, expressed as fold over chance (chance = 10/19,895). Left, 
in-distribution (ID): screens from contexts seen in training. Middle, out-of-distribution (OOD): screens 
from the context held out by that model; each context is held out by one model, so OOD screen rows 
show a single value. Summary rows pool all screens: all trained, held-out cells of the 8,964 trained 
perturbations, scored against the same 19,895 candidates; all zero-shot, the 9,874 of 10,931 
zero-shot perturbations measured in at least one screen. Remaining rows, zero-shot on individual 
screens, ordered by ID median, with the CD4+ T cell conditions in time order. Orange dot, median over 
models; light gray box, interquartile range; dark gray bar, ±1 SD of log-transformed values. Gray dot 
(random), zero-shot prototypes reassigned to random genes (mean of 20 shuffles per model). Dashed 
line, chance. Right, unique perturbations scored per row. n = 12 models (one training run each), each 
trained with one cell line, donor, or condition held out. 
 
(C) Zero-shot prototypes fall throughout the trained prototype space. Circular dendrogram (Ward 
linkage, cosine) of the 8,964 trained prototypes of the production model; each of the 10,931 zero-shot 
prototypes is placed beside its most similar trained prototype. Outer ring, trained (dark gray) and 
zero-shot (light gray) leaves. 
 
(D) Generation of gene text embeddings. Source text (left) is summarized by a description-writing 
language model (middle) and encoded by an embedding model (right); each ribbon is one 
writer-encoder combination (n = 36), including the published GenePT embeddings50. Color, zero-shot 
top-10 recall (fold over chance) multiplied by KEGG57 neighbor lift. 
 
(E) Prior quality predicts zero-shot retrieval. Zero-shot top-10 recall (effect-size-weighted, fold over 
chance) when a single prior positions the zero-shot prototypes, against a training-free measure of the 
prior's biological content: lift over chance in KEGG57 pathway co-membership among each gene's ten 
nearest neighbors, computed on a size-matched set of 2,000 genes (as in (D)). All priors are scored 
with one model trained without prior information (K562 held out). Points, individual priors (n = 48): text 
(n = 36, Methods), STRING52 (n = 3), DepMap46,47 (n = 3), and other external sources (n = 6, 
Methods). Dashed line, least-squares fit. Spearman ρ = 0.86 (all priors), 0.82 (text), 0.64 (non-text). 
 
(F) Combining priors. Zero-shot top-10 recall (as in (E)) for every subset of the ten best single priors, 
by subset size k (number of subsets = 10 choose k). Gray, subsets; white, the same subsets with gene 
labels shuffled. Filled circle, best subset; open circle, best subset selected on one half of the 
perturbations and scored on the other half. Values below each k, held-out best subset. Vertical lines, 
best single prior (dashed) and chance (dotted). 
 
(G) Schematic of combinatorial perturbation recovery. Pseudobulk queries from double perturbations 
(left) are ranked by ExPert (middle) against the single perturbations in its trained vocabulary (right). 
 
(H) Recovery of combinatorial loss-of-function perturbations by the production model. Fraction of 
double perturbations for which either targeted gene ranks within the top-k of the 8,347 candidate 
perturbations scorable by all methods. Orange, ExPert; dark gray, RNA fingerprinting38 custom-trained 
on genome-wide K562 Perturb-seq19; mid gray, nearest centroid of each perturbation's training cells;

Figure 5. ExPert identifies mRNA export dysregulation in alcohol-associated liver disease 
 
(A) Study design. Liver tissue from 15 donors (healthy, alcohol-associated cirrhosis [AC], and 
alcohol-associated hepatitis [AH]; 5 donors per condition)73, pooled into two libraries per condition, was 
profiled by single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) for ExPert inference. The same cohort 
(healthy, n = 4; AC, n = 6; AH, n = 6 livers) was profiled by bulk RNA-seq for differential expression 
(DE) and splicing analysis. 
 
(B) UMAP of 26,807 nuclei from five liver cell types in the ExPert latent space. Gray, healthy; red, 
alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD; AC and AH). Orange diamonds, the 25 highest-scoring 
perturbation prototypes for each of AC and AH (scores averaged over the five cell types). Labeled 
prototypes belong to mRNA export or splicing (bold), chromatin modification, SUMOylation, or 
macroautophagy gene sets. 
 
(C) ExPert rank of the top-500 predicted perturbations versus DE rank (pyDESeq2 absolute Wald 
statistic) in AC (top) and AH (bottom) hepatocytes relative to healthy (pseudobulk; n = 2 libraries per 
condition). Orange, ExPert top-100; black, predictions among the top-100 DE genes; gray, remaining 
predictions. Axes are log-scaled; dashed line, y = x. Spearman's ρ is indicated. 
 
(D) Volcano plots of hepatocyte DE in AC (top) and AH (bottom) versus healthy (pseudobulk; n = 2 
libraries per condition). Shrunken log2 fold change versus Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p-value; 
dashed lines, |log2 fold change| = 2 and adjusted p = 0.05. Black, top-100 DE genes; orange, top-100 
ExPert predictions (faded if also DE); gray, other genes. Labeled, the three most downregulated and 
three most upregulated of the top-100 DE genes (black) and eight ExPert predictions (orange). 
 
(E) Pathways enriched among the top-200 ExPert predictions in healthy and ALD (AC and AH 
combined) hepatocytes (one-sided hypergeometric test; background, all perturbations in the ExPert 
bank; Reactome and curated mRNA export and splicing gene sets, Methods). Shown are mRNA 
export and mRNA splicing (bold) and the 13 most significant non-redundant pathways, ordered by fold 
enrichment in ALD. Color, signed log2 fold enrichment (blue, predicted loss of function; green, 
predicted gain of function); dot size, unadjusted p. 
 
(F) As in (E), for the top-200 downregulated and top-200 upregulated DE genes in ALD hepatocytes 
(background, all tested genes), showing the 15 most significant non-redundant pathways. Blue, 
enrichment among downregulated genes; green, among upregulated genes. 
 
(G) Volcano plots of bulk RNA-seq DE in AC (top) and AH (bottom) versus healthy livers (DESeq2; n = 
4 healthy, 6 AC and 6 AH livers; thresholds as in (D)). Genes are colored by ExPert predicted 
loss-of-function score (z-score across the perturbation bank; gray, z ≤ 0, to orange, z ≥ 3) in 
snRNA-seq hepatocytes of the same condition; light gray, not scored by ExPert. Labeled, top 200 
ExPert predictions in the mRNA export or splicing gene sets. 
 
(H) Poly(A) RNA-FISH (hybridization chain reaction) on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) 
human liver sections (5 μm) from healthy individuals and patients with AH or AC 63. Representative 
Airyscan confocal images of healthy (left), AH (center), and AC (right) livers. Rows, top to bottom: 
poly(A) RNA; nuclear DNA (dsDNA; DAPI); merge (poly(A), red; DNA, blue); 3× magnification of the 
boxed region; and the magnified poly(A) signal colored by intensity percentile (scale below; < 80th to ≥ 
99.5th percentile). Scale bars, 25 μm (rows 1–3) and 10 μm (rows 4 and 5). 
 
(I) Nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio of poly(A) foci (log2 scale) in healthy and ALD hepatocytes, colored by

condition). Frames could not be assigned to individual livers and are treated as independent.
