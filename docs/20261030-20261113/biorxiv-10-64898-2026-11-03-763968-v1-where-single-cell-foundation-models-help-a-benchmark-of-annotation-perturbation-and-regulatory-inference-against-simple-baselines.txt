# Where single-cell foundation models help: a benchmark of annotation, perturbation and regulatory inference against simple baselines 

Francesca Cupaioli and Ivan Merelli* Institute for Biomedical Technologies, National Research Council (CNR-ITB), Via Fratelli Cervi 93, 20054 Segrate (MI), Italy *Corresponding author: Ivan Merelli, ivan.merelli@itb.cnr.it 

## Abstract 

Single-cell foundation models (FMs) are proposed as general tools for cell-type annotation, in silico perturbation and gene-regulatory-network (GRN) inference. To identify where they add value, we benchmarked five FMs (scGPT, Geneformer V1 and V2, Nicheformer, scFoundation), CellOracle, supervised perturbation models including STATE, and task-matched simple baselines on the same data, from K562 Perturb-seq screens and primary cells to healthy-donor and isogenic leukodystrophy brain organoids, a public organoid CRISPR screen and lung cancer biopsies. FMs were most useful when labels had to be transferred across species: from human to mouse lung, Nicheformer reached 0.98 accuracy and Geneformer V2 and scFoundation about 0.90, against 0.68 for the best baseline. Zero-shot in silico perturbations captured the direction of real responses in K562, with up to 75% of perturbations shifted in the expected direction (scFoundation, p < 1e-8), and Geneformer V2 networks recovered patient-level tumour differential expression better than co-expression (fold enrichment 2.9 versus 2.0). Within a species, FM embeddings annotated cells as accurately as principal components. In organoids, predicted knockout effects were largely explained by where the target is expressed, and an expression-matched control test isolated the gene-specific part, 12 to 35% of the effects. Isogenic Canavan and Krabbe organoids and 4H patient organoids, with one library per line and time point, did not yet separate methods from differences between clones and donors, which defines the replication needed for future validation. Fine-tuning changed classification accuracy but not knockout rankings. We release the benchmark, baselines, pipeline checks and the isogenic reference task as the fmaudit package, so that new models can be evaluated on the tasks where FMs already help. 

## Key Points 

• Five single-cell foundation models, a GRN-based simulator, supervised perturbation models including STATE, and non-foundation baselines were compared on annotation, perturbation and GRN inference across cell lines, isogenic disease organoids, a public organoid CRISPR screen and human tumour biopsies. • Foundation models were most useful for transferring cell-type labels across species. From human to mouse, Nicheformer, Geneformer V2 and scFoundation clearly exceeded principal-component and CellTypist baselines, while within a species all methods performed similarly. • Zero-shot in silico perturbations captured the direction of real K562 responses for several models, but not their magnitude. In organoids predicted knockout effects largely followed target expression, and an expression-matched control test isolates the gene-specific part.

## Introduction 

Transformer models pre-trained on tens of millions of single-cell transcriptomes — scGPT [1], Geneformer [2], Nicheformer [3] and scFoundation [4] — are presented as foundation models (FMs) that can be applied, with or without fine-tuning, to many downstream tasks. The tasks most often advertised are cell-type annotation, prediction of the transcriptional response to genetic perturbation, and inference of gene regulatory networks (GRNs). If reliable, FMs would enable in silico experiments in systems that are expensive to modify genetically, such as patient-derived organoids. Independent evaluations have tempered these expectations. For clustering and data integration, zero-shot FM embeddings often perform no better than highly variable genes or principal components [5,6]. For perturbation prediction, deep-learning models, FMs included, do not outperform deliberately simple linear or mean baselines [7,8], and zero-shot FM embeddings add little over simple baselines for perturbation effect prediction [9] and expression-forecasting methods rarely beat simple baselines [10]. These studies mostly evaluate one task in cell lines, where large Perturb-seq screens provide ground truth [11–13]. It remains unclear whether their conclusions extend to tissue-like systems and to the disease models in which in silico perturbation would be most useful, and whether a model that performs well on one task or system can be trusted on another. Here we evaluate the same methods on three tasks (zero-shot annotation, in silico perturbation and GRN inference) at three levels of biological complexity. Level (i) is cell lines and primary cells, including K562 Perturb-seq screens. Level (ii) is brain organoids, including organoids from healthy donors (HD), isogenic organoids carrying an ASPA knockout (Canavan disease) or a GALC knockout or G553R knock-in (Krabbe disease) together with their parental lines, organoids from patients with 4H leukodystrophy (POLR3A), and a public mosaic CRISPR screen of 36 genes. Level (iii) is human tissue, using biopsies of EGFR-mutant lung cancer before and during targeted therapy. Each task×system combination is compared with a non-FM baseline computed on the same split and with the same metric (Figure 1). The isogenic organoids provide a rarely available ground truth, the real per-cell-type consequence of knocking a gene out in an otherwise identical genetic background, but only if that effect can be separated from differences between clones, donors and libraries. We therefore simulated each knockout in the parental cells, treated libraries as replicates and included negative contrasts without a mutation. The disease models define three scenarios: a well-expressed target with an isogenic control (GALC), a barely expressed target with replicated clones (ASPA), and a broadly expressed target without isogenic control (POLR3A). Errors in sample identity, data scaling or model training are easy to introduce and rarely visible in the results. We therefore applied systematic checks to every dataset and analysis step: genotype-based verification of sample identity, input-scale checks, verification of weight changes after fine-tuning,

## Methods 

Benchmark design 

The benchmark is a grid of tasks (annotation, perturbation, GRN) by datasets at three levels of biological complexity (Figure 1). Methods were scGPT [1], Geneformer V1-10M [2] and V2-316M, Nicheformer [3] and scFoundation [4], used zero-shot and, where applicable, after fine-tuning. We also ran CellOracle [14] with a promoter base GRN, optionally augmented with STRING v12 interactions [15], the supervised perturbation models GEARS [16], CPA [17], scGPT fine-tuned for perturbation and STATE [18], and non-FM baselines chosen per task, including gene-independent predictors such as the number of cells per cell type. All methods in a grid row were run on the same cells, split, gene universe and metric. Full protocols are in the Supplementary Methods. 

Datasets 

Level 1, cell lines and primary cells: the Immune Human atlas (33,506 cells, 16 types) [19], Tabula Muris Senis lung (mouse) [20], and the Norman CRISPRa (91,205 cells) [11] and Adamson CRISPRi (68,603 cells, 87 conditions) [12] K562 Perturb-seq screens in GEARS processing. Level 2, brain organoids: organoids from five healthy donors (HD, 35,257 cells, 27 annotated cell types), including c107 and HD2.2, the parental lines of the isogenic lines. Canavan organoids came from three ASPA knockout clones of c107 (E27, E30 and E70, knockout validated by PCR, sequencing and western blot). Krabbe organoids came from a GALC knockout (KO) and a GALC G553R knock-in (KI) derived from HD2.2, and from a Krabbe patient-derived line. 4H leukodystrophy organoids came from two patients with POLR3A variants (variant details not available), without isogenic control. Disease organoids were annotated by label transfer from the HD organoids. Each line was sequenced as one library at day 100 and one at day 150 (Supplementary Table S4). HNOCA [21] served as annotation reference. Every library was checked for donor identity by genotype concordance at expressed SNPs and for sex by chromosome Y expression. Level 2 also included the public CHOOSE mosaic organoid screen [22], in which perturbed and control cells share the same organoids. Level 3, human tissue: EGFR-mutant non-small-cell lung cancer biopsies [23], with the HLCA core [24] as human lung reference. 

Annotation 

Zero-shot cell embeddings were used for k-nearest-neighbour label transfer (k = 30 and cosine distance within a dataset, k = 20 and Euclidean distance with a confidence override across datasets). Cross-species transfer used one-to-one orthologs in both directions and a coarse crosswalk of shared classes, and was repeated with cosine kNN (k = 30, no override) as a classifier control. Baselines were PCA on 2,000 highly variable genes with the same classifier, z-scored highly variable genes and CellTypist [25] trained on the reference. We report accuracy and macro-F1 with 95% bootstrap intervals. Organoids were scored as broad-lineage concordance with the manual annotation. Tabula Muris classes follow the authors' annotation (Supplementary Methods). 

In silico perturbation 

Zero-shot knockouts followed the embed–delete–re-embed protocol: the target gene was removed from each cell's input, and the effect was the cosine distance between original and perturbed embeddings,

averaged per cell type. For scGPT, which samples at most 1,200 input genes per cell and breaks expression-bin ties at random, gene set and tie-breaks were fixed per cell, so that the two inputs differed only by the target. In organoids we perturbed 14 genes (nine neurodevelopmental transcription factors, HTT, ASPA, GALC, POLR3A and POLR3B, Supplementary Table S27) in a random sample of 5,000 HD cells. For the isogenic comparisons ASPA was deleted in all 7,336 c107 cells and GALC in all 6,999 HD2.2 cells, so that prediction and ground truth share the genetic background. A deletion changes the input only in cells that express the target: ASPA was detected in 69 of 7,336 c107 cells (0.94%, 9 of 27 cell types), GALC in 725 of 6,999 HD2.2 cells (10.4%, all cell types) and POLR3A in 26.1% of HD cells (all cell types, Supplementary Table S96). CellOracle simulated knockouts by signal propagation on cluster-specific GRNs. The GALC knock-in carries c.1657G>A (p.Gly553Arg), associated with infantile Krabbe disease [26] and without detectable enzyme activity when expressed in cells [27,28], consistent with misfolding [29]. Because FMs see only expression, it was compared with the same in silico deletion as the knockout. In K562, each single-gene perturbation in a model's vocabulary was applied to 500 control cells. Supervised models were scored by Pearson δ on unseen GEARS test perturbations against training-mean and additive baselines [7]. Cells of one library are not independent replicates [30]. The isogenic ground truth was therefore computed on pseudobulk counts per library and cell type (at least 10 cells) with edgeR quasi-likelihood [31] and the design ~ day + genotype, from raw counts on a common gene set. The primary target, fixed before scoring, was the summed absolute log2 fold change per cell type. ASPA (five knockout versus two c107 libraries) was pre-specified as the primary test because it has the most replicates. Because ASPA is detected in fewer than 1% of parental cells, all three scenarios are reported: GALC KO, KI and the patient-derived line versus HD2.2 (one residual degree of freedom), ASPA, and 4H (two POLR3A patients, four libraries, versus five healthy donors, ten libraries, ~ day + disease). For 4H, POLR3A was the target and POLR3B, a subunit of the same polymerase that is not mutated in these patients, served as a gene-level negative control. Both were deleted in the 5,000 HD cells, the negatives were the ten contrasts of two healthy donors versus the other three, and a cell type was tested when each group kept at least one library with at least 10 cells at each day (22 cell types). The same pipeline gave negative contrasts without a mutation: clone E30 versus E27, and HD1.2, C16, HVS-420 and c107 versus HD2.2. A prediction was called knockout-specific if its Spearman ρ over cell types was positive with p < 0.05 and exceeded its highest ρ against the negatives by at least 0.2 (for 4H, also the ρ of the POLR3B prediction). ASPA and GALC KO were combined with a sign-aware Fisher test (one-sided p for ρ > 0). Naive predictors were the target's mean expression and detection rate, and the number of cells and median detected genes per cell type. Cell-level, per-day, cell-number-matched and E70-excluded ground truths are secondary analyses. Disease biology was characterised by preranked GSEA on MSigDB Hallmarks, ranked by the statistic of a whole-organoid pseudobulk edgeR analysis with the same design, and by Enrichr GO Biological Process over-representation [32], against the isogenic controls. The CHOOSE screen [22] was analysed from the authors' processed object (28,344 cells, 36 genes, 16 cell types), with FDR-corrected DEG counts and a library-stratified Mantel–Haenszel composition test per gene and cell type as ground truth and knockouts simulated in control cells. For the lung-cancer biopsies [23] (23,261 Smart-seq2 cells), the ground truth was the number of DEGs per cell type for residual disease versus treatment-naive biopsies, per cell and, as a patient-aware control, by pseudobulk edgeR, with progression versus treatment-naive as specificity control. Cells were annotated against the HLCA core [24]. In both datasets library size and genes detected per cell type served as complexity baselines. 

Gene regulatory networks

For each FM, gene–gene networks were derived from contextual gene-embedding similarity and from attention averaged over layers and heads, and compared with CellOracle, Pearson co-expression, an expression prior and random ranking. The top-50 neighbours of each hub gene were scored against knockdown targets (Adamson) and ChIP-seq targets (ChIP-Atlas [33]) in K562, genes responding to EGFR inhibition in treatment-naive cancer cells of the lung-cancer biopsies and, provisionally, CellOracle and STRING targets in organoids, as fold enrichment over an expression-matched expectation (FE@50, 1 = chance), pooled over hubs. 

Fine-tuning 

Each FM was fine-tuned once as a 27-class organoid cell-type classifier with its original pipeline (grid fine-tuning, FT): one stage for Geneformer (layers 4–5, embeddings and head), two for Nicheformer and scFoundation (head on the frozen backbone, then the last two layers with the head). scGPT was fine-tuned end-to-end for perturbation on Norman or Adamson. Geneformer, Nicheformer and scFoundation were also trained under one protocol in three arms (linear probe, partial unfreezing and LoRA, see Supplementary Methods). Knockouts were recomputed with each fine-tuned backbone. The scFoundation grid backbone, not saved, was re-trained with the same code for the parental-cell knockouts (Supplementary Table S34). 

Expression-matched specificity test 

To test whether a knockout carries information about the deleted gene beyond the loss of expressed input, the embedding shift after deleting target g in a cell was compared with the shifts after deleting, one at a time, 20 control genes matched to g in that cell for expression and across the dataset for detection rate. The specific effect is S = observed − mean(control) and z = S / sd(control). A gene×cell-type pair with z > 2 has a specific excess shift. Geneformer was also run with random controls. 

Statistics and computation 

Correlations are Spearman's ρ with two-sided p-values unless stated otherwise. Multiple testing was controlled with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [34] within pre-specified families (isogenic organoids, K562, CHOOSE), and the number of tests and of cell types per test are reported (Supplementary Tables S55–S57). All computation ran on SLURM nodes (NVIDIA A100 GPUs for embedding and training) at CNR-ITB. 

## Results 

A task-by-system map with baselines 

Figure 1 summarises all task×dataset rows and method columns, each with a non-FM baseline computed on the same split and metric. In most rows the best FM was close to the baseline or below it. The exception was cross-species annotation, where some FMs clearly exceeded the baselines.

In HD organoids all methods scored 0.54–0.65 (PCA 0.632), with low macro-F1 (≤ 0.33) reflecting the mismatch between HNOCA and manual labels. Perturbation in cell lines: zero-shot models do not predict effect size 

We applied the organoid knockout protocol to every single-gene perturbation of Norman and Adamson that the models could represent (75–152 per dataset). The predicted magnitude was unrelated to the real effect size: Spearman's ρ between predicted cosine shift and the number of real DEGs ranged from −0.08 to 0.06 for the four FMs, none significant, and ranking perturbations by target expression did no better (ρ = −0.03 and 0.06, Figure 3A). The direction of the predicted shift carried more information. A deletion should move cells away from a CRISPRa response, and scFoundation did so for 75% of Norman perturbations (sign test p < 10−8) and Nicheformer for 66% (p = 2.5 × 10−4). For Adamson knockdowns scGPT (70%, p = 0.0009) and Nicheformer (64%, p = 0.015) shifted cells in the real direction (Figure 3B, Supplementary Tables S6 and S17). CellOracle could simulate only regulators in its base GRN. Supervised models were compared with simple baselines on unseen test perturbations (Figure 3C). On Norman, GEARS reached a mean Pearson δ of 0.543 and scGPT fine-tuned 0.568, while predicting the mean training shift reached 0.557. For unseen single genes the baseline was higher than GEARS (0.498 vs 0.409), and for combinations of two seen genes the additive baseline was higher (0.887 vs 0.658), consistent with [7]. The often-shown DLX2 example is in the GEARS training set. On Adamson, the training-mean baseline (0.711) exceeded GEARS (0.688), scGPT fine-tuned (0.642) and CPA (0.375), and was better than GEARS for 19 of the 22 test perturbations (Wilcoxon p = 0.0009, Supplementary Figure S3). STATE could be scored only on perturbations in its vocabulary, all seen as CRISPRi knockdowns in other cell lines during training (9 of 152 Norman and 56 of 86 Adamson singles). With K562 held out, it reached a mean Pearson δ of 0.26 on Norman and 0.07 on Adamson, against 0.66 and 0.81 for the training mean on the same perturbations and genes (Figure 3D). It predicted the direction of Adamson knockdowns but not their magnitude (ρ = 0.10). 

Perturbation in organoids: predicted effects mostly track gene expression 

In HD organoids, the per-cell-type effect of knocking out a gene was strongly correlated across methods (Nicheformer–CellOracle ρ = 0.87), largely because the target's mean expression per cell type correlated with the predicted effect at ρ = 0.97 for CellOracle, 0.90 for Geneformer V2, 0.89 for Nicheformer, 0.85 for Geneformer, 0.79 for scGPT and 0.76 for scFoundation (378 gene×cell-type pairs, Figure 4A). With scGPT's original stochastic input, effects were almost uniform (ρ = 0.04) because re-sampling the input genes moved the embedding as much as the deletion (Supplementary Table S32). Fine-tuning as a cell-type classifier increased predicted effects 3.06-fold for Geneformer, 3.05-fold for Nicheformer and 1.32-fold for scFoundation, while conserving their ranking (ρ = 0.93, 0.94 and 0.96, Supplementary Figure S7E).

Isogenic validation: no model separates knockout effects from background 

With one library per line and time point, the real knockout effects did not clearly exceed differences between clones and donors: ASPA KO versus c107 gave 1,777 DEGs over 20 cell types and clone E30 versus E27 gave 1,600, and GALC KO (2,983) and KI (2,439) fell within the range of healthy donors versus HD2.2 (1,519–16,856, Supplementary Tables S41–S44). With knockouts simulated in the parental cells, no model correlated with the ASPA effect (ρ = −0.32 to 0.30, all p ≥ 0.17) or the GALC KO effect (ρ ≤ 0.06, 15 cell types), and none was knockout-specific in any of the four comparisons (Figure 4C). No Fisher combination of ASPA and GALC KO was significant (lowest p = 0.30, Figure 4D), no test survived correction (lowest q = 0.59, 52 tests), and the only positive association was the gene-independent number of cells per cell type (ρ = 0.50 for ASPA, Fisher p = 0.006). A cell-level ground truth followed cell number (ρ = 0.88 for GALC KO), which explains an apparent scFoundation association with GALC KO (ρ = 0.44, 0.10 after controlling for it, Supplementary Figure S7). With ASPA absent from 18 of 27 cell types, most of its predicted effects are numerically zero. ASPA acts mainly in mature oligodendrocytes [35], which are rare in organoids at this stage. The magnitudes tell the same story: every model predicted a larger mean effect for GALC than for ASPA (7.9-fold for scGPT with fixed input, 22-fold for CellOracle, 82–314-fold for Geneformer, while ASPA effects were near zero for Nicheformer and scFoundation), whereas both real knockouts were close to their negative contrasts (median ratio of summed |log2 fold change| 1.14 for ASPA and 1.05 for GALC, Supplementary Table S97). ASPA therefore tests a structural limit rather than predictive ability. The third scenario, 4H leukodystrophy, has a broadly expressed target, POLR3A, but no isogenic control. Against the five healthy donors, the two POLR3A patients differed by 645 DEGs over 22 cell types, 467 of them in astrocytes, and exceeded all ten healthy-donor negative contrasts in only 3 of 22 cell types. No method tracked the patient effect: the POLR3A deletion correlated with it at ρ = −0.29 to 0.36 (all p ≥ 0.099), within the range of the same predictions against the healthy-donor negatives (highest ρ −0.01 to 0.50) and no higher than the POLR3B control (ρ −0.22 to 0.22), so no prediction met the specificity criterion (Figure 4E, Supplementary Tables S91–S95). The only association that survived correction within the 4H family (24 tests) was the number of cells per cell type (ρ = 0.71, q = 0.005), which correlated as strongly with every negative contrast (ρ = 0.63–0.84). 

A larger model version improves annotation but not perturbation 

We repeated every Geneformer row with the V2-316M release (Supplementary Table S6). V2 annotated better than V1 on immune cells (0.950 versus 0.887), Tabula Muris lung (0.900 versus 0.839), the biopsies (0.942 versus 0.605) and from human to mouse (0.914 versus 0.642), but not HD organoids (0.532 versus 0.625). Perturbation results did not change: in K562 knockout magnitude was not associated with effect size (ρ = −0.03 Norman, 0.10 Adamson), in organoids V2 effects followed target expression more closely than V1 (ρ = 0.90 versus 0.85), and neither version tracked the isogenic ASPA (V2 ρ = −0.08, V1 0.26) or GALC KO effects (−0.29, −0.40). The V2 network stayed near chance against K562 knockdown targets (FE@50 1.02). Scale therefore helped annotation and left perturbation unchanged.

The expression-matched specificity test explains why predicted effects follow target expression (Figure 8). Across 378 gene×cell-type pairs, the observed effect correlated with the target's mean expression at ρ = 0.65 (scGPT, on 1,000 stratified cells with seeded but not fixed input, or 0.79 with the fixed input of Figure 4A, Supplementary Table S101), 0.81 (Geneformer), 0.86 (Nicheformer), 0.81 (scFoundation) and 0.97 (CellOracle). For scGPT, Geneformer and Nicheformer this came from how many cells express the gene: over expressing cells only, the effect no longer tracked expression (ρ −0.09 to 0.09). Compared with deleting 20 control genes of the same expression in the same cell, deleting the target produced a specific excess shift (z > 2) in a minority of pairs: 20.5% for scGPT, 27.9% for Geneformer, 17.6% for Nicheformer, 35.3% for scFoundation and 11.7% for CellOracle. A random-gene null itself depends on expression. Removing the non-specific component did not improve agreement with real knockouts. In K562, the specific effect correlated with the number of DEGs or the energy distance at ρ −0.16 to 0.17 (all p ≥ 0.13). In the isogenic organoids neither raw nor specific effects tracked ASPA or GALC KO beyond the negative contrasts (all positive associations p ≥ 0.07, Figure 8B). In the CHOOSE screen, deleting the target shifted embeddings as much as deleting an expression-matched control gene (median ratio 1.00 for scGPT, 0.98 for Geneformer). 

Isogenic organoids: disease phenotypes 

Disease phenotypes were assessed against the genotype-verified isogenic controls at matched days (Figure 5, Supplementary Figure S4). Oligodendrocytes were rare in both control lines (1–19 cells per library), so lineage proportions are tentative. GALC KO did not deplete the lineage (OPC 6.4% versus 1.7% at day 100, 4.6% versus 3.7% at day 150), GALC KI organoids contained more OPCs (7.7% and 8.1%), and ASPA KO did not differ from c107 (OPC 3.4% versus 3.0% at day 100, 4.6% versus 4.8% at day 150). With library-level statistics, GALC KO, KI and patient organoids shared up-regulation of oxidative phosphorylation (NES 2.96, 2.49 and 3.16), TNF-α/NF-κB and mTORC1 hallmarks, whereas ASPA KO showed the same hallmarks down-regulated against c107 (oxidative phosphorylation NES −2.73) with no whole-organoid DEG. Myelination terms were not enriched. The opposite signs against the two control lines suggest differences between lines and libraries as much as genotype, so we do not interpret these signatures mechanistically. 

Gene regulatory networks: co-expression is a strong baseline 

Across K562, organoids and lung-cancer biopsies, FM-derived networks recovered known regulatory targets at close to chance (Figure 6). For Adamson knockdown targets, the best expression-matched FE@50 was 1.32 (scFoundation attention), equal to the expression prior (1.31) and below co-expression (1.61). The attention of scGPT fine-tuned on Adamson scored 0.72. For K562 ChIP-seq targets the best FM network (scGPT attention, 2.20) was also below co-expression (2.87), and CellOracle reached 1.57 on its three scorable hubs, where co-expression reached 3.64. An apparent precision of 0.90 for BHLHE40 reflects the 71% of genes it binds in K562 (enrichment 1.27). In the biopsies, the best FM network (scGPT embedding, 1.41) matched co-expression (1.40), and with patients as the unit of analysis (pseudobulk, nominal p < 0.01) the expression-matched Geneformer V2 network exceeded it (FE@50 2.90 versus 2.04), although on few hubs (Supplementary Figure S6, Supplementary Table S90). 

A public mosaic screen: apparent wins track cell complexity

In biopsies of EGFR-mutant lung cancer before targeted therapy (TN, 7 patients) and at residual disease (RD, 8 patients) [23], every method except Nicheformer placed the cancer compartment first after an in silico EGFR knockout, and so did the naive EGFR-expression predictors (Figure 7). ## Discussion 

Evaluated against simple baselines on the same data, current single-cell FMs helped in specific settings: mapping a reference across species, the direction of perturbation responses and, with patient-level ground truth, tumour regulatory networks. Within a species their embeddings support label transfer about as well as principal components. Their zero-shot in silico knockouts do not predict how strongly a real knockout perturbs a cell line and, in organoids, largely restate where the target gene is expressed. Their gene–gene networks do not recover knockdown targets or ChIP-seq binding better than co-expression. These results extend to organoids, a public organoid screen and tumour biopsies the conclusions reached for perturbation prediction in cell lines [7,8] and for zero-shot embeddings [5,6]. The isogenic organoids were the most demanding and least conclusive part of the benchmark. Knockout and parental organoids share genetic background, but with one library per line and time point the knockout effect could not be separated from differences between clones or healthy donors, and against this background no model predicted which cell types the ASPA or GALC knockout affects. The three organoid scenarios show what an in silico knockout needs in order to be tested: an expressed target, a controlled genetic background and replicates, and no comparison had all three. ASPA, with the best replicates, marks a structural limit: a deletion changes a cell's input only where the gene is detected, and its cells of action are rare at this stage. POLR3A, broadly expressed, shows that expression alone is not sufficient without a controlled background. In silico deletion is also cell-autonomous: it cannot represent effects that a mutation exerts on other cells, such as the accumulation of N-acetylaspartate in Canavan disease. Apparent successes depended on how the ground truth was built, so a model chosen on one validation would be misleading. Agreement between methods, often used as a surrogate for validity, was here mostly shared dependence on target expression, which a naive predictor reproduces. In public data, apparent wins followed cell complexity and patient identity. The specificity test gives a mechanism for this dependence and a practical control. Deleting a gene from a cell's input changes the embedding roughly as much as deleting any other gene of similar expression, so

the per-cell-type effect mostly measures how many cells express the target. Only a minority of gene×cell-type pairs exceed an expression-matched null, and the excess does not predict real knockout outcomes. We propose that in silico perturbation results be reported against such a null. A random-gene null is not sufficient, because it inherits the expression dependence it is meant to remove. The test is implemented in fmaudit. Fine-tuning did not change this picture. Whatever the regime, no adapted backbone agreed better with isogenic ground truth than its zero-shot version. Adapting the backbone rescaled predicted effects (about three-fold for Geneformer and Nicheformer) without changing their ranking, so it altered sensitivity rather than correctness. Supervised models trained on Perturb-seq did not beat the mean training shift for unseen genes and cannot be applied to systems without perturbation data, such as patient organoids. The checks applied in this study show how easily such problems arise. Without them, a control matrix normalised twice would have altered the ground truth for all GALC comparisons. A fine-tuning run that silently fell back to the pretrained model would have suggested that fine-tuning leaves predictions unchanged. An input re-sampled before and after each deletion would have made scGPT's effects unrelated to the deleted gene. A cell-level ground truth would have rewarded predictors of cell number. Table 1 summarises the resulting recommendations, including a target-detection check now included in fmaudit, for researchers who want to apply FMs to their own data. Limitations: each organoid line was sequenced as one library per time point, so the isogenic tests have few replicates (five versus two libraries for ASPA, one residual degree of freedom for GALC) and 15–20 cell types each. Designs with replicate differentiations are needed before isogenic organoids can rank models. ASPA is barely expressed in the parental cells, as expected for a gene of mature oligodendrocytes, so its deletion left most predictions unchanged. In silico deletion also represents only cell-autonomous effects. The 4H comparison is not isogenic, and the patients' POLR3A variants, which were not available to us, may reduce rather than abolish function, so deletion is an approximation. FMs see only expression, so the G553R knock-in, without enzyme activity [27,28] but with a normally expressed transcript (median log2 ratio −0.18 versus HD2.2), was compared with the knockout deletion. Missense variants will require models that combine expression with sequence. The deletion protocol was also applied to CRISPRa for comparability. Organoid GRN references lack orthogonal ground truth, and the biopsies come from few patients. Newer models may behave differently.

## Conclusions 

Across annotation, perturbation and GRN inference, and from cell lines to isogenic disease organoids, a public organoid screen and tumour biopsies, single-cell FMs were matched by simple baselines in most comparisons, and no model predicted the effects of real knockouts in isogenic organoids beyond negative contrasts. Isogenic disease models can provide the ground truth that in silico perturbation needs, provided that they include replicate libraries and negative contrasts. Together with task-matched baselines and pipeline checks, they should become a standard component of FM benchmarks.
