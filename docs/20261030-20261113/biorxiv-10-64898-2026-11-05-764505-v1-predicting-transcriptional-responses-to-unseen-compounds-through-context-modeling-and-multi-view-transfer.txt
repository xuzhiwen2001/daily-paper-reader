# Predicting Transcriptional Responses to Unseen Compounds through Context Modeling and Multi-View Transfer 

Yuqi Lei 1, Hanzhe Liang 1, Keqin Peng 1, and Jun Wen 1⋆

Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence, Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates 

{yuqi.lei, hanzhe.liang, keqin.peng, jun.wen}@mbzuai.ac.ae 

Abstract. Predicting drug-induced transcriptional responses could support in silico screen-ing of new compounds, yet existing models barely use the compound: on a drug-blind L1000 benchmark, removing the drug input from seven published models changes accuracy by at most 0.012 Pearson correlation. They capture shared cellular and experimental variation while overlooking the compound-specific signals that distinguish drug effects. We present UnDetect, a framework that separates contextual variation from compound effects to predict responses to transcriptionally unprofiled compounds. UnDetect integrates chemical structure, annotated targets, mechanisms of action and Cell Painting morphology into a normalized multi-view ker-nel that accommodates incomplete compound information, and transfers residual responses within and across profiled cell lines, so that pharmacological and phenotypic relationships in-form predictions beyond chemical similarity. On a drug-blind benchmark, where the paired control reveals the assay plate, UnDetect is the only model to beat a network that ignores the compound, in every fold; on a within-plate score that is zero for any compound-blind prediction, it reaches 0.117 against 0.092 for the strongest published model. On DeepCE’s benchmark it reaches 0.602 Pearson correlation against the reported 0.491. Transfer across cell lines improves 28 of 28 LINCS L1000 and, at the two higher doses, 47 and 49 of 50 Tahoe-100M cell-line evaluations. These results show that separating context from compound effects and integrat-ing complementary compound information improves transcriptional prediction and extends the utility of perturbation atlases for virtual screening.

## 1 Introduction 

The transcriptional response of cells to a compound is a central readout in drug discovery: it reveals the compound’s mechanism of action, connects it to other compounds and to disease signatures, and suggests new uses for existing drugs [29,6]. Such profiling is now performed at atlas scale: the LINCS L1000 assay has measured 978 landmark genes after treatment with nearly 20,000 compounds [29], and Tahoe-100M has profiled 100 million single cells across 50 cancer lines [37]. Yet every new compound starts without any profile, and profiling each candidate across many cell lines is slow and costly. A model that predicts the response to an unseen compound, one never profiled, would turn these atlases into an in silico screen for new chemistry. The task is hard for three reasons. First, the compound-specific part of a response is small compared with the cellular context, the cell line and the assay plate that all compounds measured together share. Second, chemical structure is a weak guide: a compound’s measured responses in other cell lines are a strong prior for a new line, whereas chemical fingerprints predict a held-out compound poorly [21,18]. Third, information about compounds is partial. Atlases are far from complete: in the drug-blind L1000 benchmark [3] a compound is profiled in 9 of 164 cell lines on average, almost always on a single plate per line, so less than 6% of

Existing deep models encode the compound with a fingerprint or graph network [24,23,36,25,17], a pretrained molecular encoder [15,32,12] or a knowledge graph [10,12], and learn context and compound jointly by conditioning an expression decoder on that encoding. These models barely use the compound. Bai et al. [3] retrained seven of them on a common drug-blind L1000 benchmark: replacing the drug input by zeros changes the accuracy by at most 0.012 , and a multilayer perceptron that receives no drug information matches every one of them, as simple baselines do for genetic perturbations [1,35]. The reason is the plate. Each treated profile is paired with a control profile shared by all compounds on the same plate, so the control identifies the plate, and a plate-response baseline, which predicts a compound by the mean response of the other training compounds on its plate and uses no model and no drug information, outperforms five of the seven published models. Joint training trades one part for the other: the strongest published model, XPert, does learn the compound, as removing its compound input costs a third of its plate-centered score, but it falls below the drug-free network on the standard score. Once the plate and cell line are taken into account, the response that remains is small, but it is shared across cell lines and predictable from how compounds relate to each other. We present UnDetect (UNseen Drug dEcomposition for Transcriptional rEsponse prediCTion), which fits the context first and the compound effect second (fig. 1). A backbone predicts the response that any compound would show in a sample. A multi-view kernel over chemical structure, annotated targets, mechanism of action and Cell Painting morphology, normalized so that compounds lacking a view are not shrunk, generalizes the residual responses of profiled compounds to unprofiled ones, shared across cell lines and refined within each, and a bilinear interaction lets the cellular context rescale the compound effect gene by gene. Every step after the backbone has a closed-form solution. Our contributions are threefold. (i) A closed-form model for unseen compounds that fits the compound effect after the context, shares it across cell lines and lets the context rescale it gene by gene (section 2). (ii) A multi-view compound kernel normalized so that the effect a compound can receive does not shrink with the views it lacks (section 2.3). (iii) The finding that the paired control encodes the plate, and plate-centered scores that isolate the compound-specific response (section 3.1). On the drug-blind benchmark UnDetect is the only model above the drug-free baseline on the standard score, in every fold. On the within-plate score, which is zero for any prediction that ignores the compound, it raises the correlation of the strongest published model from 0.092 to 0.117 , and it picks out a compound’s own measured signature among those of its plate in 27% of cases, where that model does so in 19%. For compounds known only by structure the two are level; UnDetect’s lead comes from the pharmacological and phenotypic views. On DeepCE’s benchmark it reaches a Pearson correlation of 0.602 , where DeepCE reported 0.491 (section 3).

2.2 Context backbone 

The backbone fθ sees no compound information, and its residuals 

ri = yi − fθ (xi) (2) are what the compound effect must explain. It is the drug-free network of Bai et al. [3], a multilayer perceptron on the control profile (three hidden layers of 2,048 units), trained on training compounds only and then frozen; because the control identifies the plate, it absorbs cellular and plate context. Where the control has already been removed, the backbone is the training mean of each line. Fitting the backbone first keeps the large, compound-independent part of the response from swamping the compound part; jointly trained models trade one for the other (section 3.3). Supplementary Appendix A.1 gives the training details.

Related designs. Published models couple context and compound inside one network (section 3.2), also through a nonlinear decoder over additive latent embeddings [19,15]. Tensor completion over compound, gene and cell line needs every compound profiled in some cell line [16], and response decompositions [21] and observed-statistics models, which express a response through the target line’s control profile (Rhaister-O) or through a small panel of drugs profiled in it (Rhaister) [30], carry a compound’s measured responses to new cell lines; for an unseen compound, UnDetect predicts these compound terms from its views. 

Model selection. The ridge λ, the scales a, b and κ, the number q of components (and ρ, σ when doses are resolved) are chosen from fixed grids by the benchmark’s own score on validation compounds, which are disjoint from training and test compounds, or by compound-grouped inner cross-validation on LINCS and Tahoe-100M; each test set is scored once. All compound views are fixed annotations or measurements in other assays; no response of a test compound is used at any stage. Supplementary Table 6 lists the grids. 

Computational cost. After the backbone, UnDetect solves linear systems only; one eigendecomposition of 

˜KDD serves every λ and every leave-one-compound-out value. On one A100 GPU, fitting UnDetect on a fold of the drug-blind benchmark takes about 5 minutes (backbone and kernel terms 3.2 minutes, interaction 2 minutes, 5 GB of GPU memory), against 268 minutes for training XPert on the same fold. 

## 3 Experiments 

3.1 Datasets and setup 

We use four unseen-compound benchmarks (table 1). On the drug-blind benchmark of Bai et al. [3] the response is the treated profile minus its paired control, and the metric is the mean over test samples of the Pearson correlation between true and predicted responses across all 978 genes (PCC DEG , named as in [3]). The other three use plate-normalized Level-5 signatures [29], or pseudobulk deltas for Tahoe-100M, and report the Pearson correlation per signature or per (compound, line), averaged over lines. The drug-blind and LINCS benchmarks have one dose and time point (10 μM, 24 h); Tahoe-100M is scored separately at each of its three doses, and only DeepCE’s benchmark resolves doses within one model. Supplementary Table 21 lists the data sources and versions. 

Plate structure. In the drug-blind benchmark, the control takes 9,951 distinct values, exactly one per pair of cell line and detection-plate set: every treated sample on a plate shares the same control vector, up to 321 samples per control. Because the split holds out compounds rather than plates, 90% of test samples share their control, and hence their plate, with training samples, so a model that receives the control can recognize the plate. The other three benchmarks remove the shared plate response before modeling (robust z-scores against the population of the same plate [29], or subtraction of the plate’s DMSO profile); on LINCS we also hold out compounds together with their plate maps.

Plate-centered metrics. Plate structure inflates PCC DEG for any model that can see the control, and a prediction of the plate mean is of no use for a compound that has not yet been assayed. We therefore also score the deviation of a compound from the other compounds on its plate. For a test sample i with training plate-mates, let mi be their mean response; the plate-centered score is 

PCC plate (i) = corr (yi − mi, ˆyi − mi

), (8) the correlation across genes, averaged over test samples. The error of mi enters both terms, so a compound-blind prediction can score above zero. The within-plate score centers truth and prediction each on its own mean over the test compounds of a plate p, PCC within (p, d ) = corr( ypd − ¯yp, ˆypd − ¯ˆyp), averaged over (plate, compound) pairs. Any prediction constant within a plate, and hence any prediction that ignores the com-pound, scores zero. PCC within is our main compound-specific score and PCC plate , covering more samples, its complement; all our settings are chosen by PCC DEG , so neither is optimized. Samples without a training plate-mate are scored with PCC DEG and reported separately. This is the chemical-perturbation counterpart of centering on systematic variation in genetic perturbation benchmarks [33]; here the systematic compo-nent has a concrete source, the shared control. Supplementary Appendix A.11 defines PCC within in full, and Table 18 gives it per fold. 

3.2 Compared methods 

On the drug-blind benchmark we compare with the seven published models retrained by Bai et al. [3], CIGER [23], MultiDCP [36], PRnet [25], PertDiT [17], DeepCE [24], TranSiGen [32] and XPert [12], scored from their released predictions, and with their drug-free network, a multilayer perceptron on the control alone. The three strongest of them couple the compound and the context in different ways. DeepCE [24] encodes the molecular graph with a graph convolutional network and relates its substructures to genes by multi-head attention. TranSiGen [32] learns latent spaces of the control and treated profiles with variational autoencoders and maps the control latent, together with a pretrained molecular embedding, to the treated one. XPert [12] turns the control profile into gene tokens that attend to the atoms of a pretrained three-dimensional molecular encoder, and adds a compound embedding pretrained on a graph of protein interactions, drug–target links and drug–drug similarities. The plate-response baseline predicts a test sample by the mean response of the training samples that share its control; it uses no compound information and fits no parameters. The 

reference predicts each test sample by the backbone plus the mean residual of the same compound’s other test samples (other cell lines); it uses test responses and marks how much a compound’s own measurements add. On DeepCE’s benchmark we compare with the models reported by Pham et al. [24], our reproduction of DeepCE, a tuned Morgan nearest-neighbor search, and a neural network given the same compound views as UnDetect. On LINCS and Tahoe-100M we compare with the training mean of each line and with per-line kernel regressions on chemistry alone or on all views.

Each component of UnDetect raises the compound-specific score (table 4): the chemistry kernel gives about half of the gain, the multi-view kernel most of the rest, and the interaction improves both metrics further, in every fold with intervals above zero. ## 4 Discussion 

Current models for unprofiled compounds score well on the drug-blind benchmark for a reason unrelated to the compound: the paired control identifies the plate, and the plate predicts most of the response. Fitting the compound after this context, UnDetect attains, without any measurement of the compound, 60% of the gain of a reference that uses the compound’s measured responses in other cell lines, and its average with XPert 86%. As for genetic perturbations [1,35], a simple model with the right decomposition, fitted in minutes, is a strong baseline for end-to-end models. The compound information that generalizes is pharmacological and phenotypic (table 5), which fits two uses. In drug repurposing, approved and investigational drugs already come with annotated targets and mechanisms. For new chemistry, one Cell Painting assay supplies the most informative view at a fraction of the effort of transcriptional profiling across cell lines, a practical first experiment; profiles from a single cell line (A549) already generalize to 164 lines. Protein-interaction priors, in contrast, add nothing beyond node degree, so network priors should be calibrated against rewired nulls. Drug-blind evaluations should report a plate-aware score, such as PCC within , or keep plate-mates together in the split; otherwise a model that ignores the compound can rank first. All cell lines in our evaluations are seen in training, and one assay (A549 Cell Painting) supplies the phenotypic view; for unseen cell lines [30,21], the interaction through the control profile is a natural starting point.

A.1 Implementation details 

Backbone. On the drug-blind benchmark the backbone is the drug-free network of Bai et al. [3]: three hidden layers of 2,048 ReLU units with dropout 0.1, loss 12 MSE + 12 (1 − PCC) , Adam with learning rate 

10 −4, batch 512 and gradient norm clipped at 1, at most 500 epochs, early stopping on the PCC DEG of validation compounds with patience 50. It is trained on training compounds only and then frozen. On the Level-5 (LINCS, DeepCE) and Tahoe-100M benchmarks the control has already been removed, and the only compound-free inputs are the cell line and, on DeepCE’s benchmark, the dose; the least-squares backbone is then the training mean of each line (and dose), and all compound information enters through the kernel terms. Retrained under two further seeds, in an earlier configuration (kernel terms with a neural head, no interaction), the five-fold mean PCC DEG of the backbone and of the model on it changed by at most 0.0003, and the gain over the drug-free network was positive in all 15 fold–seed runs. 

Kernel normalization. Structure receives a view weight of 0.28 to 0.31 in every fold, so every diagonal entry of ∆ is positive; the implementation clamps it at 10 −12 , so a compound whose views all had zero weight would keep a zero row and be predicted by the backbone. 

Selection grids are listed in table 6. On the drug-blind benchmark λ is chosen with the shared term alone, then (a, b ) jointly, then (κ, q ), each on validation compounds. A.8 Controls for the compound terms 

Permutation null: one permutation of compound identities, drawn within each group of compounds that have the same views, is applied to all four view kernels at once; every sample of a compound therefore receives the same permuted compound, and plates, cell lines, samples and view coverage are unchanged.
