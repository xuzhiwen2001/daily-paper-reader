Abstract 

The human immune system differs markedly between individuals and changes in response to infection, vaccination and treatment 1–3 . AI virtual cells are being built to simulate cellular responses to perturbation 4–6 . Yet these models, like most immune-response predictors, describe each person by a single pre-challenge measurement 7–11 and forecast change from patterns pooled across people. Dig-ital twins, updated with every new observation of an individual, could forecast how that person’s immune state changes 4,12 . Here we present ImmuneTwins, which builds these twins from single-cell profiles by personalizing a shared virtual-cell model with each individual’s observations. In two external popu-lations, ImmuneTwins reduced immune-age error by 32% and 42% relative to the best existing method. During repeated influenza vaccination, its twins pre-dicted the direction of immune-cell change 35% more accurately than the best published method, and their error fell by 27% as observations accumulated. The twins revealed that immune cells and plasma proteins recover about half their deviation from a persistent personal reference within 90 days, whereas response size varies between challenges. They further localized B-cell remodelling in exceptional longevity and anticipated patient-specific immune trajectories during myeloma treatment. # Introduction 

The immune system protects against infection and maintains tissue homeostasis through the coordinated activity of diverse cell populations 13 . Genetic background, microbial encounters and environmental exposures shape these populations through-out life, producing substantial differences in immune composition and function between people 1,14–18 . Large-scale profiling has linked immune variation to infection suscepti-bility 19 and yielded measures of immune age and health 20–22 that place each person within population variation and are associated with disease burden, survival and treat-ment outcomes 23–26 . These measures, however, are usually developed within particular cohorts 26 , and their estimates can shift systematically when applied to populations that differ in age distribution or measurement conditions 27 . Group-level comparisons can also obscure differences between individuals of the same age or diagnosis. In mono-genic immune disorders, inter-individual differences account for more variance in many immune measurements than disease category does 25 . Age or diagnosis alone therefore provides an incomplete account of a person’s immune composition and function. Mod-els of individual immune state need to capture these differences and remain accurate when applied across populations. Single-cell atlases allow these differences to be examined across immune lineages, tissues and human populations 28–31 , providing the data on which virtual cells simu-late cell states and their responses to perturbation 4,32,33 . Multimodal integration and foundation models learn universal representations of cell states from large reference datasets 34–37 . Models such as PaSCient and PULSAR combine these cell-level repre-sentations to characterize patient phenotypes 11,38,39 . For the immune system, virtual 2

A virtual cell trained in one cohort and applied to another population encounters differences in cellular distributions and study conditions 27 , which can bias its individual-level predictions 44 and make it unclear whether a difference reflects immune state or cohort-specific effects 45 . Comparisons across populations therefore require virtual cells that preserve individual biological differences despite these changes in study context. Following the same person over time reveals which immune features remain stable and which change after immune challenge 2,46 . Previous exposures can leave lasting effects 47,48 , and measurements made before a challenge can help predict the subse-quent response 7–9,40 . Predicting such responses is a central task of virtual cells 4,33 :cellular perturbation models and multicellular foundation models predict how immune cells and individuals respond to an intervention 5,10,11,49,50 . Yet these predictions describe each person by a single measurement and forecast change from patterns pooled across people. The individual’s own earlier observations, which longitudinal studies show to carry stable and lasting features, are not used to anticipate how that person will change. Modelling each person from their own record of observations is the premise of digital twins. Digital twins build such individual models and update them as new observa-tions are made 12 . Organ digital twins reproduce the physiology of individual ex vivo human lungs to evaluate therapies 51 , and longitudinal clinical models use each person’s recorded history to forecast disease trajectories and complete missing mea-surements 52,53 . A proposed next step is to combine structure shared across patients with each person’s own trajectory, distinguishing stable personal baselines from indi-vidual change 53 . # Results 

ImmuneTwins builds a personal digital twin for each individual on a shared base model, a virtual-cell model that learns a patient-level representation of immune-cell states and generates an individual’s future cells from their current cells (Fig. 1). Each twin adds two personal components to the base model: personal history, the responses the individual has already completed, and a personal reference, the individ-ual’s usual composition estimated from earlier resting visits. We evaluated whether the shared representation generalized across populations, tested whether personal obser-vations distinguished the future cell states of individuals after vaccination, identified the persistent individual feature on which these forecasts rely and applied the twins to exceptional longevity and myeloma treatment (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). 

## Cross-population immune-age prediction 

ImmuneTwins learned patient-level aggregation and age prediction from 417 partic-ipants in the Chinese Immune Multi-Omics Atlas (CIMA) 31 . To test whether this representation generalized beyond the context in which it was learned, the fitted model was applied zero-shot to the Immunobiology of Aging cohort (hereafter Immunobiol-ogy) 2,55 and to the Northern European ancestry OneK1K cohort 29 , which differ from CIMA in ancestry, study source and age distribution (Fig. 2A,B). Within CIMA, ImmuneTwins performed comparably to the strongest of the five comparator methods 11,27,37,38,56 (Fig. 2C). ## Return to a personal immune reference 

The dependence of these forecasts on personal observations raised the question of which individual features persist. Personal histories contained persistent individual features alongside transient responses to vaccination. Most cell subsets and plasma pro-teins retained their relative ordering between participants across two years (adjusted for age, sex, cytomegalovirus status and, for proteins, assay plate), whereas early responses, late responses and net changes over each season showed little cross-year correlation (Fig. 4A). 8

In the second-year forecast window, deviations from this personal reference were followed by change towards it 46 , with roughly half of the deviation recovered by day 90 for both cell subsets and proteins (Fig. 4C). This return occurred in all seven vaccinated and unvaccinated windows examined, spanning one week to nearly six months (Fig. 4D). For cell subsets, it persisted after correction for cell-sampling noise and was weakest in the first week after vaccination (Supplementary Fig. 7). In every window and feature set, forecasts based on the personal reference were more accurate than those based on the population reference ( q < 0.05 for all 21 comparisons), distinguishing this return from regression towards the population mean (Fig. 4E). ImmuneTwins forecasts with this return. Its personal-reference layer moves each forecast composition from the current state towards the individual’s reference by a return coefficient, which is estimated from training participants in each cross-validation fold. When the reference was defined as in the twin, from all earlier resting visits, the coefficient estimated by the twin agreed with the coefficient measured directly from participants’ compositions, and both exceeded the coefficient obtained with references from the two first-year visits alone (Fig. 4H). This difference reflects the reliability of the reference: a reference built from fewer visits is noisier and attenuates the measured return. The twin therefore returns each individual towards their own reference at the rate present in the data. 9

## B-cell remodelling in supercentenarians

Both trends were sig-nificant in each cohort and persisted after adjustment for lineage fraction ( q < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 9), suggesting that B-cell states carry age-related information beyond B-cell numbers. ## Immune trajectories during treatment 

Unlike vaccination, myeloma induction therapy imposes a sustained perturbation, and no resting visits are available during treatment to estimate a personal reference; twins in this setting therefore relied on personal history alone. Among 20 patients receiving myeloma induction therapy 3,59 , host immune trajectories differed even within the same tumour response category (Fig. 6A). Notably, CD4 T-cell fractions rebounded late in treatment, while B-cell fractions continued to fall and CD8 T-cell and natural killer-cell fractions decreased (Fig. 6F). Matched complete blood counts (CBC) from nineteen patients supported an increase in estimated CD4 T-cell counts during this period. ImmuneTwins anticipated these later differences from each patient’s response over the first two treatment cycles, predicting the end-of-induction cellular distribu-tion more accurately than the base model and published perturbation models 10,49,57 

(Fig. 6B,C). This prediction depended on personal history: removing history or sub-stituting that of another patient matched on regimen and current state increased prediction error (Fig. 6D). The forecasts captured individual differences in subse-quent cytotoxic-programme decline and myeloid remodelling (Supplementary Fig. 11). Associations between predicted and measured changes persisted after adjustment for regimen and response grade (Fig. 6E), indicating that personal early responses pro-vided information about later host immune changes beyond the tumour response category. # Discussion 

Here we present ImmuneTwins, a virtual-cell framework that builds a digital twin of each person’s immune system from single-cell profiles. Its patient representation, learned in one cohort, generalized zero-shot to populations of different ancestry and age, where it estimated immune age more accurately than existing methods. Twins that adapt a shared virtual-cell model to each person’s completed responses and per-sonal reference forecast immune-cell changes after vaccination and during myeloma treatment more accurately than published methods. These twins differ from earlier personalized predictions in how the individual enters the model. Studies of baseline immune states that predict vaccine responses 7–9 and virtual-cell models of perturbation responses 5,10,49,50 describe a person by a single measurement. Medical digital twins are personalized through organ physiology 51 or recorded history 52 , and separating stable personal baselines from individual change has been proposed as a next step for longitudinal models 53 . ImmuneTwins imple-ments this separation in a virtual-cell model: personal history conveys how a person has responded, the personal reference conveys the state to which they return, and both enter a shared model as observed inputs. This changes how a virtual cell gener-alizes. Virtual cells still struggle in contexts not seen during training and often fail to outperform simple baselines 6,43 . Each person is such a context, and what defines it is not contained in any single sample. Supplied as an observed record, this information lets a shared model adapt to a new individual without retraining, so that a twin can be built from a few visits, refined at each new visit and queried counterfactually. Because the twins operate on cells, they can also locate where individual differ-ences arise. Similar overall phenotypes can rest on different cellular foundations: in 13

Tracing such differences to cell states turns broad descriptions of immune enhancement or decline into testable biological questions. Blood counts are read against population reference ranges and immune age against people of the same age 23–25 ,comparisons that discard what is specific to the person. We propose that each per-son’s own state should serve as the reference, and virtual-cell digital twins make this practical. The same view recasts immune ageing: the conver-gence of older adults towards shared immune levels 23 may reflect drifting personal states, a weakening return to them, or both, and twins trained on records spanning decades are the means to tell these apart. The question for each person would then be not how old their immune system looks, but whether it still returns to its own state.

## Patient representation learning 

The patient encoder projected UCE features to 128 dimensions and combined them by attention pooling 65 . Attention weights were normalized across cells within each visit, and the weighted sum of projected features formed the patient representation. Pooling was invariant to cell order 66 and accommodated different cell counts 67,68 .Projection and pooling parameters were learned jointly by predicting chronological age in CIMA, using a regression head and squared error on ages standardized within the fitting partition. UCE parameters remained fixed. After model selection, the model was refitted on all CIMA participants for external application. The age head was retained for external age prediction. The cell projection and pooling layers were kept fixed in the virtual-cell model. 

## Virtual-cell model 

The base model of every twin was a virtual-cell model, consisting of the patient encoder and a response decoder shared by all individuals, that generated an individual’s future cells from their current cells. For each cell zc of the current visit, the response decoder generated a virtual cell ˆ yc at the target visit, conditioned on the current visit and response phase, and each virtual cell carried the cell-type label of the current cell from which it was generated. Cells were sampled independently at current and target visits. Learned query vectors (slots) attended separately to projected UCE features and RNA coordinates, followed by self-attention among the slots 69 . Their pooled output, the patient representation and an encoding of the RNA mean, standard deviations and covariance formed a response context. The response decoder first predicted mean and covariance changes within fixed response bases obtained by singular value decomposition of measured changes in training participants. ## Model training and evaluation 

After representation learning, the affine response model, shape refinement and history adaptation were fitted sequentially, holding previously fitted components fixed. Fore-casting losses compared virtual-cell and observed distributions and mean responses, with additional terms for response direction, magnitude and between-participant response geometry. History learning compared own-history response errors with errors obtained using shuffled histories and the fixed base model. Losses were averaged across seasons within each participant, then across participants. Data were split by participant, keeping all visits and vaccination seasons from each participant in the same fold. Age prediction used five CIMA folds, giving out-of-fold (OOF) predictions within CIMA; external predictions were zero-shot, applying the model refitted on all CIMA participants without target-cohort calibration. Sound Life forecasting used five outer folds, with training duration selected within the outer-training participants before refitting. During inner selection of history-adaptation duration, the outer-fold RNA coordinates and base model were kept fixed. The personal-reference layer was then fitted in outer-training participants: K and m by least squares on measured compositions, λ and the variance terms by inner cross-validation, and γ from out-of-sample predictions of neural components refitted within inner folds. 19

Fig. 1 ImmuneTwins builds personal digital twins of the immune system by adapting a shared single-cell model to each individual’s own observations. A , Cells from the current visit are embedded by the frozen Universal Cell Embeddings (UCE) model and pooled by the patient encoder (1) into a patient representation, from which the response decoder (2, 3) predicts the cells at a later visit. The encoder and decoder form the base model shared by all individuals. A digital twin adds two personal components: a history adapter (4) that encodes the individual’s prior responses into a history embedding h, which adapts the decoder, and a correction of the predicted composition towards the personal reference, the individual’s usual composition across earlier resting visits (5). B, The response decoder. Slots attend to cell embeddings and RNA coordinates (2) and are combined with the patient representation into a response context, which sets an affine change in the mean and covariance of the cell distribution; shape refinement then reshapes the distribution while keeping its mean and covariance (3). Through personal gates on shared low-rank maps, h adapts these steps. C, Applications: zero-shot transfer of immune age from the Chinese Immune Multi-Omics Atlas (CIMA) to the Immunobiology of Aging and OneK1K cohorts, in silico exchange of B cells between supercentenarians and controls, and forecasts of individual responses to influenza vaccination and myeloma treatment from personal history. Snowflakes mark frozen models.
