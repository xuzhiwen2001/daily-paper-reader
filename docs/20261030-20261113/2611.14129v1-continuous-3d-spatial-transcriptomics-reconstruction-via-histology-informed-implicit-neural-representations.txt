ABSTRACT 

Three-dimensional spatial transcriptomics (3D ST) enables the anal-ysis of gene-expression patterns across tissue depths. However, 3D ST measurements are acquired from discrete 2D tissue sections, which are often sparsely sampled due to high acquisition costs. To address this limitation, we propose a histology-informed multi-task implicit neural representation (HIM-INR) for continuous 3D gene-expression reconstruction from sparse ST observations. HIM-INR jointly reconstructs gene-expression profiles and histological representations using an INR with a shared backbone, leveraging densely sampled histological images as auxiliary spatial supervi-sion. Once trained, HIM-INR predicts gene expression at arbitrary 3D coordinates without requiring histological images. Experiments on two 3D ST datasets demonstrate improved reconstruction over existing methods, particularly as the number of missing ST sections increases. 

Index Terms — 3D spatial transcriptomics, Implicit neural rep-resentatio (INR) 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Three-dimensional spatial transcriptomics (3D ST) [1] has emerged as a promising approach for investigating the spatial organization of gene expression within tissues. Building on conventional 2D ST [2], which measures gene expression in individual tissue sections, 3D ST enables the characterization of molecular interactions across tis-sue depths and the spatial extent and connectivity of pathological regions. Such capabilities offer new opportunities to investigate cell-to-cell communication across sections, characterize tumor invasion and inflammatory infiltration, and reveal spatial relationships be-tween tissue architecture and molecular states that cannot be fully captured by individual 2D sections [1, 3]. Despite the potential of 3D ST, gene expression is measured only on discrete tissue sections. Furthermore, the high cost of ST mea-surements restricts the number of sections that can be profiled, re-sulting in sparse observations along the tissue depth axis. Previous studies have aligned ST measurements from serial tissue sections into a common spatial coordinate system to reconstruct tissue struc-tures along the depth axis [4, 5]. However, gene-expression infor-mation remains restricted to the observed sections, leaving the con-tinuous distribution of gene expression between them unresolved. These limitations motivate the reconstruction of a continuous 3D gene-expression field from sparsely and discretely observed ST sec-tions. However, this task remains challenging. While gene expres-sion can vary substantially with changes in tissue structure across sections, no direct information on gene expression is available be-tween sparsely sampled ST sections. Consequently, not only con-ventional neighborhood-based interpolation methods but also deep learning-based approaches [6, 7] that model nonlinear relationships between spatial positions and gene expression face difficulties in ac-curately reconstructing gene expression in unobserved regions. To address this challenge, our key idea is to leverage histological images, which provide detailed morphological information about tis-sue structure. Gene-expression patterns are known to be associated with tissue structure and morphology [8, 9]. Moreover, histologi-cal images are commonly acquired alongside ST measurements and are often available from more densely sampled tissue sections due to their substantially lower acquisition cost [10]. Thus, histologi-cal information can provide valuable cues about variations in tissue morphology, even at locations where ST measurements are unavail-able, potentially facilitating continuous 3D gene-expression recon-struction. In this study, we propose a histology-informed multi-task implicit neural representation (HIM-INR) framework for recon-structing gene-expression profiles at arbitrary 3D coordinates from sparsely sampled ST sections, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We base our method on an INR model, which represents continuous signals by learning a function that maps spatial coordinates to corresponding signal values [11]. Using the INR as a shared backbone, our frame-work jointly reconstructs gene-expression profiles and histological representations extracted using a pretrained pathology foundation model designed to capture tissue morphology [12]. By incorporating histological reconstruction as an auxiliary task, the shared INR back-bone receives additional spatial supervision from densely sampled histological sections, even at locations where ST measurements are unavailable. This encourages the model to capture continuous vari-ations in tissue morphology and learn shared spatial representations for gene-expression and histological reconstruction. Importantly, inference requires only 3D coordinates, enabling gene-expression reconstruction even at locations where neither ST measurements nor histological images are available. We evaluate the proposed framework on two publicly available 3D ST datasets and compare it with conventional interpolation meth-ods and INR-based reconstruction approaches. Experimental results demonstrate that our method outperforms existing approaches un-der most evaluation settings, highlighting the effectiveness of incor-porating histological information for reconstructing gene expression profiles at unobserved sections. 2. RELATED WORK 3D ST Reconstruction. Recent studies have explored 3D ST anal-ysis by integrating transcriptomic measurements from serial tissue sections. Most approaches focus on aligning consecutive sections to reconstruct their spatial organization [4, 5]. Although these methods enable the analysis of spatial relationships across observed sections, they do not reconstruct gene-expression patterns in unobserved re-gions between sections, limiting continuous 3D analysis. Recent methods have addressed gene-expression reconstruction between sparsely observed tissue sections. STAGE [6] aligns the latent space of a gene-expression autoencoder with spatial coor-dinates, enabling prediction at arbitrary locations by subsequently feeding only spatial coordinates into the decoder. SINTER3D [7] uses an INR [11] to predict gene expression at unobserved 3D coordinates. However, learning spatial continuity solely from tran-scriptomic measurements remains challenging when ST sections are sparsely sampled along the depth axis. In contrast, we explore the potential of leveraging more densely sampled histological images as auxiliary supervision to encourage the INR to capture morphological variations between observed ST sections. 

INR for ST Analysis. INR parameterizes continuous signals us-ing neural networks that map spatial coordinates to signal val-ues [11]. Recent studies have applied INR to impute missing gene-expression values and reconstruct expression at unobserved locations in 2D ST [13, 14]. STINR [13] directly regresses gene-expression profiles from spatial coordinates, whereas SUICA [14] addresses high-dimensional expression reconstruction by learn-ing a low-dimensional latent representation predicted using INR. However, these methods primarily focus on reconstruction within individual 2D tissue sections rather than continuous modeling of gene expression in 3D space across aligned sections. 

Histology-based ST Estimation. Predicting gene expression from histological images has emerged as a major research direction in medical image analysis [8, 9]. These studies have demonstrated a strong relationship between tissue morphology and gene expression through image-based prediction. However, their reliance on his-tological images at inference time prevents prediction at locations where images are unavailable, limiting their applicability to our task. 

3. HISTOLOGY-INFORMED MULTI-TASK INR 3.1. Overview 

We propose HIM-INR to reconstruct gene expression at arbitrary 3D coordinates using sparsely observed ST sections and more densely sampled histological images. As illustrated in Fig. 1, HIM-INR con-sists of two reconstruction branches for gene expression and his-tology image features, sharing a SIREN-based INR backbone [11]. Inspired by Zhu et al. [14], we address the difficulty of modeling high-dimensional vectors with INR by learning spatial continuity in low-dimensional latent spaces for both modalities. The predicted la-tent representations are then decoded into gene-expression profiles and histology image features. During training, the model is jointly optimized using reconstruction losses for both modalities. Impor-tantly, the histological-feature reconstruction branch is also trained on sections without ST measurements, encouraging the shared INR backbone to capture tissue structural information that guides gene-expression reconstruction in unobserved regions. We consider sparsely observed ST measurements, denoted by 

SG = {(pi, gi)}i∈ΩG , and more densely sampled histological ob-servations, denoted by SI = {(pi, vi)}i∈ΩI . Here, ΩG and ΩI de-note the index sets of observed ST and histology image features, re-spectively, with ΩG ⊆ ΩI . The spatial coordinate pi ∈ R3 specifies the location of each observation, while gi ∈ RG and vi ∈ RD rep-resent gene-expression profiles and image-derived features, respec-tively, with G genes and D feature dimensions. To encourage the INR to capture tissue morphology rather than low-level pixel infor-mation, we reconstruct features extracted by a pretrained pathology foundation model [12] instead of raw histological images. All serial tissue sections are registered into a common 3D coordinate system prior to training. HIM-INR reconstructs gene-expression profiles and histology image features at arbitrary 3D coordinates:

We jointly learn continuous representations of sparsely observed ST and densely sampled histological images through a shared INR back-bone f . By incorporating image-only sections during training, the shared backbone captures spatial variations in tissue structure even at locations without ST observations, thereby guiding gene-expression reconstruction. 4. EXPERIMENTS Datasets and Experimental Settings. Although histological im-ages are commonly acquired alongside ST measurements [17], large-scale, publicly available 3D ST datasets with paired histolog-ical images remain limited. We therefore select two datasets with relatively large numbers of sections, 3DST-RA [15] and Hemi-Brain [16]. To evaluate our method, we artificially withhold tissue sections to simulate missing observations. 3DST-RA contains paired ST measurements and histological images from serial synovial tissue sections of patients with rheuma-toid arthritis. We select the patient with the largest number of sections, consisting of seven consecutive sections numbered from 1 to 7. ST measurements are retained for Sections 1, 4, and 7. Among the remaining four sections, three are used as image-only observa-tions, while one is completely withheld for evaluation. We repeat this evaluation four times, each time selecting a different section to be completely withheld. The HemiBrain dataset contains serial brain sections from three mice. We use two samples, HemiBrainA and HemiBrainB, contain-ing 42 and 9 sections, respectively. We retain both ST measurements and histological images at every fourth section. Among the three in-termediate sections, the first and third are used as image-only obser-vations, while the second is withheld for evaluation. Prior to training and evaluation, adjacent tissue sections are aligned using XFeat-based feature matching [18] and RANSAC-based similarity transformations [19]. These transformations are se-quentially composed to register all sections to a common coordinate system, with the middle section as the reference. Evaluation-section histological images are also used for registration to ensure accurate spatial correspondence between predicted and ground-truth gene-expression profiles, but are excluded from model training, inference, and performance evaluation. 

Implementation Details and Evaluation Metrics. All experiments used PyTorch [20] with Adam [21]. Histology image features were extracted using the frozen UNI2-h [12]. The best model was selected based on validation performance, using 5% of ST spots randomly held out from each observed section. Hyperparameters were set to a learning rate of 10 −3, up to 1,000 epochs, a batch size of 512, latent dimensions dG = dI = 5 , and a loss weight λ = 1 .To evaluate reconstruction performance while minimizing slice-specific batch effects, we compute the Pearson correlation coef-ficient (PCC) [9] between predicted and ground-truth expression across spatial locations for each gene within each section, then average across genes and sections. Comparisons. We compare our method with six baselines. 1) “NN” and 2) “KNN” interpolate gene expression from the nearest and k = 5 nearest observed spots, respectively. 3) “STAGE” [6] aligns autoencoder latent representations with spatial coordinates, enabling prediction at unobserved locations through its decoder. 4) “3D-STINR” [13] and 5) “3D-SUICA” [14] are INR-based methods originally developed for 2D ST and extended to 3D using spatially aligned coordinates across sections. 3D-STINR directly regresses gene expression from 3D coordinates, whereas 3D-SUICA first learns gene-expression embeddings using slice-wise graph autoen-coders and then trains an INR to predict these embeddings from 3D coordinates, which are subsequently decoded into gene expression. 6) “SINTER3D” [7] predicts gene expression using an INR with additional intermediate-feature reconstruction losses to encourage spatial continuity. Table 1 presents the quantitative comparison. Simple interpola-tion methods, NN and KNN, show poor performance. STAGE per-forms well on HemiBrainA but shows limited performance on other datasets, indicating a lack of robustness across datasets. This may be because explicitly aligning gene-expression latent representations with spatial coordinates restricts their ability to capture complex expression patterns. Similarly, 3D-STINR and SINTER3D show limited performance, potentially due to the difficulty of directly re-gressing high-dimensional expression vectors using INR. In contrast, 3D-SUICA achieves relatively consistent performance, suggesting that low-dimensional latent modeling facilitates INR training. Our method achieves the best performance on all metrics for 3DST-RA and HemiBrainB and on HVGs250 for HemiBrainA, demonstrating the effectiveness of histological supervision for continuous 3D re-construction from sparse ST observations across datasets.

As shown in Table 2, our method achieves relatively consistent and competitive performance across datasets, suggesting that dense histological supervision helps the INR learn spatial conti-nuity more reliably than gene expression alone. To further assess histological supervision, we vary the number of missing ST sections (3, 5, and 7) on HemiBrainA, the largest dataset. As shown in Fig. 2, both methods perform comparably with three missing sections, whereas our method increasingly outperforms Ours w/o HIM as the number of missing sections increases. This suggests that histological super-vision is particularly beneficial when sparse ST observations make learning spatial continuity from gene expression alone challenging. 

Validity of Histology as Multi-task Supervision. To validate the use of densely sampled histological images as auxiliary supervision in multi-task learning, we consider an alternative that first interpo-lates histology image features at unobserved sections and then uses them as direct inputs to predict gene expression. To evaluate this alternative without errors from histological feature interpolation, we train a linear probe on frozen UNI2-h features from ST-observed sections (UNI2-h+LP) and evaluate it using ground-truth histologi-cal images at the evaluation sections. Importantly, these images are unavailable in the actual reconstruction setting and are never pro-vided to our method. As shown in Table 3, our method outperforms the UNI2-h+LP baseline even when ground-truth histological im-ages are provided at the evaluation sections. This suggests that histo-logical supervision can be beneficial for learning continuous spatial representations beyond this simple image-based prediction strategy. 

5. CONCLUSION 

We proposed HIM-INR, a histology-informed multi-task INR for continuous 3D gene-expression reconstruction from sparse ST ob-servations. By jointly learning gene-expression and histological rep-resentations through a shared INR backbone, our method leverages densely sampled histological images as auxiliary supervision to cap-ture spatial continuity between observed ST sections. Experiments on two 3D ST datasets demonstrated improved reconstruction over existing methods, with increasing benefits as ST observations be-come sparser. Importantly, our method enables gene-expression pre-diction at arbitrary 3D coordinates without requiring histological im-ages at inference time. 6. COMPLIANCE WITH ETHICAL STANDARDS
