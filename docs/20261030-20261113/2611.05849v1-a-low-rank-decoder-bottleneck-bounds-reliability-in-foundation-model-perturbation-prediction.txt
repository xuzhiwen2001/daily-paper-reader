## Abstract 

Foundation-model predictors of perturbation response are evaluated primarily by aggregate accuracy. Using their predictions to prioritize experiments requires assessing each prediction’s reliability. We audit the state-transition (ST) decoder used in STATE across three encoders (SE-600M, scGPT, and Tahoe-x1) and four cell lines under CRISPRi. The decoder’s first attention layer has a low-rank output. Projecting this output onto 4–32 fixed directions estimated from training activations preserves mean per-perturbation Pearson delta correlation (PDC). This low-rank at-tention bottleneck bounds the decoder’s reliability by distance to training responses: PDC declines as the cosine distance from a perturbation’s observed response to its nearest training response increases. We propose predicted-NN-distance , the cosine distance from a predicted response to its nearest training response, as a per-prediction confidence score. The score has a mean absolute Spearman corre-lation of 0.81 with PDC, close to the 0.88 obtained with the observed response. Thresholds calibrated on this score transfer across encoders and cell lines, and the score remains predictive of PDC for drug perturbations. 1

## 1 Introduction 

Predicting how cells respond to a genetic or chemical intervention would let biologists choose which perturbations to test, a goal often described as building a virtual cell [10, 40]. Observational single-cell atlases record correlations across millions of cells [38, 45], and CRISPR-based Perturb-seq [17, 33, 39] measures the effect of intervening directly. The perturbation space, however, spans tens of thousands of genes, combinatorial pairs, and diverse cellular contexts, far more than any lab can screen. Predictive models are therefore needed to extrapolate from measured to unmeasured perturbations. Single-cell foundation models [14, 20, 24, 37, 47] pretrain encoders on large single-cell atlases, and models trained on perturbation screens predict responses from the encoders’ representations. STATE [2] pairs a cell-state encoder with a trainable state-transition (ST) decoder: the encoder represents control cells, and the decoder combines these representations with a perturbation embedding to predict post-perturbation gene expression (Figure 1A). Later models pair this decoder with other encoders or use in-context conditioning or generative diffusion instead [12, 19, 25, 44, 51, 56, 57]. Recent benchmarks find that simple linear baselines can match these models on aggregate metrics [3, 13, 54, 55], and Viñas Torné et al. [50] show that a predictor can score well on these metrics by capturing response patterns shared across perturbations. An experiment, however, tests a specific intervention in a specific cellular context, usually one outside the training data. We ask why simple baselines can match these models, and how a user can tell whether a given prediction can be trusted.

We measure accuracy by per-perturbation Pearson delta correlation (PDC; Eq. (1)), the correlation across genes between predicted and observed expression changes relative to control. PDC declines smoothly as the cosine distance from an observed response to its nearest training response increases (Figure 1B). The three encoders (SE-600M [2], scGPT [14], and Tahoe-x1 [20]) use different pretraining recipes, yet the decline has the same shape with each. We therefore audit the component they share, the ST decoder, across four CRISPRi cell lines (§3). Its first attention layer is a low-rank bottleneck: for each perturbation, the layer adds nearly the same vector to every control cell. Interventions on this vector with SE-600M and Tahoe-x1 show that the decoder tells perturbations apart by the vector’s direction. Projecting the layer’s output onto 4–32 directions estimated from training activations preserves PDC, so what the later layers need from this layer lies in a few directions fixed by the training perturbations. A perturbation whose response needs other directions is predicted poorly. A linear read-out of the decoder’s inputs is nearly as accurate as the full decoder, and an oracle that averages the training responses nearest to a perturbation’s observed response is more accurate. The decoder’s accuracy therefore comes from the similarity of a held-out response to training responses, and this is why simple baselines can match it. We propose predicted-NN-distance (Definition 1), the cosine distance from the predicted response to its nearest training response, as a per-prediction confidence score. It needs only the prediction and the training responses, and its correlation with PDC is nearly as high as that of the distance computed from the observed response (Spearman |ρ| ≈ 0.81 against 0.88 ). A threshold on the score fitted on the other encoders and cell lines selects accurate predictions on a held-out one without recalibration. 2Our main contributions are: • We identify a low-rank bottleneck in the ST decoder: its first attention layer passes each perturbation to the later layers through a low-rank output, and its directions lie in a small subspace fixed by the training perturbations (§4.2). • We show that this bottleneck explains why simple baselines match these models: accuracy falls with distance from the nearest training response, and on perturbations far from training the decoders are no more accurate than a linear model on their inputs (§4.1, 4.2). • We propose predicted-NN-distance, a confidence score for each prediction computed from the prediction and the training data. One threshold on this score selects accurate predictions across encoders and cell lines, and the score also ranks predictions for drug perturbations (§4.3, 4.4). 

## 2 Related work 

Perturbation prediction has evolved through several paradigms [16]: linear models [17], VAE-based latent arithmetic [29, 30], optimal transport [9], graph-based approaches with regulatory priors [41], and diffusion in expression space [25]; a parallel line frames perturbation response as causal inference and identifiable-representation learning [18, 28, 58]. Most recently, single-cell foundation models (scFMs) introduce mask-prediction transformers [14, 47] and architectures that couple a cell-state encoder with a state-transition decoder (SE+ST factorizations) [2, 20], with concurrent work pursuing in-context conditioning [19], agentic architecture search [12], ranking-based pretraining [44], and generative diffusion [51, 56]. Yet recent benchmarks find that linear baselines match scFM-decoder performance on standard splits [3, 13, 54, 55], and Viñas Torné et al. [50] show that a predictor can score well on standard metrics by capturing response patterns shared across perturbations. We open the ST decoder hidden state and trace this parity to a low-rank bottleneck inside ST; by pairing the same ST architecture with scGPT, SE, or Tahoe-x1 as the upstream encoder, we test whether the bottleneck is encoder-side or ST-side, finding the latter (§4.2). Mechanistic interpretability provides activation patching [26, 32, 52], subspace interventions [11, 49], and sparse autoencoders [8, 15, 46], applied in biology to protein language models [1, 36, 43] and scFMs for cell-type analysis [42], and analyses of perturbation predictors have inspected attention maps [2, 14]. We open the ST decoder with low-rank projections and activation patching. For deployment, predicted-NN-distance is a per-prediction confidence score, and Learn-then-Test [5], a method from the conformal prediction framework [4], selects a threshold on it with a finite-sample guarantee. Extended related work is provided in Appendix H. 

## 3 Methods 

3.1 Preliminaries Model. We study predictors that couple an upstream encoder with a state-transition (ST) decoder, henceforth encoder +ST (Figure 1A). The encoder embeds each of S control cells. The ST decoder adds a perturbation embedding to every cell embedding and passes the S cells through eight attention blocks L0, . . . , L 7, and a gene decoder maps each cell to its predicted post-perturbation expression. We pair three frozen encoders with different pretraining, SE-600M [2], scGPT [14], and Tahoe-x1 [20], each with an ST decoder of the same architecture and training recipe. This gives three predictors, SE +ST, scGPT +ST, and Tahoe +ST. We denote by Xp ∈ RS×H the output of the self-attention sub-layer of L0 for perturbation p, before it is added to the residual stream. 

Data and protocol. We use the four cell lines of the Replogle–Nadig CRISPRi panel [33, 39] (HepG2, Jurkat, RPE1, and K562) under the STATE few-shot protocol [2]. For each target cell line, the decoder is trained on all perturbations of the other three cell lines and on the training perturbations of the target line, and it is tested on the remaining perturbations of the target line. Three encoders and four target lines give twelve encoder–cell configurations. 3.2 Mechanism audit 

We characterize the ST decoder’s prediction mechanism in three increasingly specific steps. First ,a linear or MLP probe, trained only on training perturbations, asks how much of encoder +ST’s per-prediction quality can be recovered from the decoder’s inputs alone. Second , given high recovery, we locate the prediction inside the decoder by projecting the output of its first attention block onto a few directions, chosen per perturbation or fixed across perturbations. Third , we test whether the leading direction found this way is shared across encoders by moving it between models trained with different encoders.

4.3 A per-prediction confidence score 

The distance in §4.1 is computed from the observed response, so it is unavailable before an experi-ment. We test whether predicted-NN-distance (§3.3), computed from the prediction and the training responses, can take its place when deciding which predictions to accept. 

Ranking predictions. Predicted-NN-distance ranks predictions almost as well as the oracle distance (Spearman |ρ| = 0 .81 against 0.88 , averaged over configurations; Appendix F). Figure 3A shows the mean PDC of the accepted predictions as more of them are accepted in increasing order of distance. For every encoder the curve for predicted-NN-distance nearly coincides with that of the oracle distance, and the tenth of predictions with the smallest distance is about twice as accurate as the average prediction. The score can therefore replace the distance of §4.1 when the observed response is not available. 

Beyond the size of the prediction. Predictions with a large predicted response tend to be more accurate. Among predictions of similar size the score still ranks them by PDC, and it ranks them better than the distance to the ten nearest training responses, the Euclidean distance, and the alignment with the leading axis of the training responses (Appendices F.1 and F.2). 

One threshold for all configurations. A threshold fitted for each configuration needs observed responses from that configuration. We therefore use one threshold, τ ≈ 0.10 , for all configurations (selection rule in Appendix F.3). In every configuration the accepted predictions are far more accurate than the rejected ones (Figure 3B). The share accepted is smallest in Jurkat and K562, which have the fewest held-out perturbations with a close training response. A threshold refitted without one cell line or one encoder gives nearly the same accuracy on the left-out one (Appendix F.3), so a threshold fitted on the other configurations can be used on a left-out cell line or encoder. 4.4 Beyond the audited setting 

We now test the account of §4.1–4.3 when the perturbation type, the resolution of the evaluation, and the split change (Figure 4). 

Drug perturbations. On Tahoe-100M [57] we ran the ST model that Arc Institute released for this dataset, without retraining, on the drug–dose conditions of its five test cell lines. The score correlates with PDC in all five (Figure 4A), also when references to the same drug are removed from the training pool (Appendix G.2). The model has seen most of these drugs in other cell lines, so this tests distance to training within a cell line rather than drugs the model has never seen. 

Single-cell PDC. PDC computed against individual cells instead of the pseudobulk also falls with predicted-NN-distance for all three encoders (Figure 4B). For Tahoe +ST part of this relationship reflects the size of the response, since dividing each perturbation’s single-cell PDC by the highest value its cells allow weakens the correlation (Appendix G.3). 

Gene families held out. To test whether accuracy depends on distance or on the presence of the same gene family in training, we removed each of eight gene families from training and validation in all four cell lines, retrained the decoder, and scored only the held-out family. The score stays negatively correlated with PDC in nine of the twelve configurations (Figure 4C). In the three RPE1 configurations the correlation is positive, and its interval includes zero (Appendix G.4). When the nearest training perturbations come from other families, distance ranks predictions less reliably than on random held-out perturbations. 

## 5 Discussion 

In this work we found that the encoder +ST predictors are reliable for a perturbation when the perturbation’s response is close to a response seen in training. We traced this dependence to the decoder’s first attention layer, where each perturbation is written as one direction in a small subspace estimated from training perturbations. These results suggest why perturbation models built on foundation-model encoders often do no better than linear or mean baselines [3, 13, 50, 55]. Projecting the first-layer output onto a few directions leaves PDC unchanged, and ridge regression on the same embeddings scores about the same as the full decoder (Table 1, Figure 2C). Both predict well when a held-out response resembles a training response and poorly otherwise, so a benchmark score depends largely on how many test perturbations lie close to training. Replacing a perturbation’s first-layer direction with another perturbation’s direction makes the decoder predict the second perturbation’s response (Appendix C.2). Similar interventions have shown that single directions mediate behaviors in language models [7], and causal abstraction gives a general 9framework for such tests [23]. Running them on other perturbation decoders is a cheap first test of whether they share this structure. For users of the current models, predicted-NN-distance needs only the prediction and the training responses, so it can be computed before an experiment. A new cell line or encoder rarely comes with the measurements needed to calibrate a threshold. In our data, a threshold chosen without a cell line or an encoder worked on each of the four cell lines and three encoders we held out (Appendix F.3). When a guarantee is needed, Learn-then-Test provides one from measurements in the same setting. 

Limitations and future work. We audited one decoder architecture with three encoders, on CRISPRi screens in four cell lines and on drug perturbations in Tahoe-100M. The bottleneck is clearest for SE +ST; in Tahoe +ST part of the perturbation signal bypasses the first attention layer (Figure 2A; Appendix C.5). The score is weaker evidence of accuracy when a whole gene family is absent from training, because the nearest training responses then come from other families (§4.4), and the Learn-then-Test guarantee fails when deployment perturbations lie farther from training than the calibration perturbations (Appendix F.4). We intervened on attention outputs but not on the MLPs of later layers, and we have not established what each perturbation’s direction encodes in biological terms. Predicted-NN-distance also correlates with PDC for TxPert, a GNN-based predictor (Appendix G.1), and auditing such predictors would show whether their dependence on distance has the same cause. Predicted-NN-distance could also guide which perturbations to test next. Perturbations with a large distance are those whose predictions the training data support least, so they are natural candidates for the next experiment. How to choose such experiments is studied in active learning and experimental design for genetic perturbations [6, 31, 48, 59]. 

Broader impacts. Perturbation predictors are used to choose which experiments to run. Predicted-NN-distance is computed from the prediction and the training data, so researchers can tell before an experiment which predictions are likely to hold and spend experiments accordingly. A risk is that accepted predictions are treated as measured responses, although some of them are wrong.

Cell lines and split. We use the four Replogle–Nadig cell lines HepG2, Jurkat, RPE1, and K562 [33, 39] with the few-shot train, validation, and test perturbation lists of Adduri et al. [2]. The test sets have 945 (HepG2), 1,084 (Jurkat), 1,061 (RPE1), and 968 (K562) perturbations. Test perturbations are never used to train probes or to estimate the fixed basis. The replacement experiments of §A.5 act on test perturbations and therefore use their own L0 directions. PDC is computed on the 2,000 highly variable genes of the published selection, as in Adduri et al. [2]. 

Models and inference. Each predictor is a frozen encoder followed by an ST decoder. The encoder computes an embedding for every cell once, and the decoder is trained on these cached embeddings with a one-hot perturbation encoding, so the encoder receives no gradient from the perturbation task. The decoder is a Llama-style transformer with bidirectional attention and a batch encoder (Table 2). SE +ST uses the published few-shot checkpoints of Adduri et al. [2]. For scGPT +ST and Tahoe +ST we trained the decoder with the same configuration, changing only the input embedding, and trained Tahoe +ST on HepG2 with two further seeds. Unless stated otherwise, the decoder receives 64 control cells from the same batch (gem group) as context for each perturbation and returns 64 predicted cells, which we average into a pseudobulk prediction. Comparisons between two predictors are paired on the test perturbations they share.

A.4 Projection and ablation of the L0 output Keeping the top directions. During the forward pass we replace each perturbation’s L0 attention output Xp with its truncation to the top k singular directions (Eq. (3)), for k ∈ { 1, 4, 8, 16 , 32 },rescale each row to its original norm, and score PDC (the rank-1 rows of Table 1). The random control keeps k random orthonormal directions instead, with the same rescaling, drawn either from the whole space (SE +ST and scGPT +ST in Figure 2C) or from the subspace orthogonal to the top 32 singular directions of Xp (Tahoe +ST and the layer scan). The layer scan applies the same top-1 truncation and the same random control to the attention output of one layer at a time, L0 to L7

(Figure 2A, Appendix C.1). The ablation sets the L0 attention output to zero (Appendix C.1). 

Fixed training basis and sufficient rank. The fixed basis is estimated from the L0 outputs of the target line’s training perturbations only (§3.2). For each k we compare its PDC with that of the unprojected decoder, perturbation by perturbation, and compute a paired bootstrap interval. The fixed basis preserves PDC at rank k if this interval lies within ±0.02 (the margin of Appendix A.7), and it outperforms a random orthonormal basis of the same rank if the interval of their difference lies above 0.02 . The sufficient rank is the smallest k at which the fixed basis meets both conditions and the per-perturbation truncation also preserves PDC.

A.7 Statistical analysis 

A comparison between two conditions is the mean per-perturbation difference in PDC on the shared perturbations, with a paired 95% percentile bootstrap interval over perturbations ( 5,000 resamples). As a check we also resample whole gene families (the nine groups of Appendix D), which roughly doubles the interval widths (Appendices B.1 and C.5). Rerunning the decoder without any interven-tion, with different sampled control cells or with a checkpoint from a different training step, changes mean PDC by up to about 0.02 . We therefore do not interpret differences of this size, and use 0.02 

PDC as the margin in the sufficient rank (Appendix A.4) and in the per-configuration threshold (Appendix A.6).

C.4 Retrieval from the training responses 

Section 4.2 compares the decoder with retrieval from the training responses. The retrieval oracle never runs the decoder. For a held-out perturbation it finds the training perturbations whose observed responses are closest, in cosine distance over the 2,000 HVGs, to the perturbation’s own observed response, and predicts a weighted average of the five nearest, with weights wi ∝ exp( −di/τ ) and

Each retrieved response is added to the control cell the decoder used for that row, passed through the decoder’s non-negativity floor, and averaged into a pseudobulk, so the oracle and the decoder are scored the same way. A ridge on the control and batch inputs alone reaches PDC 0.27 –0.46 , the same to three decimals for the three encoders, so what the probes know about the perturbation comes from the decoder’s perturbation embedding. For SE +ST and scGPT +ST the probes are about as accurate as the decoder. The ridge probe reaches mean PDC 0.377 and 0.367 , against 0.374 and 0.337 , and the two-layer MLP recovers 94 –108% and

PDC is a correlation over genes, so a prediction that reproduces the shared response of Appendix D scores well on every perturbation whose response lies along it, without telling perturbations apart. We add two scores that measure this. For each held-out perturbation we take the cells’ responses (each cell minus the mean of the observed control cells, over the 2,000 HVGs) and their top right singular vector, the gene-space direction of the response, for the predicted and for the observed cells. 

Direction recovery is the absolute cosine between the predicted and observed directions, averaged over perturbations. Identity retrieval asks whether the predicted directions keep which perturbations resemble each other: for each held-out perturbation we take the other held-out perturbation whose predicted direction is closest to its predicted direction, and the one whose observed direction is closest to its observed direction, and report the fraction of perturbations for which the two are the same. Tahoe +ST recovers the direction best in every cell line, by 0.026 –0.068 over SE +ST, and reaches 

0.874 on RPE1. Identity retrieval is low for all three decoders: 0.034 –0.054 for SE +ST, 0.050 –0.074 

for Tahoe +ST, and 0.013 –0.046 for scGPT +ST. Chance level is 1/(N − 1) , about 0.001 . An oracle that predicts the response of the training perturbation whose observed response is closest to the perturbation’s own gives 0.201 , 0.109 , and 0.123 on HepG2, Jurkat, and RPE1. The decoders recover the direction of each response well but keep little of which perturbations resemble each other, in line with Appendices C.2 and D.

F.2 Beyond the size of the prediction 

Predictions with a large predicted response lie closer to a training response: the Spearman ρ between predicted-NN-distance and the norm of the predicted response is −0.72 to −0.89 in 11 of the 12 

configurations ( +0 .27 for scGPT +ST on K562). We therefore split each configuration’s held-out perturbations into five equal-count bins of predicted norm and compute, within each bin, the Spearman 

ρ between predicted-NN-distance and PDC (Tables 16 and 17, Figure 10). Within bins the median |ρ| over configurations is 0.40 in the bin of smallest predictions and 0.73 in the bin of largest. In 10 of the 12 configurations |ρ| is at least 0.2 in three or more bins; the exceptions are Tahoe +ST and scGPT +ST on RPE1, and finer bins ( 7 or 10 ) give the same pattern. Within the same bins predicted-NN-distance is more strongly associated with PDC than the remaining variation in predicted norm in 52 of the 60 bins (paired Wilcoxon over configurations, p = 0 .0012 ). Regressing the predicted norm out of both the score and PDC leaves a median partial |ρ| of 0.58 , against 0.84 

without it. The loss from binning is largest where the observed responses lie closest to one direction (Table 18, Figure 11). In RPE1, with participation ratio 2.6, the score keeps 39% of its unbinned |ρ|, against 

66 –77% in the other cell lines. The oracle distance keeps a partial |ρ| of 0.81 (median) and 0.86 

for Tahoe +ST on RPE1. The observed responses in RPE1 therefore still carry this information; the predictions there collapse toward the leading direction and lose it. 

F.3 Pooled threshold Selection and leave-one-out refits. The pooled threshold is chosen on a grid of 50 values of τ

between 0.048 and 0.82 . For each value we compute, in each of the 12 configurations, the mean PDC of the accepted predictions above that cell line’s rank-1 SVD baseline, and we keep the value with the largest mean over configurations among those at which every configuration accepts at least five predictions. This gives τ = 0 .0957 . Over 5,000 bootstrap resamples that draw configurations and, within each, perturbations with replacement, with the grid rebuilt in each resample, the selected τ has mean 0.099 and 95% interval [0 .073 , 0.116] . ## H Extended related work 

Perturbation prediction before foundation models. Latent-arithmetic methods such as scGen [29] treat a perturbation as a vector added in a learned VAE latent space. Graph models such as GEARS [41] use gene regulatory networks and the Gene Ontology; they work well when the prior annotation covers a perturbation and less well otherwise. Optimal-transport methods, including CellOT [9] and CINEMA-OT [18], learn maps between unpaired control and perturbed cells, and CINEMA-OT separates confounding variation from the effect of the intervention. Causal repre-sentation learning treats perturbations as sparse mechanism shifts [28], building on identifiability results [58]. Dimitrov et al. [16] review the field. The encoder +ST factorization we study is more recent than these families. 

Single-cell foundation models and the encoder–decoder split. Geneformer trains a transformer on about 30 M cells with masked rank prediction [47], and scGPT uses a generative masked objective with task-specific fine-tuning [14]; both predict perturbations with the same network that encodes the cell. STATE [2] separates the two: a cell-state encoder (SE-600M) feeds a trainable state-transition decoder (ST). Tahoe-x1 [20] is another cell-state encoder, trained on data that include the Tahoe-100M chemical-perturbation atlas [57], and Stack [19] uses in-context conditioning instead of a separate decoder. We pair the same ST architecture with SE-600M, scGPT, or Tahoe-x1, which separates what the decoder does from what each encoder’s pretraining contributes. 

Concurrent work on perturbation prediction. VCHarness runs an agentic architecture search over a library of scFM components [12] on the same four Replogle–Nadig cell lines. Markov-GPL replaces the count-based pretraining objective with a Plackett–Luce ranking likelihood [44]. X-Cell is a masked discrete-diffusion transformer with multi-modal cross-attention [51]; its weights were not available in a form we could probe. PerturbDiff [56] treats the perturbed population as a random variable in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space and runs diffusion over kernel mean embeddings, and Squidiff [25] is a conditional diffusion model in expression space whose semantic latents add for combined perturbations. These works change the architecture, objective, or generative model; we study the internal computation of one existing architecture. 

Mechanistic interpretability. Activation patching replaces the value of a hidden component with its value from another run and measures the effect downstream; Meng et al. [32] used it for factual recall in GPT-2, and Heimersheim and Nanda [26] discuss its use as a method. Causal tracing of circuits was extended to in-context behavior by Olsson et al. [35], Wang et al. [52]. Interventions on low-rank subspaces rely on the linear-feature view behind steering vectors [11, 49] and sparse autoencoder dictionaries [8, 15, 21, 46], and sparse autoencoders have been applied to protein language models [1, 36, 43]. Our interventions are of these kinds, with the L0 attention output as the site; replacing the top direction with that of another encoder or another cell line is the one addition. Causal abstraction [23] formalizes the correspondence between a high-level causal model and the network that implements it. 

Perturbation biology and shared responses. Genome-scale CRISPRi Perturb-seq [39] and its extension to more cell lines [33] provide our benchmark; combinatorial CRISPRa screens [34] raise the question of how perturbations compose, which we do not address. The mean responses in these screens are dominated by a few co-regulated programs, and Viñas Torné et al. [50] describe the shift shared by perturbed cells as systematic variation. We locate this structure in the decoder ( L0,39 Appendix C), relate the leading direction to gene programs (Appendix D), and show that the direction is specific to the encoder and the cell line. Benchmarks report that simple linear baselines match scFM predictors [3, 13, 54], and Ahlmann-Eltze et al. [3] show that PDC rewards predictions that collapse toward the mean on small-effect perturbations, which Appendix E addresses with scores that separate perturbations.
