# Abstract 

Experiments involving the perturbation of individual cells are central to understanding cellular mechanisms and can accelerate drug discovery. Causal representation learning allows us to uncover the latent factors that regulate biological systems and predict the impact of novel perturbations. Unfortunately, existing methods fail to address intervention spillover in a closed-world setting where intervention targets are known apriori , such as in Perturb-seq experiments, due to their reliance on dense encoders. # Author summary 

Understanding how genes work together can help explain changes in cell behavior and guide the search for treatments. Yet testing every possible combination of genetic changes is impractical. We developed a computer model to predict how individual cells respond to changes that have not been tested experimentally. We address two problems: existing models can obscure how a change leads to a predicted outcome and overlook differences between individual cells. In our model, each genetic change enters through a single starting point, and its effects can spread through a network learned from the data. This allows us to examine how a predicted response develops. We also match cells from separate groups with and without genetic changes, helping our model retain the diversity of cellular responses. In tests on two datasets, we improved predictions of responses to genetic perturbations. On the Norman et al. dataset, we also improved predictions of responses to combined genetic changes whose effects could not be explained by adding the effects of the individual changes. We also used the model to suggest genetic changes that could explain an observed cellular response. Our approach could help researchers choose informative experiments and investigate how genes jointly shape cell behavior. November 3, 2026 2/26

# Introduction 1

Understanding how cells respond to genetic perturbations, such as those induced by CRISPR screens [1], is 2

fundamental to advancing our understanding of basic biology and therapeutic discovery [2–4]. However, the 3

combinatorial explosion across ∼20,000 protein-coding genes makes exhaustive perturbation experiments 4

infeasible [4], motivating computational models that predict cellular responses to unseen perturbations or 5

combinations thereof. 6

Existing approaches to predict the transcriptional outcome following a perturbation face a fundamental 7

challenge: generative quality comes at the cost of causal interpretability. Models such as GEARS [5] and 8

scGPT [6] have demonstrated the ability to predict transcriptional responses to genetic perturbations, 9

including previously unseen perturbations, although their predictive performance varies across datasets and 10 

evaluation metrics; however, they act as “black boxes” providing little mechanistic insight into how 11 

perturbations affect cellular systems. This lack of interpretability prevents researchers from understanding 12 

why certain combinations of perturbations produce synergistic effects, failing to provide testable biological 13 

hypotheses. 14 

As such, computational biology demands models that provide mechanistic insights into cellular 15 

function [7], making causal representation learning (CRL) [8] a promising approach for mapping genes to 16 

biological processes. In particular, recent CRL approaches such as DiscrepancyVAE (dVAE) [9] or SENA [10] 17 

aim at recovering the underlying causal generative factors and their interactions, enabling counterfactual 18 

reasoning. However, these CRL approaches suffer from fundamental architectural limitations that prevent 19 

them from fulfilling their theoretical causal identifiability guarantees: the use of a dense interventional 20 

encoder inherently means that every gene, including those intervened upon, can influence all latent variables. 21 

We term this phenomenon intervention spillover , as it renders the task of identifying a unique latent target 22 

for an intervention ill-posed. Consequently, any mechanism designed to apply the intervention is forced to 23 

converge to a suboptimal solution, because it cannot perform the clean, single-node manipulation required 24 

for valid causal inference [11, 12]. 25 

Further, the destructive nature of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) makes it impossible to observe 26 

the same cell before and after a genetic intervention, complicating the prediction of cell-specific responses. 27 

Existing methods address this issue by randomly pairing control and perturbed cells. Since the model must 28 

minimize error across all possible arbitrary pairings, it is incentivized to learn the population-mean response. 29 

We refer to this phenomenon as mean collapse , as this regression to the mean erodes cellular diversity. 30

# Background 45 

## In silico high-throughput screening models 46 

Accurate in silico prediction of perturbation effects remains critical to guide experimental perturbation 47 

assays, as it would allow us to predict, for instance, how cells respond to environmental stress [14]. However, 48 

a key limitation in learning perturbation responses lies in the destructive nature of single-cell transcriptomic 49 

assays, which prevents measuring the same cell before and after a perturbation. While paired measurements 50 

are infeasible, we do have access to independent single-cell observations from control and perturbed cells. 51 

Recent works have demonstrated the potential of representation learning in leveraging these perturbation 52 

data to predict transcriptional outcomes of novel combinations of perturbations. For example, Kamimoto et 53 

al. [15] relied on inferring (linear) transcriptional relationships between genes in the form of a gene regulatory 54 

network to infer the effect of unseen perturbations. Shortly after, the Compositional Perturbation 55 

Autoencoder (CPA) [16] predicted combinations of perturbations by vector arithmetic in the latent space 56 

generated by a Variational Autoencoder (VAE). In parallel, GEARS [5] leveraged Graph Neural Networks 57 

(GNNs) to incorporate prior knowledge of gene–gene relationships into the model architecture, thereby 58 

predicting unseen genetic interventions and their combinations. More recently, CellOT [17] proposed to 59 

directly learn optimal transport maps between control and perturbed cell states, thus accounting for 60 

heterogeneous subpopulation structures in the data. Additional efforts, such as scGen [18] and scGPT [6], 61

empirical validation. The directed acyclic structure (Requirement 1) and the atomic, single-target 122

architectural constraint rather than an experimentally verified property. 132

## The intervention spillover problem 133 

While identifiability theory provides a strong theoretical foundation, a key practical challenge arises from the 134 

architectural choices in most CRL models. We term this the intervention spillover problem : the phenomenon 135 

where a targeted, single-variable intervention in the high-dimensional observation space ( x) is transformed 136 

into a dense, multi-node signal in the lower-dimensional latent space ( u). 137 

This issue stems from a direct conflict with the atomic intervention requirement discussed above. An 138 

Ideal Causal Encoder , f ∗, must satisfy this requirement by ensuring that a targeted intervention on a single 139 

gene isolates its distributional effect to the parameters of a corresponding single latent variable. 140 

Consequently, the expected change in the latent space would be perfectly sparse, manifesting as a clean, 141 

single-node perturbation rather than a dense signal. 142 

Intervention spillover problem in current models . However, current interventional encoder 143 

networks fθ are typically dense architectures like MLPs. These encoders possess a dense Jacobian matrix, 144 

meaning each component of the latent vector is a function of all input components. Consequently, a sparse 145 

change in the input x propagates through the network, and the model is thus forced to choose the ‘best’ 146 

single target from a dense signal that suggests multiple viable targets. 147 

This architectural mismatch has several critical implications for the reliability of learned intervention 148 

targets. First, models that use a softmax function for target identification are forced into a “winner-take-all” 149 

decision on a dense signal, creating a risk of misattributing the primary causal effect. Furthermore, while the 150 

perturbed gene is known a priori , its corresponding target in the latent space is not, making the task of 151 

reliably discovering it from an entangled signal intractable. This reframes the primary modeling objective: 152 

the model should be designed not to explore for a latent target, but to accurately learn the magnitude of the 153 

intervention’s effect on the causal representation. 154 

Spillover problem in genetic perturbation prediction . This phenomenon is particularly concerning 155 

in genetic perturbation experiments, as interventions are applied to individual genes in the high-dimensional 156 

expression space, yet their effects propagate through the cell’s regulatory network and manifest as 157 

coordinated changes across multiple (latent) biological processes. It is thus crucial to distinguish this 158 

intervention spillover, an artifact of dense encoders, from true biological correlation. High correlations 159 

between genes do not violate the single-node intervention requirement. Rather, dense encoders fail by 160 

transforming a sparse perturbation into a dense entry point in the latent space. By enforcing a sparse 161 

mapping for the intervention target, our proposed architecture satisfies identifiability while still modeling 162 

complex gene–gene correlations downstream via the dense components of the encoder and the learned causal 163 

graph. 164

## The dilemma of prior knowledge in causal discovery 165 

Existing causal discovery models can be positioned on a spectrum from purely exploratory approaches like 166 

dVAE, which learns all components from data, to more confirmatory models like SENA, which leverages 167 

predefined biological pathways to enhance interpretability. While this confirmatory strategy aims to ground 168 

the model in known biology, it introduces a significant limitation stemming from poor knowledge transfer. 169 

Biological pathways are known to exhibit strong cell-type and cell-line specificity [22–25], meaning that 170 

mechanisms characterized in one cellular context often do not generalize to another. 171 

# Results 172 

To first establish the foundational accuracy of RAPTORGraph, we benchmarked its generative performance 173 

against four state-of-the-art models: SENA, scGPT, dVAE, and GEARS. We assessed two key aspects of 174 

prediction fidelity primarily using the single-cell perturbation dataset from Norman et al. [4]. We prioritized 175 

this dataset for the comprehensive comparison because the implementations of several baseline models have 176 

auxiliary files and preprocessing steps that are tightly coupled to it, making adaptation to other datasets 177 

non-trivial. However, to further demonstrate generalizability, we provide an additional evaluation on the 178 

Replogle2020 dataset, restricted to a comparison between RAPTORGraph and scGPT. 179 

To validate our hyperparameter selection, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study on the β-VAE 180 

regularization parameter ( β) and the DAGMA loss weights ( μ, λ 1). Our analysis reveals a critical trade-off 181 

between distribution matching and the preservation of biological heterogeneity. While higher regularization 182 

(β ≥ 0.8) achieves competitive Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) scores, it induces severe posterior 183 

collapse, with the variance of predicted control cell states dropping by an order of magnitude compared to 184 

the optimal setting (10 −4 vs 10 −3). Conversely, lower regularization ( β = 0 .1) preserves higher variance but 185 

results in unstable training and significantly higher MMD. We identified β ≈ 0.4 as the optimal operating 186 

point, minimizing MMD while maintaining a latent KL divergence ( ≈ 10 −3) consistent with established 187 

baselines (dVAE and SENA), thereby ensuring the model captures meaningful cellular diversity rather than 188 

regressing to the population mean. 189 

Reconstruction capabilities. First, we measured the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the ground 190 

truth and predicted profiles of control cells to evaluate how well each model reconstructs the basal, 191 

unperturbed cellular state. Second, we calculated the MMD between the distributions of ground truth and 192 

predicted profiles for double-gene perturbations to assess the model’s ability to capture the global 193 

transcriptional shift following a complex intervention. 194

Reverse perturbation analysis. While predicting genetic interactions demonstrates a model’s forward 210 

predictive power, a more profound test is its ability to reverse-engineer the genetic cause of an observed 211 

phenotype. To this end, we challenged a model to identify the specific genetic perturbation responsible for a 212 

given cellular gene expression profile. The analysis was performed on a controlled combinatorial space of 20 213 

transcription factors from the Norman et al. [4] dataset. For each model, we generated a database of 214 

predicted expression profiles for all possible double-gene perturbations within this subset. We then used the 215 

ground truth mean expression profiles from experimentally observed double perturbations as queries, ranking 216 

the database entries by similarity to identify the most likely causal perturbation. 217 

We first evaluated the models on the highly stringent task of identifying the exact causal gene 218 

combination, quantified by the Correct (Exact Match) Hit Rate @ K (S1 Fig). As shown there, all models 219 

achieve modest hit rates, underscoring the profound difficulty of this reverse-engineering challenge. However, 220 

a more practical measure of a model’s utility is its ability to identify biologically influential genes. We 221 

assessed this using the Relevant (Gene Overlap) Hit Rate @ K, which credits a model for retrieving 222 

perturbations containing at least one of the correct genes (Figure 2 and Table 4). RAPTORGraph 223 

demonstrates competitive performance, consistently ranking among the top models. This highlights that 224 

while pinpointing exact higher-order interactions is an ongoing challenge, RAPTORGraph is highly effective 225 

at identifying the key genetic players driving a cellular response, making it a valuable tool for hypothesis 226

intervention spillover, where dense encoders create entangled latent signals that confound causal discovery, 257

# Conclusion 276 

Developing interpretable in silico models is crucial for therapeutic discovery, yet current approaches face two 277 

key challenges: intervention spillover, where dense encoders create entangled latent signals that confound 278 

causal discovery, and mean collapse, where the absence of one-to-one cell pairings erodes cellular diversity. 279 

We introduce RAPTORGraph, a framework that directly resolves these issues. To counteract intervention 280 

spillover, it combines a preconditioned GraphPathway layer, enforcing the sparse mappings required for clean 281 

single-node interventions, with a DAGMA layer for robust DAG discovery. This architectural design 282 

eliminates the artifactual entanglement of input genes with latent pathways, while the subsequent DAG 283 

module explicitly learns the legitimate biological causal relationships and correlations between the resulting 284 

sparse pathway activities. To prevent mean collapse, RAPTORGraph globally pairs control and perturbed 285 

populations through OT. RAPTORGraph not only achieves a strong balance between reconstruction fidelity 286 

and distributional accuracy relative to state-of-the-art methods, but also shows the ability to predict 287 

complex, non-additive genetic interactions, and can reverse-engineer genetic drivers to generate testable 288 

hypotheses. RAPTORGraph bridges predictive power and mechanistic insight, enabling not only prediction 289

## Deep causal graph learning with DAGMA 329 

The GraphPathway Encoder with preconditioning fundamentally alters the causal discovery task. By fixing 330 

the mapping from known interventions to specific latent variables, it eliminates the need for 331 

permutation-based discovery methods that assume a dense, entangled encoder. However, this fixed ordering 332 

makes simple acyclicity constraints, such as enforcing an upper-triangular adjacency matrix, unsuitable. 333 

These methods impose a strict and arbitrary causal hierarchy, as the influence of one latent variable on 334 

another is entirely restricted by their ordering (e.g., latent i can only influence latent j if i < j ), which would 335 

conflict with the true biological relationships we aim to discover. For this, we adapt the DAGMA 336 

framework [31] for use in a deep learning context. 337 

DAGMA reformulates the combinatorial search for a DAG into a continuous optimization problem, 338 

following the continuous acyclicity-constrained paradigm of NOTEARS [32]. This is achieved by minimizing 339 

a score function that measures data fit, subject to a differentiable constraint that is satisfied only if the 340 

graph is acyclic. The DAGMA acyclicity constraint, hs(A), is based on a log-determinant function: 341 

hs(A) = − log det( sI − A ◦ A) + d log s = 0 (5) where A ∈ Rd×d is the weighted adjacency matrix of the latent graph, s > 0 is a scaling scalar, and the 342 

condition holds if and only if A represents a DAG. The optimization then solves a sequence of unconstrained 343

DAGMA implementation. We implement a modified DAGMA approach designed to learn the 345

path-following schedule where the score weight μ is gradually decreased (see Eq. (7)), allowing the model to 348

gradient flow by computing LDAGMA on detached latent representations. This ensures the acyclicity 350 

constraint optimizes the graph structure without distorting the encoder’s feature extraction. 351 

## Training objective 352 

The model is trained end-to-end by minimizing a composite objective function, Ltotal , that integrates four 353 

distinct components, each tailored to a specific aspect of the learning problem. The overall objective is a 354 

weighted sum of these individual losses: 355 

Ltotal = αrec LMSE + βKL LKL + γMMD LMMD + δDAGMA LDAGMA (6) where αrec , β KL , γ MMD , and δDAGMA are scalar hyperparameters that balance the contribution of each term. 356 

Reconstruction Loss ( LMSE ). For observational data, the model is trained to faithfully reconstruct the 357 

input gene expression profiles. This is measured by the MSE between the decoder’s output and the original 358 

unperturbed input, ensuring the model learns a high-fidelity representation of the basal cellular state. 359 

KL Divergence ( LKL ). As a standard component of a VAE, a Kullback-Leibler divergence term is 360 

included to regularize the latent space. It encourages the learned posterior distribution over the latent 361 

variables, q(z|x), to be close to a prior distribution, p(z), which is typically a standard normal distribution. 362 

This promotes a smooth and well-structured latent space. 363 

Interventional Prediction Loss ( LMMD ). For interventional data, the model’s ability to predict 364 

out-of-distribution outcomes is evaluated using the MMD [33]. This loss measures the distributional distance 365 

between the model-predicted cell states following a perturbation and the ground-truth distribution of 366 

experimentally observed cells for that same perturbation. 367 

Causal Graph Loss ( LDAGMA ). The DAGMA loss is computed on the latent representations of 368 

observational data to learn the invariant causal graph structure between the learned meta-pathways. This 369 

objective integrates three key components: a data-fit score, an L1 penalty to encourage sparsity, and the 370

## Optimal transport for deterministic interventional learning 374 

A fundamental challenge in learning from single-cell perturbation data is the absence of a direct one-to-one 375 

mapping between control and perturbed cell populations, as it is impossible to observe the same cell before 376 

and after the intervention. This makes learning the treatment effect a fundamental counterfactual problem, 377 

where the central challenge is to identify a general transformation that maps the control distribution to the 378 

perturbed distribution in the absence of paired examples. To address this counterfactual gap, many models 379 

create training pairs by randomly matching control and interventional samples. While computationally 380 

efficient, this random pairing is a primary cause of what we term mean collapse : a process where the model 381 

learns the average response, collapsing the distribution towards its mean and eroding cellular diversity 382 

(Fig. 5). 383

employ a Wasserstein-based OT [34, 35] framework to globally 385 

pair control cells with perturbed cells under unique conditions 386 

(Fig. 5b). Although several recent methods address unpaired 387 

single-cell perturbation data [17, 36], we adopt OT as an 388 

offline pairing step for simplicity while avoiding mean collapse. 389
