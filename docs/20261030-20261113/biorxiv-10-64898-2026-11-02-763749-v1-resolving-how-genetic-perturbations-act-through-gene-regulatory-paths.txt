Resolving how genetic perturbations act through gene regulatory paths 

Lixin Ren 1, Zhana Duren 1* 1 Center for Computational Biology and Bioinformatics, Department of Medical and Molecular Genetics, Indiana University School of Medicine, Indianapolis, IN 46202, USA 

Abstract 

Perturb-seq measures transcriptional responses to thousands of genetic perturbations, but each gene’s response mixes the contributions of many regulatory paths, so how a perturbation acts cannot be resolved from expression alone, however large the model. Here we present REGA-perturb, which supplies this missing information rather than adding model complexity: reference epigenomes define a scaffold of possible transcription factor (TF)–regulatory element–gene paths, and perturbation responses, pooled through shared latent programs, determine how each path is used, requiring only RNA measurements. Across three CRISPR interference screens totaling 2.4 million cells, REGA-perturb inferred TF–target relationships that agreed with ChIP-seq binding with a gain over random more than four times that of the best existing method, separated candidate direct from indirect targets, and predicted targets of TFs withheld from training. It also inferred perturbation-induced changes in TF activity not reflected in TF transcript levels, and resolved perturbation-specific regulatory rewiring in which opposing regulatory changes cancel at the expression level. 

Introduction 

Genetic perturbations provide a direct means of investigating gene function and the organization of regulatory relationships. Perturb-seq couples pooled CRISPR perturbations with single-cell RNA sequencing, enabling the transcriptional consequences of thousands of perturbations to be measured in a single experiment 1–3 , and genome-scale screens comprising millions of cells are now available as public resources 3. The goal of such screens is to understand how perturbations act. However, the expression change of each gene is the net outcome of multiple regulatory inputs, combining direct effects with secondary effects mediated by other regulators 4,5 . The response therefore reflects regulatory mechanism but mixes the contributions of many regulatory paths, so the response alone cannot reveal which paths produced it. Even for the most direct perturbation, knockdown of a transcription factor (TF), genes bound by the TF often show little or no response 5–7 , and the magnitude of expression changes is largely unrelated to where the TF binds, as we confirm across hundreds of TF perturbations in this study. Because many different combinations of regulatory paths can produce the same expression response, this is a limitation of the information in the data rather than of model capacity, and it cannot be overcome simply by building larger models. Yet computational analysis of Perturb-seq data has largely measured progress by how accurately models predict perturbation responses, including responses to unseen perturbations,

Recent benchmarks have further found that increasingly complex prediction models often do not outperform simple linear baselines 13 .As a result, neither expression changes nor predictive models reveal which TFs regulate which genes, or through which regulatory elements (REs), and without such a map several questions central to interpreting a perturbation screen cannot be answered. First, when a regulator gene is perturbed, which of the responding genes does it regulate directly, and which respond only secondarily through other regulators? Second, how does a perturbation alter the activity of TFs? Most genes perturbed in a screen are not TFs, yet they can still change TF activity, for example by disrupting cofactors, chromatin regulators or signaling pathways that control TF function, or by triggering stress responses that activate specific TFs 14,15 . Because such changes often act at the protein level, TF activity need not be reflected in TF transcript levels 16 . Third, can the regulatory inputs to a gene change without changing its expression? If a perturbation strengthens some inputs to a gene while weakening others, their effects may cancel, leaving expression stable even though the gene’s regulation has been substantially reorganized. Such compensation is a recognized feature of regulatory systems, arising for example from feedback loops and from redundant regulators that offset the loss of one another 17,18 . Yet because it leaves no trace in expression, it is invisible to differential expression analysis, which would classify these genes as unaffected. A regulatory map that answers these questions would also be most useful if it reached beyond the perturbations actually performed. Most TFs expressed in a cell type are never perturbed in a given screen, and a map that captures shared regulatory structure, rather than the idiosyncrasies of individual perturbations, should anticipate their effects as well. Existing approaches that aim to model regulatory mechanism address this problem only in part. Latent program analyses identify coordinated transcriptional responses to perturbations 19 but do not link these programs to the TFs and REs that produce them. Gene regulatory network (GRN) inference methods, including SCENIC, CellOracle and GRNBoost2 20–22 , do model regulatory relationships, but they infer them largely from covariation in expression, in which the contributions of different regulatory paths remain confounded. They show limited agreement with TF binding in benchmarking studies 23,24 and yield static networks that do not describe how individual relationships change across perturbations. Most also do not exploit perturbations as interventions on a shared regulatory structure. Methods that additionally use single-cell chromatin accessibility, such as LINGER and SCENIC+ 25,26 , cannot learn from Perturb-seq screens, which measure only RNA, and when applied to unperturbed cells, they share the same static limitation. Resolving these contributions requires information that expression data alone cannot provide: which regulatory paths can exist. This information is available from a complementary source. Reference epigenomic catalogs from large-scale projects such as ENCODE 27 , combined with TF motifs and RE– gene proximity, specify candidate regulatory paths linking TFs to target genes through REs 28 , providing a scaffold of possible regulation. Perturb-seq provides this complementary information: the consequences of thousands of interventions, without the paths responsible. Integrating the two through latent regulatory programs shared across perturbations could restrict inference to biologically plausible paths, pool information across thousands of interventions, and allow the contribution of each path to vary from one perturbation to another. Because the resulting paths make explicit mechanistic claims, they can also be evaluated directly against independent measurements of regulation, such as TF binding and chromatin contacts, rather than by prediction accuracy alone. Here we present REGA-perturb (Reference REgulatory Path-Guided Analysis), which supplies this missing information rather than adding model complexity. REGA-perturb represents each perturbation as an activity profile over shared latent regulatory programs and propagates these activities to downstream genes through TF motifs and REs defined by a cell-type-matched reference epigenome, learning from perturbation data how strongly each path is used. It requires only RNA measurements and scales to millions of cells, so it can be applied directly to existing Perturb-seq screens. Across three CRISPRi screens totaling 2.4 million cells, REGA-perturb inferred TF–target relationships that agreed with ChIP-seq binding substantially better than existing methods, and predicted regulons responsive to TFs withheld from training. It also addressed each of the three questions above: it separated candidate direct from indirect targets of perturbed TFs, inferred how perturbations alter TF activity in ways not reflected in TF transcript levels, and resolved perturbation-specific regulatory rewiring, including opposing changes in regulatory inputs that cancel at the expression level. 

Results 

Overview of REGA-perturb 

We developed REGA-perturb, a Reference REgulatory Path-Guided Analysis framework for tracing single-cell perturbation responses through gene regulatory paths. Rather than directly modeling perturbation–gene relationships, REGA-perturb models the propagation of perturbation signals through a hierarchical regulatory architecture, linking perturbation targets to downstream transcriptional responses via latent regulatory programs, transcription factor (TF) motifs, regulatory elements (REs) and target genes (Fig. 1a). A central feature of REGA-perturb is the incorporation of a reference regulatory path connecting TF motifs to target genes through REs. This path is constructed from a cell-type-matched reference RE catalog, with motif occurrences defining candidate TF motif–RE associations and genomic proximity defining candidate RE–gene associations. The reference regulatory path provides a structural prior that constrains the regulatory connections considered by the model while allowing their strengths to be refined using Perturb-seq data. By integrating prior regulatory knowledge with perturbation-associated transcriptional profiles, REGA-perturb learns regulatory relationships within a biologically informed framework.

By jointly learning program activities and regulatory weights, REGA-perturb reconstructs observed transcriptional profiles and provides an interpretable decomposition of perturbation-associated regulatory effects. This representation enables the contributions of individual perturbations to downstream gene expression to be traced through specific regulatory programs, TF motifs and REs. Although the regulatory architecture is shared across perturbations, differences in program activity profiles allow the inferred contributions of individual regulatory paths to vary across perturbations. REGA-perturb is designed to scale to large Perturb-seq datasets and was applied to CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screens comprising millions of single cells and thousands of perturbation targets. By capturing perturbation-associated regulatory relationships, REGA-perturb supports downstream analyses including gene regulatory network inference (Fig. 1b), identification of candidate direct and indirect targets of perturbed TFs (Fig. 1c), prediction of regulons responsive to unseen TF perturbations (Fig. 1d), characterization of perturbation-induced changes in TF activity (Fig. 1e), and investigation of perturbation-specific regulatory rewiring (Fig. 1f). 

REGA-perturb improves GRN inference from single-cell perturbation data 

To evaluate REGA-perturb, we applied it to three large-scale Perturb-seq datasets from two well-studied human cell lines, each comprising more than 2,000 perturbation targets. Two were generated in myelogenous leukemia lymphoblasts (K562): a genome-wide screen (K562 GWPS; ~1.97 million cells and 9,866 perturbation targets) and an essential-gene screen (K562 essential; ~302,000 cells and 2,057 perturbation targets) 3. The third was generated in liver hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2; ~142,000 cells and 2,393 perturbation targets) 29 . For each dataset, peaks from ENCODE bulk ATAC-seq data of the matching cell line served as the reference RE catalog (see Data Availability). To assess whether REGA-perturb prioritizes functional REs, we compared the REs with the highest inferred regulatory importance scores with ENCODE ChIP-seq peaks 27 for the active promoter mark H3K4me3 30 and the active enhancer mark H3K27ac 31 in the same cell lines. The top-ranked REs were significantly enriched for both marks in all three datasets, with average fold enrichments of 2.36 for H3K27ac and 3.24 for H3K4me3 (Fig. 2a). They were also more evolutionarily conserved: across thresholds from the top and bottom 10% to 50%, REs with high importance had significantly higher sequence conservation scores than REs with low importance in all three datasets (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Fig. 1a,b). These results indicate that the REs to which REGA-perturb assigns high importance are functionally active and evolutionarily conserved, even though neither histone-mark nor conservation data were provided to the model. To evaluate the cis-regulatory inferences of REGA-perturb, we used RNA polymerase II ChIA-PET loops from ENCODE 27 in K562 and HepG2 cells as ground truth. These loops capture chromatin interactions at actively transcribed genes, including enhancer–promoter contacts 32 . REGA-perturb distinguishes direct from indirect targets in TF perturbation responses 

Knockdown of a TF changes the expression of the genes that it regulates directly as well as genes that respond secondarily 4,5 . We therefore asked whether REGA-perturb can distinguish direct from indirect targets among the genes that respond to a TF perturbation. Following previous studies that integrated TF perturbation responses with ChIP-seq binding to identify direct targets 5,7,35 , we defined putative direct targets of a TF as genes that both responded to knockdown of the TF and showed strong binding by that TF, as quantified by the regulatory potential (RP) score; the remaining differentially expressed genes were considered indirect targets (Methods). Differentially expressed genes were obtained from independent bulk knockdown experiments in KnockTF 36 for K562 (29 TFs) and from the Perturb-seq screen for HepG2 (15 TFs). For each TF, each method assigned a score to every differentially expressed gene, and we evaluated how well these scores prioritized putative direct targets over indirect targets. REGA-perturb achieved mean AUPR ratios of 1.40 in K562 and 1.73 in HepG2, significantly higher than all four baselines (Fig. 3a,b). In K562, this corresponded to a gain over random prediction about 2.5 times that of the best baseline (1.09 for PCC, 1.11 for GRNBoost2, 1.07 for SCENIC and 1.16 for CellOracle), whereas in HepG2, where the baselines remained close to random (0.96 for PCC, 0.97 for GRNBoost2, 1.01 for SCENIC and 1.08 for CellOracle), the gain was about nine times that of the best baseline. To test whether this result depended on restricting the analysis to differentially expressed genes, we repeated the analysis using all genes in the model as the background, comparing the genes with the highest RP scores with all remaining genes. REGA-perturb again achieved significantly higher AUPR ratios than all four baselines, with mean AUPR ratios of 1.34 in K562 and 1.64 in HepG2, compared with 0.95–1.09 for the baselines (Fig. 3c,d). The advantage of REGA-perturb was robust to both the definition of direct targets and the evaluation metric: the results were consistent when direct targets were additionally required to be supported by a ChIA-PET loop and when performance was assessed by AUROC (Supplementary Fig. 4a–l). The same pattern was also observed when the K562 analysis was repeated using differentially expressed genes from the Perturb-seq screens, with the two screens analyzed separately (Supplementary Fig. 5). This advantage was also evident at the level of individual TFs. For example, among genes differentially expressed after E2F6 knockdown in K562 essential, TF–TG scores from REGA-perturb were significantly higher for putative direct targets than for indirect targets (P = 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴), whereas scores from PCC, GRNBoost2, SCENIC and CellOracle did not differ between the two groups (P = 0.78, 0.17, 0.085 and 0.20, respectively). The magnitude of the expression response itself, measured as the absolute log fold change (|logFC|) after knockdown, likewise did not distinguish direct from indirect targets (P = 0.38; Fig. 3e). The same pattern was observed for TBP in HepG2 when all genes in the model were used as the background, with REGA-perturb significantly separating the two groups (P = 2.0 × 10⁻¹²⁷), whereas the other scores did not (P ≥ 0.15; Fig. 3f). Similar results were observed for two additional K562 TFs, BHLHE40 and STAT1 (Supplementary Fig. 4m,n). Together, these results indicate that REGA-perturb more effectively prioritizes ChIP-supported direct targets over indirect targets in TF perturbation responses than both expression-based and motif-informed methods.

REGA-perturb predicts regulons responsive to unseen TF perturbations 

Most perturbation screens cover only a subset of TFs in a given cell type, and a regulatory network is more useful if it can anticipate the effects of perturbations that have not been performed. We therefore asked whether REGA-perturb can predict regulons responsive to unseen TF perturbations. We retrained REGA-perturb using only data from non-TF perturbations, and applied the baseline methods to the same data. SCENIC was not included in this comparison because it failed to yield a regulon for a large fraction of the unseen TFs. For each unseen TF, we defined its predicted regulon as the genes with the highest TF– TG scores and its regulon activity as the mean expression of these genes for each cell (Methods). For each unseen TF, the activity of the regulons predicted by REGA-perturb distinguished cells in which that TF was perturbed from control cells. The average AUPR ratios were 2.07, 2.08 and 2.04 for the 35, 262 and 41 unseen TFs in K562 essential, K562 GWPS and HepG2, respectively, significantly higher than all three baselines (1.38–1.62; paired two-sided t-test, P < 2 × 10⁻³ for all comparisons; −log10(P) = 2.92– 26.56; Fig. 4a–c). The advantage of REGA-perturb was consistent when performance was assessed by AUROC (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). For FOXM1 in K562 GWPS, for example, the activity of the regulon predicted by REGA-perturb differed significantly between FOXM1 knockdown and control (two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, P = 7.4 × 10⁻⁴; Fig. 4d), whereas the activities of the regulons predicted by PCC, GRNBoost2 and CellOracle did not differ significantly (P = 0.66, 0.67 and 0.41, respectively; Fig. 4e–g). Similar results were observed for BDP1 in K562 essential, STAT1 in K562 GWPS and ATF5 in HepG2 (Supplementary Fig. 6d–f). The weaker performance of PCC, GRNBoost2 and CellOracle may reflect their reliance on covariation between the expression of a TF and its target genes, which depends on variation in the expression of the TF and may therefore be less informative when the perturbation of the TF is withheld. Together, these results indicate that REGA-perturb predicts regulons responsive to unseen TF perturbations more accurately than both expression-based and motif-informed methods, suggesting that it can extend regulatory inference to TFs whose perturbations have not been performed. 

REGA-perturb captures perturbation-induced changes in TF activity 

Beyond identifying the downstream targets of individual TFs, understanding cellular responses to perturbations requires characterizing their effects on other regulators. REGA-perturb links each perturbation target directly to TFs through the regulatory path of the model, connecting perturbation targets to regulatory programs and subsequently to TF motifs (Fig. 1a). By integrating these relationships, REGA-perturb estimates TF activity for each perturbation–TF pair by combining the loadings of the TF’s motifs on the latent regulatory programs with the program activities of the perturbation, and quantifies perturbation-induced changes relative to non-targeting control cells (Methods). We investigated whether these inferred changes capture the regulatory consequences of perturbations. Perturbations with strong transcriptomic effects changed the inferred activity of many TFs, as illustrated by the most strongly perturbed TFs in each dataset (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 7a,b). Together, these results indicate that REGA-perturb infers perturbation-induced changes in TF activity that are consistent with changes in the expression of ChIP-seq target genes but are not simply reflected by changes in the expression of the TFs themselves. 

REGA-perturb resolves perturbation-specific regulatory rewiring 

Genes directly bound by a perturbed TF might be expected to show the largest expression responses. Contrary to this expectation, expression changes following TF perturbation are often only weakly associated with TF binding measured by ChIP-seq 5,7 . We observed this negative association in KnockTF knockdown experiments: across 118 experiments targeting 96 TFs in K562, absolute gene expression changes showed negative correlation with the ChIP-seq regulatory potential (RP) scores of the perturbed TFs (median Spearman's ρ = −0.02; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test against shuffled RP scores, P = 8.5 × 10⁻³; Supplementary Fig. 8d). For example, following ZBTB33 knockdown, absolute gene expression changes were negatively correlated with ZBTB33 RP scores (Spearman's ρ = −0.07, P = 5.7 × 10⁻⁹; Fig. 6a). Similarly negative associations were observed across the three Perturb-seq screens, including 300 perturbed TFs in K562 GWPS (median ρ = −0.03; Fig. 6b; Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). Thus, the genes showing the largest transcriptional responses were not necessarily those most strongly bound by the perturbed TF.

This apparent disconnect may arise because gene expression reports only the net outcome of multiple regulatory inputs. Following perturbation, some inputs to a gene may strengthen while others weaken, causing their effects to cancel and leaving expression nearly unchanged despite extensive reorganization of the gene’s regulatory architecture. Although regulatory compensation is a fundamental feature of biological systems 18,42 , it has been difficult to quantify directly from perturbation-induced expression changes. REGA-perturb addresses this limitation by inferring how each TF–gene interaction changes following perturbation. For each perturbation, we constructed a differential regulatory network in which each TF–gene edge represents its change in weight relative to non-targeting control cells (Fig. 6c). We then defined two complementary gene-level scores: the net regulatory shift score (NRS), the signed sum of changes in incoming edges, captures the net regulatory effect after opposing inputs cancel; the regulatory rewiring score (RRS), the sum of their absolute changes, captures the total magnitude of regulatory change before cancellation (Fig. 6c; Methods). The NRS showed strong correlations with observed expression changes, with median Spearman's ρ ranging from 0.51 to 0.79 among the top 1% of perturbations across the three datasets (Fig. 6e; Supplementary Fig. 8e,f). For example, following GATA1 knockdown in K562 essential, gene-level NRS values correlated with the direction and magnitude of expression changes (Spearman's ρ = 0.52, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰; Fig. 6d). Whereas the NRS summarizes the net regulatory shift, the RRS captures the total magnitude of regulatory changes. We therefore examined whether the RRS was more closely associated with binding of the perturbed TF than absolute expression changes. Across nearly all perturbed TFs with available ChIP-seq data, the RRS showed stronger correlations with the RP scores of the perturbed TFs than did absolute expression changes (87 of 88, 297 of 300 and 63 of 63 TFs in K562 essential, K562 GWPS and HepG2, respectively; median ρ = 0.09–0.12 versus −0.03 to −0.02; two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 10⁻¹¹ for all comparisons; −log10(P) = 11.29–50.15; Supplementary Fig. 9c–h). For example, among 20 selected TFs in K562 GWPS, the RRS was more strongly correlated with RP scores than absolute expression changes for every TF (median ρ = 0.11 versus −0.02; two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 1.9 × 10⁻⁶; Fig. 6h), with similar results in K562 essential and HepG2 (Supplementary Fig. 9a,b). This advantage persisted when expression changes were obtained from independent KnockTF knockdown experiments (Supplementary Fig. 9i–l). Genes bound by the perturbed TF were enriched among genes with high RRS but not among genes with large expression changes. Across all perturbed TFs, genes with the highest RP scores were enriched in the upper half of the RRS distribution (median 1.18-, 1.19- and 1.23-fold in K562 essential, K562 GWPS and HepG2, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 8i–k), whereas absolute expression changes showed little correlation with RP scores (Fig. 6b). For example, TBP-bound genes in HepG2 were concentrated in the upper half of the RRS distribution regardless of their expression changes (Fig. 6f). Similarly, TRIM28-bound genes in K562 GWPS were enriched among genes with high RRS but small expression changes (1.36-fold; one-sided binomial test, P = 5.1 × 10⁻⁴; Fig. 6g). The differential regulatory networks further revealed distinct patterns of regulatory rewiring across genes following the same perturbation (Fig. 6i). The NRS captures the net regulatory shift reflected in gene expression, whereas the RRS captures the total reorganization of regulatory inputs, including opposing changes that can cancel at the expression level. Consequently, genes with little expression change can nevertheless undergo extensive inferred regulatory rewiring. By recovering the binding footprint of the perturbed TF more consistently than differential expression, REGA-perturb provides a framework for detecting—and potentially quantifying—regulatory compensation that is hidden from conventional perturbation analyses. 

Discussions and conclusions 

Perturb-seq responses reflect regulatory mechanism, but because each gene’s expression change mixes the contributions of many regulatory paths, the mechanism cannot be resolved from the response alone, however large the model. REGA-perturb addresses this by supplying information that expression data cannot provide. Reference epigenomes define a scaffold of possible TF–RE–gene paths, and responses to thousands of perturbations determine how strongly each path is used and how its use changes from one perturbation to another. Because the inferred paths make explicit mechanistic claims, we evaluated them against independent measurements of regulation rather than by prediction accuracy alone. Across three CRISPRi screens, REGA-perturb inferred TF–target relationships that agreed with TF binding far better than existing methods, prioritized direct over indirect targets, generalized to TFs withheld from training, and resolved perturbation-specific changes in TF activity and in individual regulatory inputs. These gains likely reflect how the scaffold and the perturbation data compensate for each other’s weaknesses. Motifs and RE–gene proximity define far more candidate interactions than are functional in any given cell type. Expression covariation, on which SCENIC, CellOracle and GRNBoost2 rely,

confounds direct regulation with co-regulation and indirect effects. Perturbations resolve part of this ambiguity because they are interventions, and pooling thousands of them through shared programs selects, among the candidate paths, those that consistently explain the observed responses. The two components play distinct roles. The scaffold supplies information external to expression, restricting inference to paths that can exist. The shared programs allow thousands of perturbations to inform the same set of path weights. SCENIC and CellOracle also use motifs, yet remained close to random in our benchmarks, suggesting that the improvement comes not from the motif prior alone but from combining it with perturbation responses. The same pooling may explain why REGA-perturb generalized to TFs withheld from training: a TF’s regulatory paths can be informed by perturbations of other genes that alter its activity. Covariation-based methods, by contrast, depend on variation in the TF’s own expression, which is largely absent when its perturbation is withheld. Notably, these gains were achieved with a linear model, consistent with the view that resolving mechanism depends more on supplying the right information than on model capacity 13 . Methods that integrate single-cell chromatin accessibility, such as LINGER and SCENIC+ 25,26 , offer another way to add information beyond expression, but they require accessibility profiles that large-scale Perturb-seq screens lack. Because REGA-perturb requires only RNA, it can be applied directly to existing screens. Most genes targeted in a genome-wide screen are not TFs, yet many of their effects are likely mediated by TFs. By linking perturbations to TFs through shared programs, REGA-perturb estimates how each perturbation alters the activity of each TF. These estimates agreed with expression changes in independently defined ChIP-seq regulons but matched changes in the TF’s own transcript in only about half of cases, consistent with regulation of TF activity through cofactors, complexes and post-transcriptional mechanisms 14–16 . For example, MAX knockdown reduced the inferred activity of MXI1 and its co-repressor SIN3A, consistent with the requirement of MAX for MXI1-mediated repression 39–41 .Such estimates can help identify the regulators through which a perturbation acts, and can prioritize candidate TFs for follow-up when the perturbed gene is not itself a TF. A central observation of this study is that expression change and regulatory change are distinct layers of the perturbation response. The same mixing of regulatory inputs that prevents mechanism from being read directly from expression can also conceal regulatory change altogether. Genes bound by a perturbed TF often change little in expression, a pattern usually attributed to nonfunctional binding, context dependence or redundancy 5–7,46 . Our results suggest an additional, not mutually exclusive, explanation: some of these genes undergo substantial changes in regulatory inputs that offset one another. The total magnitude of inferred regulatory change tracked binding of the perturbed TF more closely than absolute expression change did, for 447 of 451 perturbed TFs across three datasets, although the correlations were modest in absolute terms. Such offsetting changes are consistent with regulatory compensation through feedback and redundancy 17,42 . Because successful buffering preserves expression, differential expression analysis classifies these genes as unaffected. Yet a gene whose expression is maintained by rebalanced inputs is not necessarily regulated as before, and may become more dependent on the compensating regulators.

Combinatorial perturbation of a primary target together with its predicted compensating regulators 47 

provides a direct test of this prediction and may reveal genetic interactions invisible to single-gene screens. Several limitations should be noted. First, REGA-perturb relies on a reference RE catalog from unperturbed cells, together with TF motifs and proximity-based RE–gene candidates. It cannot capture regulation through REs absent from the catalog, TFs without known motifs or interactions beyond the proximity window. It also does not model perturbation-induced changes in chromatin accessibility, so the inferred rewiring reflects changes in the use of existing paths rather than the creation of new ones. Second, because candidate TF–RE links are restricted to motif occurrences, motif-based inference and binding-based evaluation are not fully independent. The near-random performance of motif-informed baselines argues that the prior alone does not account for our results, but this dependence should be considered when interpreting absolute performance. Third, the model is linear and represents each perturbation by a single profile of program activities. It therefore does not capture nonlinear interactions between regulators, or heterogeneity among cells receiving the same perturbation. Fourth, inferred changes in TF activity and regulatory inputs are model-based estimates derived from transcriptional data. They do not by themselves establish changes in physical binding or causal mediation through specific REs, which will require targeted RE perturbations, binding measurements after perturbation and time-resolved designs. Finally, we evaluated REGA-perturb in CRISPRi screens of two extensively annotated cell lines. Its performance in primary cells and tissues, where reference epigenomes are sparser, and with other perturbation modalities remains to be established. Perturb-seq screens are expanding to more cell types, perturbation modalities and molecular readouts 47– 50 , and reference epigenomic atlases are expanding in parallel 27,51,52 . As these resources grow, evaluating perturbation models against independent measurements of regulatory mechanism, rather than prediction accuracy alone, will be important for determining whether they capture how perturbations act. REGA-perturb provides a way to bring perturbation screens and reference epigenomes together, turning screens from catalogs of transcriptional responses into maps of the regulatory paths through which perturbations act, and of how those paths are reorganized from one perturbation to another.

Methods 

Data collection and preprocessing 

Perturb-seq data were obtained from three published CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) 53 screens: genome-wide (K562 GWPS) and essential-gene (K562 essential) screens in K562 cells from Replogle et al. 3, and a screen in HepG2 cells from Nadig et al. 29 . We used the single-cell unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrices and corresponding perturbation target annotations provided by the original studies, with cells carrying non-targeting guides serving as controls. Cells with 1,000–50,000 UMIs were retained, and mitochondrial genes and genes detected in fewer than 30 cells were excluded. Following quality control, K562 GWPS contained 1,971,958 cells across 9,866 perturbation targets, including 74,698 non-targeting control cells, with 8,231 genes retained; K562 essential contained 301,774 cells across 2,057 perturbation targets, including 10,476 control cells, with 8,550 genes retained; and HepG2 contained 141,560 cells across 2,393 perturbation targets, including 4,825 control cells, with 9,609 genes retained. Gene expression counts were library-size normalized and log-transformed, and the resulting gene-by-cell expression matrices were used as model inputs. Reference regulatory element (RE) catalogs were constructed from bulk ATAC-seq data for the corresponding cell lines (K562 and HepG2; GRCh38) obtained from ENCODE 27 . For each cell line, ATAC-seq peaks from multiple experiments were pooled, and overlapping peaks were merged. TF ChIP-seq data were also obtained from ENCODE, comprising 423 experiments covering 304 TFs in K562 and 160 experiments covering 135 TFs in HepG2. For each ChIP-seq experiment, gene-level regulatory potential (RP) scores were calculated using the BETA model 35 . RNA polymerase II (POLR2A) ChIA-PET loops and H3K27ac and H3K4me3 ChIP-seq peaks for both cell lines were also obtained from ENCODE. Base-wise evolutionary conservation scores (phastCons 54 , 100-way vertebrate alignment; hg38) were downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser 55 . Bulk TF knockdown and knockout data for K562 cells were obtained from KnockTF 2.0 36 . Paired single-nucleus RNA-seq and ATAC-seq profiles of K562 cells generated using SNARE-seq 33 , together with single-nucleus multiome data of hepatocytes from human liver tissue 34 , were used to calculate RE–gene correlation baselines. 

REGA-perturb model 

REGA-perturb models the effect of a genetic perturbation on single-cell gene expression by explicitly propagating perturbation signals through a hierarchical regulatory cascade. Each perturbation target is first represented by its activity over a small set of latent regulatory programs, which capture shared modes of perturbation response. These programs are linked to transcription factor (TF) motifs, whose activities are propagated to regulatory elements (REs), and then from REs to their downstream target genes. In this way, the model connects each perturbation target to the observed expression profile of a cell through the chain perturbation target → regulatory programs → TF motifs → REs → target genes (Fig. 1a).

A central component of REGA-perturb is the reference regulatory path, which defines the downstream segment of this cascade from TF motifs to REs and from REs to genes. This path is derived from the reference RE catalog constructed for the matched cell type and encodes prior regulatory knowledge, including motif occurrence within REs and candidate RE–target gene links. By constraining how perturbation-induced program activity can flow to genes, the reference regulatory path narrows the solution space, anchors the model in biologically plausible regulatory structure, and enables mechanistic interpretation of predicted perturbation responses. In contrast, the mapping from perturbation targets to regulatory programs is learned directly from the Perturb-seq data, allowing the model to capture perturbation-specific regulatory effects while reusing a shared regulatory architecture.

Metacell construction 

To make the baseline methods computationally tractable and ensure consistent inputs across methods, we aggregated cells into metacells separately within each perturbation target using SEACells 60 . For benchmarking, both REGA-perturb and the baseline methods were fitted using metacell expression profiles, whereas all other analyses used REGA-perturb models fitted to the complete single-cell datasets. 

Benchmarking trans-regulatory inference 

We compared the performance of REGA-perturb in predicting trans-regulatory (TF–TG) interactions with that of PCC, GRNBoost2 (arboreto v0.1.6) 22 , SCENIC (pySCENIC v0.12.1) 20,61 and CellOracle (v0.20.0) 21 .

Evaluation metrics 

The performance of gene regulatory network inference was evaluated using the area under the precision– recall curve (AUPR), the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and precision, recall and F1-score based on the top 25% of predicted regulatory interactions.
